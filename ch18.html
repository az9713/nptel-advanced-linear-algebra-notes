18. Invariant direct sums and block-diagonal operators

Let $T$ be the operator on $\mathbb{Q}^3$ whose standard matrix is

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}3&0&-2\\0&3&-2\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

This matrix is the running example of the chapter (an original construction). Suppose we need $A^{10}$, or $A^{-1}$, or a square root of $A$. Repeated multiplication gives the first, row reduction the second, and neither suggests how to get the third. There is a better route. We shall find two fixed matrices $E_1$ and $E_2$ with

$$A=E_1+3E_2,\qquad A^{10}=E_1+3^{10}E_2 .$$

The same two matrices give every polynomial in $A$. They are projections that split $\mathbb{Q}^3$ into two subspaces that $T$ does not mix. This chapter studies such splittings: when the summands of a direct sum are invariant, how that is detected by the projections, and what happens when $T$ merely rescales each summand.

Conventions. $F$ is a field, $V$ is a vector space over $F$, and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is a linear operator on $V$. Finite dimension is assumed only where it is stated. $I$ is the identity. A subspace $W$ is $T$-invariant if $T(W)\subseteq W$. From Chapter 17: a direct sum $V=W_1\oplus\cdots\oplus W_k$ means that every $\alpha\in V$ has exactly one expression $\alpha=\alpha_1+\cdots+\alpha_k$ with $\alpha_i\in W_i$. The operators $E_i\alpha=\alpha_i$ satisfy

$$E_i^2=E_i,\qquad E_iE_j=0\ (i\ne j),\qquad E_1+\cdots+E_k=I,\qquad\operatorname{im}E_i=W_i,\tag{0.1}$$

and conversely, operators with $E_iE_j=0$ for $i\ne j$ and $\sum E_i=I$ are projections whose ranges form a direct sum equal to $V$. We call $E_1,\dots,E_k$ the projections of the decomposition.

1. Invariant summands 00:25

Suppose $V=W_1\oplus\cdots\oplus W_k$ and every $W_i$ is $T$-invariant. Each restriction $T_i:W_i\to W_i$, $T_i\alpha=T\alpha$, is an operator on $W_i$, and

$$T\alpha=T_1\alpha_1+\cdots+T_k\alpha_k .$$

The behaviour of $T$ is therefore the behaviour of $k$ smaller operators placed side by side. If $V$ is finite-dimensional, $\mathcal{B}_i$ is an ordered basis of $W_i$ with $d_i$ elements and $\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}_1,\dots,\mathcal{B}_k)$, then

$$[T]_{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}A_1&&0\\&\ddots&\\0&&A_k\end{pmatrix},\qquad A_i=[T_i]_{\mathcal{B}_i}\in F^{d_i\times d_i},\qquad\sum_id_i=\dim V,$$

as shown in Chapter 17. Such a matrix is block diagonal.

On the running example. Let $b_1=(1,1,1)$, $b_2=(1,0,0)$ and $b_3=(0,1,0)$, writing columns as tuples. These are linearly independent. Direct multiplication gives $Ab_1=b_1$, $Ab_2=3b_2$ and $Ab_3=3b_3$. So $W_1=\operatorname{span}(b_1)$ and $W_2=\operatorname{span}(b_2,b_3)$ are invariant, $\mathbb{Q}^3=W_1\oplus W_2$, and in the basis $(b_1,b_2,b_3)$ the matrix of $T$ is $\operatorname{diag}(1,3,3)$.

2. Invariance means commuting with the projections 09:08

To test invariance of the summands without choosing bases, we use the projections.

Theorem 2.1. Let $V=W_1\oplus\cdots\oplus W_k$ with projections $E_1,\dots,E_k$, and let $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Then every $W_i$ is $T$-invariant if and only if $TE_i=E_iT$ for every $i$. The dimension of $V$ may be infinite.

Proof. Suppose $T$ commutes with every $E_i$. Let $\alpha\in W_i$. Then $\alpha=E_i\alpha$, so $T\alpha=TE_i\alpha=E_iT\alpha$, which lies in $\operatorname{im}E_i=W_i$.

Suppose every $W_i$ is invariant. Fix $j$ and $\alpha\in V$. By $\sum_iE_i=I$,

$$T\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^kTE_i\alpha .$$

Each term $TE_i\alpha$ lies in $W_i$, because $E_i\alpha\in W_i$ and $W_i$ is invariant. So this is the decomposition of $T\alpha$, and its $j$th component is $TE_j\alpha$. But the $j$th component of any vector $\gamma$ is $E_j\gamma$. Hence $E_jT\alpha=TE_j\alpha$. $\square$

The hinge of the second part is uniqueness in a direct sum: once each summand of an expression is known to lie in the right subspace, the expression is the decomposition. Invariance of all summands is needed. With only $W_j$ invariant, the terms $TE_i\alpha$ with $i\ne j$ may leak into $W_j$.

Corollary 2.2. For a projection $E$ and an operator $T$, we have $TE=ET$ if and only if both $\operatorname{im}E$ and $\ker E$ are $T$-invariant.

Proof. Apply Theorem 2.1 to $V=\operatorname{im}E\oplus\ker E$, whose projections are $E$ and $I-E$. An operator commutes with $E$ exactly when it commutes with $I-E$. $\square$

On the running example. The projections on $W_1$ and $W_2$ are obtained by keeping the corresponding coordinates in the basis $(b_1,b_2,b_3)$:

$$E_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&0&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad E_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&1&-1\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

One checks (0.1), and one checks $AE_1=E_1A$ and $AE_2=E_2A$, as Theorem 2.1 predicts. Concretely, $E_1(x,y,z)=z\,b_1$ and $E_2(x,y,z)=(x-z,y-z,0)$.

3. Diagonalizable operators: the spectral form 19:26

An operator on a finite-dimensional space is diagonalizable if $V$ has a basis of eigenvectors. Let $c_1,\dots,c_k$ be the distinct eigenvalues of $T$ and $W_i=\ker(T-c_iI)$ the eigenspaces. Eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are independent (Chapter 17), and when $T$ is diagonalizable they span $V$. So $V=W_1\oplus\cdots\oplus W_k$.

Theorem 3.1. Let $\dim V$ be finite and $T$ diagonalizable with distinct eigenvalues $c_1,\dots,c_k$, and let $E_i$ be the projections of the eigenspace decomposition. Then, besides (0.1) with every $E_i\ne0$,

$$T=c_1E_1+c_2E_2+\cdots+c_kE_k .$$

Proof. For $\alpha\in V$ we have $\alpha=\sum_iE_i\alpha$ with $E_i\alpha\in W_i$, so $TE_i\alpha=c_iE_i\alpha$. Hence $T\alpha=\sum_iTE_i\alpha=\sum_ic_iE_i\alpha$. Each $E_i$ is nonzero because its range, an eigenspace of an eigenvalue, is nonzero. $\square$

We call this the spectral form of $T$. Every operator satisfying the hypotheses of Section 1 splits into restrictions. What is special here is that each restriction is a scalar, $T_i=c_iI_{W_i}$. For an operator that is not diagonalizable, one can still have $I=\sum E_i$ with invariant ranges, but not $T=\sum c_iE_i$.

On the running example. The eigenvalues are $1$ and $3$ with eigenspaces $W_1$ and $W_2$. So $A=1\cdot E_1+3\cdot E_2$, which one verifies entry by entry.

4. Powers and polynomials 27:16

Theorem 4.1. Let $T=\sum_ic_iE_i$ where the $E_i$ satisfy $E_i^2=E_i$, $E_iE_j=0$ for $i\ne j$, and $\sum E_i=I$. Then for every $g\in F[x]$,

$$g(T)=g(c_1)E_1+\cdots+g(c_k)E_k .$$

Proof. In $T^2=\sum_{i,j}c_ic_jE_iE_j$ the cross terms vanish and $E_i^2=E_i$, so $T^2=\sum c_i^2E_i$. By induction $T^r=\sum c_i^rE_i$ for $r\ge1$, and for $r=0$ the formula is $I=\sum E_i$. A polynomial is a linear combination of powers, and the right side is linear in the values. $\square$

On the running example. $A^{10}=E_1+3^{10}E_2=E_1+59049\,E_2$. For $g(x)=x^3-2x$ we get $g(1)=-1$ and $g(3)=21$, so $A^3-2A=-E_1+21E_2$. Since $1$ and $3$ are nonzero, the operator $B=E_1+\tfrac13E_2$ satisfies $AB=(E_1+3E_2)(E_1+\tfrac13E_2)=E_1+E_2=I$. So $A^{-1}=B$. Over $\mathbb{R}$, the operator $S=E_1+\sqrt3\,E_2$ satisfies $S^2=A$: this is the square root of the opening.

5. The projections are polynomials in the operator 29:52

The projections were built from eigenvectors. They can be computed from $T$ alone, with no eigenvector in sight. For distinct scalars $c_1,\dots,c_k$ define the Lagrange polynomials

$$p_j(x)=\prod_{i\ne j}\frac{x-c_i}{c_j-c_i}.$$

Each $p_j$ has degree $k-1$, and $p_j(c_i)=\delta_{ij}$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is $1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise. Distinctness makes the denominators nonzero.

Theorem 5.1. Under the hypotheses of Theorem 4.1 with distinct $c_i$, $E_j=p_j(T)$ for every $j$.

Proof. By Theorem 4.1, $p_j(T)=\sum_ip_j(c_i)E_i=\sum_i\delta_{ij}E_i=E_j$. $\square$

Consequently each $E_j$ commutes with $T$ and with every operator that commutes with $T$.

Corollary 5.2. If in addition every $E_i$ is nonzero, then $g(T)=0$ exactly when $g(c_i)=0$ for all $i$. Hence the minimal polynomial of $T$ is $m_T=(x-c_1)\cdots(x-c_k)$.

Proof. If $g(c_i)=0$ for all $i$, then $g(T)=0$ by Theorem 4.1. Conversely, if $g(T)=0$, multiply $\sum_ig(c_i)E_i=0$ by $E_j$ to get $g(c_j)E_j=0$. Since $E_j\ne0$, $g(c_j)=0$. The monic polynomial of least degree vanishing at the distinct points $c_1,\dots,c_k$ is their product. $\square$

On the running example. With $c_1=1$ and $c_2=3$ the polynomials are $p_1(x)=\frac{3-x}{2}$ and $p_2(x)=\frac{x-1}{2}$. Then $E_1=\tfrac12(3I-A)$ and $E_2=\tfrac12(A-I)$, matching the matrices of Section 2. The minimal polynomial is $(x-1)(x-3)$, while the characteristic polynomial is $(x-1)(x-3)^2$. The formula for $p_j$ divides by $c_1-c_2=-2$, so over a field in which $2=0$ the two eigenvalues would coincide and the example would change character.

6. The converse: a spectral form proves diagonalizability 09:22

Theorem 3.1 says that a diagonalizable operator has a spectral form. The converse 06:56 holds, and it identifies all the data.

Theorem 6.1. Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$. Suppose there are distinct scalars $c_1,\dots,c_k$ and nonzero operators $E_1,\dots,E_k$ with

  1. $T=c_1E_1+\cdots+c_kE_k$,
  2. $I=E_1+\cdots+E_k$,
  3. $E_iE_j=0$ for $i\ne j$.

Then each $E_i$ is a projection, $c_1,\dots,c_k$ are exactly the eigenvalues of $T$, $\operatorname{im}E_i=\ker(T-c_iI)$, and $V$ is the direct sum of the eigenspaces. In particular, if $\dim V$ is finite, $T$ is diagonalizable.

Proof. Projections. Multiply 2 by $E_j$. By 3 only one term survives: $E_j=E_j^2$.

Each $c_j$ is an eigenvalue. From 1 and 3, $TE_j=\sum_ic_iE_iE_j=c_jE_j$, that is, $(T-c_jI)E_j=0$. Since $E_j\ne0$, some $\beta$ has $\alpha=E_j\beta\ne0$, and $(T-c_jI)\alpha=0$. The same equation shows $\operatorname{im}E_j\subseteq\ker(T-c_jI)$.

There are no other eigenvalues. Suppose $T\alpha=c\alpha$ with $\alpha\ne0$. By 1 and 2,

$$0=(T-cI)\alpha=\sum_i(c_i-c)E_i\alpha .$$

Apply $E_j$: by 3 and $E_j^2=E_j$, we get $(c_j-c)E_j\alpha=0$ for every $j$. Since $\alpha=\sum_jE_j\alpha\ne0$, some $E_j\alpha$ is nonzero, and for that $j$ we get $c=c_j$.

The ranges are the whole eigenspaces. Let $\alpha\in\ker(T-c_jI)$. The computation just made, with $c=c_j$, gives $(c_i-c_j)E_i\alpha=0$ for every $i$. For $i\ne j$ the scalar is nonzero because the $c_i$ are distinct, so $E_i\alpha=0$. Then $\alpha=\sum_iE_i\alpha=E_j\alpha\in\operatorname{im}E_j$.

Direct sum. By Chapter 17, conditions 2 and 3 give $V=\operatorname{im}E_1\oplus\cdots\oplus\operatorname{im}E_k$, and each summand is an eigenspace. In finite dimension, the union of bases of the summands is a basis of eigenvectors. $\square$

The hinge is to apply a projection to the equation $(T-cI)\alpha=0$: it separates one equation in $V$ into $k$ scalar equations, one in each summand.

Corollary 6.2 (uniqueness). The spectral form of a diagonalizable operator with distinct coefficients and nonzero projections is unique.

Proof. By Theorem 6.1, the $c_i$ must be the eigenvalues of $T$, and by Theorem 5.1, $E_i=p_i(T)$. Both are determined by $T$. $\square$

A second example. Keep the basis $(b_1,b_2,b_3)$ and let $F_1,F_2,F_3$ be the projections on the three lines. Then $S=2F_1-F_2+7F_3$ satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem 6.1, with $F_1=E_1$ and $F_2+F_3=E_2$. So $S$ is diagonalizable with eigenvalues $2,-1,7$, each eigenspace a line, and by Corollary 5.2, $m_S=(x-2)(x+1)(x-7)$. In the standard basis,

$$S=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&3\\0&7&-5\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}.$$

7. Where the hypotheses bind

A complement that is not invariant. In the running example, the line $\operatorname{span}(e_1)$, with $e_1=(1,0,0)$, is invariant, and $\mathbb{Q}^3=\operatorname{span}(e_1)\oplus\operatorname{span}(e_2,e_3)$. But the plane is not invariant, since $Ae_3=(-2,-2,1)$ has a nonzero first coordinate. By Theorem 2.1 the projection on $\operatorname{span}(e_1)$ along the plane cannot commute with $A$, and it does not.

Invariant summands without a spectral form. Let

$$N=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}.$$

The subspaces $\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)$ and $\operatorname{span}(e_3)$ are invariant, the matrix is block diagonal, and the projections commute with $N$. Yet $N$ is not diagonalizable: its minimal polynomial is $(x-2)^2(x-3)$, which has a repeated factor. The first block is as far as this decomposition goes. Describing what remains inside such blocks is the subject of Chapter 19 and Chapter 20.

Nonzero projections. Take $E_1=I$, $E_2=0$, $c_1=1$, $c_2=5$. Conditions 1 to 3 hold for $T=I$, but $5$ is not an eigenvalue.

Distinct scalars. Take the running projections with $c_1=c_2=4$. Then $T=4I$ satisfies 1 to 3, but $\ker(T-4I)=V$ is larger than $\operatorname{im}E_1$.

Characteristic. No restriction on the characteristic of $F$ is used in Theorems 2.1 to 6.1. Only the distinctness of the $c_i$ as elements of $F$ matters. Integers that are distinct may coincide in $F$.

Exercises

  1. (Computation.) For the running example, write $A^{-1}$ and $A^2-4A+3I$ using $E_1,E_2$, and give both as explicit matrices.
  2. (Construction.) Compute $F_1,F_2,F_3$ for the basis $(b_1,b_2,b_3)$, check the matrix $S$ of Section 6, and recover $F_1$ as a polynomial in $S$.
  3. (Hypotheses.) Show by examples that in Theorem 6.1 the identification $\operatorname{im}E_i=\ker(T-c_iI)$ can fail if the scalars are not distinct, and that $c_i$ need not be an eigenvalue if $E_i=0$. Where does the proof use each hypothesis?
  4. (Commutant.) Let $\dim V=n$ and let $T$ have $n$ distinct eigenvalues. Prove that an operator $S$ commutes with $T$ if and only if $S=g(T)$ for some polynomial $g$.
  5. (Blocks.) For the matrix $N$ of Section 7, find the projection onto $\operatorname{span}(e_3)$ along $\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)$ as a polynomial in $N$.
  6. (Infinite dimension.) Let $F$ have characteristic different from $2$ and let $T$ on $F[x]$ be $(Tp)(x)=p(-x)$. Find nonzero projections $E_1,E_2$ and scalars satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem 6.1, and describe the eigenspaces.
Fully worked solutions
  1. By Theorem 4.1, or directly from $(E_1+3E_2)(E_1+\tfrac13E_2)=I$, we have $A^{-1}=E_1+\tfrac13E_2$. Adding the matrices,

$$A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1/3&0&2/3\\0&1/3&2/3\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

For $g(x)=x^2-4x+3=(x-1)(x-3)$ both values $g(1),g(3)$ vanish, so $g(A)=g(1)E_1+g(3)E_2=0$. This is the minimal polynomial.

  1. A vector $(x,y,z)$ has coordinates $c_1=z$, $c_2=x-z$, $c_3=y-z$ in $(b_1,b_2,b_3)$. So $F_1(x,y,z)=z(1,1,1)$, $F_2(x,y,z)=(x-z,0,0)$ and $F_3(x,y,z)=(0,y-z,0)$. Then $2F_1-F_2+7F_3$ sends $(x,y,z)$ to $(2z-(x-z),\,2z+7(y-z),\,2z)=(-x+3z,\,7y-5z,\,2z)$, which is the displayed $S$. By Theorem 5.1, $F_1=p_1(S)$ with the Lagrange polynomial for the eigenvalue $2$ among $2,-1,7$: $$p_1(x)=\frac{(x+1)(x-7)}{(2+1)(2-7)}=-\frac{(x+1)(x-7)}{15}.$$

  2. With $E_1=I$, $E_2=0$, $c_1=1$, $c_2=5$ the conditions hold for $T=I$, and $5$ is not an eigenvalue. The step "some $\beta$ has $E_j\beta\ne0$" is where $E_j\ne0$ is used. With $E_1,E_2$ the running projections and $c_1=c_2=4$, we have $T=4(E_1+E_2)=4I$, and $\ker(T-4I)=\mathbb{Q}^3$ has dimension $3$, while $\operatorname{im}E_1$ has dimension $1$. The proof of the inclusion $\ker(T-c_jI)\subseteq\operatorname{im}E_j$ divides by $c_i-c_j$, which is where distinctness is used.

  3. If $S=g(T)$ then $S$ commutes with $T$. Conversely, let $ST=TS$. Each eigenspace $W_i=\ker(T-c_iI)$ is one-dimensional, because there are $n$ of them, they are nonzero, and they are independent in an $n$-dimensional space. If $v_i$ spans $W_i$, then $T(Sv_i)=S(Tv_i)=c_iSv_i$, so $Sv_i\in W_i$ and $Sv_i=s_iv_i$ for a scalar $s_i$. Choose $g$ with $g(c_i)=s_i$, namely $g=\sum_is_ip_i$. Then $g(T)v_i=g(c_i)v_i=s_iv_i=Sv_i$ for every $i$. The $v_i$ form a basis, so $S=g(T)$.

  4. The matrix $N$ is not diagonalizable, so Theorem 5.1 does not apply, and we look for $p$ with $p(N)=\operatorname{diag}(0,0,1)$. On the first block $N_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ we need $p(N_1)=0$, which holds if $(x-2)^2$ divides $p$. On the second block, the number $3$, we need $p(3)=1$. The polynomial $p(x)=(x-2)^2$ does both, since $p(3)=1$. So the projection is $(N-2I)^2$. Direct computation confirms $(N-2I)^2=\operatorname{diag}(0,0,1)$.

  5. Let $E_1p=\tfrac12\big(p(x)+p(-x)\big)$ and $E_2p=\tfrac12\big(p(x)-p(-x)\big)$. Then $E_1+E_2=I$. The range of $E_1$ consists of even polynomials and that of $E_2$ of odd polynomials, and $E_1E_2=E_2E_1=0$ because the even part of an odd polynomial is $0$ and conversely. Both are nonzero, since $E_1 1=1$ and $E_2x=x$. Also $T=E_1-E_2$, with $c_1=1\ne c_2=-1$ because $2\ne0$. By Theorem 6.1 the only eigenvalues are $\pm1$, the eigenspaces are the even and the odd polynomials, and $F[x]$ is their direct sum. Both are infinite-dimensional.

"""Chapter 18 laboratory: invariant direct sums and block-diagonal operators.

Exact arithmetic over Q (fractions.Fraction).  A matrix is a list of rows,
a vector is a list, a polynomial is a list of coefficients from degree 0
upward.  Every routine returns new objects and never changes its input.

What the checks cover: the chapter's running operator on Q^3, the
commutation criterion, the spectral form T = c1 E1 + c2 E2, the Lagrange
formula E_j = p_j(T), the converse theorem on a second example, and three
boundary cases.  They verify finite examples only; they prove no theorem.
"""
from fractions import Fraction


def Q(rows):
    return [[Fraction(x) for x in row] for row in rows]


def eye(n):
    return [[Fraction(int(i == j)) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def zeros(m, n):
    return [[Fraction(0)] * n for _ in range(m)]


def matmul(A, B):
    return [[sum(A[i][k] * B[k][j] for k in range(len(B)))
             for j in range(len(B[0]))] for i in range(len(A))]


def matadd(A, B):
    return [[a + b for a, b in zip(r, s)] for r, s in zip(A, B)]


def scale(c, A):
    return [[Fraction(c) * a for a in row] for row in A]


def matsub(A, B):
    return matadd(A, scale(-1, B))


def trace(A):
    return sum(A[i][i] for i in range(len(A)))


def column(A, j):
    return [row[j] for row in A]


def matvec(A, v):
    return [sum(a * x for a, x in zip(row, v)) for row in A]


def rref(A):
    """Row-reduced echelon form and the list of pivot columns."""
    R = [row[:] for row in A]
    pivots = []
    r = 0
    for c in range(len(R[0]) if R else 0):
        p = next((i for i in range(r, len(R)) if R[i][c] != 0), None)
        if p is None:
            continue
        R[r], R[p] = R[p], R[r]
        R[r] = [x / R[r][c] for x in R[r]]
        for i in range(len(R)):
            if i != r and R[i][c] != 0:
                f = R[i][c]
                R[i] = [x - f * y for x, y in zip(R[i], R[r])]
        pivots.append(c)
        r += 1
        if r == len(R):
            break
    return R, pivots


def rank(A):
    return len(rref(A)[1])


def nullspace(A):
    """A basis (list of vectors) of {x : A x = 0}."""
    R, pivots = rref(A)
    n = len(A[0])
    basis = []
    for f in (c for c in range(n) if c not in pivots):
        v = [Fraction(0)] * n
        v[f] = Fraction(1)
        for r, p in enumerate(pivots):
            v[p] = -R[r][f]
        basis.append(v)
    return basis


def inverse(A):
    n = len(A)
    aug = [row + e for row, e in zip(A, eye(n))]
    R, pivots = rref(aug)
    assert pivots[:n] == list(range(n)), 'matrix is singular'
    return [row[n:] for row in R]


def is_invariant(A, vectors):
    """True when A maps span(vectors) into span(vectors)."""
    base = [list(v) for v in vectors]
    image = base + [matvec(A, v) for v in vectors]
    return rank(image) == rank(base)


# Polynomials: coefficient lists, lowest degree first.

def poly_mul(f, g):
    out = [Fraction(0)] * (len(f) + len(g) - 1)
    for i, a in enumerate(f):
        for j, b in enumerate(g):
            out[i + j] += a * b
    return out


def poly_at_matrix(f, A):
    """f(A) by Horner's rule."""
    n = len(A)
    out = zeros(n, n)
    for c in reversed(f):
        out = matadd(matmul(out, A), scale(c, eye(n)))
    return out


def lagrange(cs, j):
    """p_j(x) = product over i != j of (x - c_i)/(c_j - c_i)."""
    p = [Fraction(1)]
    for i, ci in enumerate(cs):
        if i != j:
            d = Fraction(cs[j]) - ci
            p = poly_mul(p, [-Fraction(ci) / d, 1 / d])
    return p


def charpoly(A):
    """Faddeev-LeVerrier: monic det(xI - A), lowest degree first."""
    n = len(A)
    coeffs = [Fraction(0)] * (n + 1)
    coeffs[n] = Fraction(1)
    M = zeros(n, n)
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        M = matadd(matmul(A, M), scale(coeffs[n - k + 1], eye(n)))
        coeffs[n - k] = -trace(matmul(A, M)) / k
    return coeffs


def min_poly(A):
    """Monic polynomial of least degree d with A^d in span(I..A^(d-1))."""
    n = len(A)
    powers = [eye(n)]
    for d in range(1, n * n + 1):
        powers.append(matmul(powers[-1], A))
        flat = [[x for row in P for x in row] for P in powers]
        cols = [[flat[k][i] for k in range(len(flat))]
                for i in range(n * n)]
        null = nullspace(cols)
        if null:
            v = null[0]
            return [x / v[-1] for x in v]
    raise AssertionError('no dependency found')


def poly_from_roots(roots):
    p = [Fraction(1)]
    for r in roots:
        p = poly_mul(p, [-Fraction(r), Fraction(1)])
    return p


def poly_str(f):
    terms = []
    for k in range(len(f) - 1, -1, -1):
        if f[k] != 0:
            terms.append('%s*x^%d' % (f[k], k))
    return ' + '.join(terms)


def show(M):
    """Compact text for a matrix with integer or Fraction entries."""
    return '[' + '; '.join(' '.join(str(x) for x in row)
                           for row in M) + ']'


def diag_selector(n, idx):
    D = zeros(n, n)
    for i in idx:
        D[i][i] = Fraction(1)
    return D


def is_zero(A):
    return all(x == 0 for row in A for x in row)


def commutes(A, B):
    return matmul(A, B) == matmul(B, A)

# 1. The running operator T on Q^3, in the standard basis.
A = Q([[3, 0, -2], [0, 3, -2], [0, 0, 1]])
b1, b2, b3 = [1, 1, 1], [1, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0]
P = Q([[1, 1, 0], [1, 0, 1], [1, 0, 0]])  # columns b1, b2, b3
Pinv = inverse(P)
assert [column(P, j) for j in range(3)] == Q([b1, b2, b3])

# W1 = span(b1) and W2 = span(b2, b3) are invariant; matrix is block-diag.
assert is_invariant(A, Q([b1])) and is_invariant(A, Q([b2, b3]))
D = matmul(Pinv, matmul(A, P))
assert D == Q([[1, 0, 0], [0, 3, 0], [0, 0, 3]])
print('[T]_B =', show(D))

# 2. The projections of V = W1 + W2 along the decomposition.
E1 = matmul(P, matmul(diag_selector(3, [0]), Pinv))
E2 = matmul(P, matmul(diag_selector(3, [1, 2]), Pinv))
assert E1 == Q([[0, 0, 1], [0, 0, 1], [0, 0, 1]])
assert E2 == Q([[1, 0, -1], [0, 1, -1], [0, 0, 0]])
assert matmul(E1, E1) == E1 and matmul(E2, E2) == E2
assert is_zero(matmul(E1, E2)) and is_zero(matmul(E2, E1))
assert matadd(E1, E2) == eye(3)
assert rank(E1) == 1 and rank(E2) == 2
print('E1 =', show(E1))
print('E2 =', show(E2))

# 3. Commutation criterion: A commutes with E1, E2.
assert commutes(A, E1) and commutes(A, E2)
print('A E1 = E1 A and A E2 = E2 A: True')

# 4. Spectral form and powers.
assert matadd(E1, scale(3, E2)) == A
for n in (2, 5, 10):
    An = eye(3)
    for _ in range(n):
        An = matmul(An, A)
    assert An == matadd(E1, scale(3 ** n, E2))
print('A^10 =', show(matadd(E1, scale(3 ** 10, E2))))

# g(x) = x^3 - 2x gives g(A) = g(1) E1 + g(3) E2 = -E1 + 21 E2.
g = Q([[0, -2, 0, 1]])[0]
assert poly_at_matrix(g, A) == matadd(scale(-1, E1), scale(21, E2))
print('g(1), g(3) =', 1 - 2, 27 - 6)

# 5. Lagrange polynomials give the projections.
cs = [1, 3]
p1, p2 = lagrange(cs, 0), lagrange(cs, 1)
assert p1 == [Fraction(3, 2), Fraction(-1, 2)]
assert p2 == [Fraction(-1, 2), Fraction(1, 2)]
assert poly_at_matrix(p1, A) == E1 and poly_at_matrix(p2, A) == E2
print('p1(x) =', poly_str(p1), '; p2(x) =', poly_str(p2))

# Minimal and characteristic polynomials of A.
assert min_poly(A) == poly_from_roots([1, 3])
assert charpoly(A) == poly_from_roots([1, 3, 3])
print('m_A =', poly_str(min_poly(A)), '; c_A =', poly_str(charpoly(A)))

# 6. Converse theorem on a second example with three summands.
cs3 = [2, -1, 7]
Es = [matmul(P, matmul(diag_selector(3, [i]), Pinv)) for i in range(3)]
T = zeros(3, 3)
for c, E in zip(cs3, Es):
    T = matadd(T, scale(c, E))
total = zeros(3, 3)
for E in Es:
    total = matadd(total, E)
assert total == eye(3)
for i in range(3):
    for j in range(3):
        if i != j:
            assert is_zero(matmul(Es[i], Es[j]))
    assert matmul(Es[i], Es[i]) == Es[i] and rank(Es[i]) == 1
    kernel = nullspace(matsub(T, scale(cs3[i], eye(3))))
    assert len(kernel) == rank(Es[i])
    assert is_zero(matmul(matsub(T, scale(cs3[i], eye(3))), Es[i]))
assert charpoly(T) == poly_from_roots(cs3)
assert min_poly(T) == poly_from_roots(cs3)
print('T =', show(T), 'has eigenvalues', cs3)
print('m_T =', poly_str(min_poly(T)))

# 7. Boundary: W1 = span(e1) is invariant, W2 = span(e2, e3) is not.
e1, e2, e3 = [1, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 1]
assert is_invariant(A, Q([e1])) and not is_invariant(A, Q([e2, e3]))
F1 = diag_selector(3, [0])
assert not commutes(A, F1)
print('invariant W1 with non-invariant complement: A F1 != F1 A')

# 8. Boundary: invariant decomposition, block-diagonal, not diagonalizable.
N = Q([[2, 1, 0], [0, 2, 0], [0, 0, 3]])
assert is_invariant(N, Q([e1, e2])) and is_invariant(N, Q([e3]))
G1, G2 = diag_selector(3, [0, 1]), diag_selector(3, [2])
assert commutes(N, G1) and commutes(N, G2)
mN = min_poly(N)
assert mN == poly_from_roots([2, 2, 3])
assert charpoly(N) == poly_from_roots([2, 2, 3])
# m_N is the lcm of the minimal polynomials of the blocks (x-2)^2, (x-3).
assert min_poly(N) == poly_mul(poly_from_roots([2, 2]), [-3, 1])
# T = a G1 + b G2 is impossible: it is diagonal, N is not.
assert N[0][1] != 0
print('m_N =', poly_str(mN), '= (x-2)^2 (x-3): not diagonalizable')

# 9. Boundary: converse needs nonzero E_i and distinct c_i.
T_id = matadd(matadd(E1, E2), scale(5, zeros(3, 3)))  # 5 * (zero E)
assert T_id == eye(3)
assert charpoly(T_id) == poly_from_roots([1, 1, 1])  # 5 is not a root
T_eq = matadd(scale(4, E1), scale(4, E2))
assert T_eq == scale(4, eye(3))
assert len(nullspace(matsub(T_eq, scale(4, eye(3))))) == 3
assert rank(E1) == 1  # ker(T - 4I) has dimension 3, range E1 has 1
print('zero E_2 with c_2 = 5: 5 is not an eigenvalue of I')
print('c_1 = c_2 = 4: ker(T - 4I) has dimension 3, range E1 has dim 1')

S = matadd(matadd(scale(2, Es[0]), scale(-1, Es[1])), scale(7, Es[2]))
