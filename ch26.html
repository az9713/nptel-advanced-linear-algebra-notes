26. Singular value decomposition and forms on inner product spaces

The matrix

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{pmatrix}$$

maps the unit circle of $\mathbb{R}^2$ to an ellipse. How long are its axes? Eigenvalues do not answer: they are $3$ and $5$, while the longest vector $Ax$ with $\|x\|=1$ has length $3\sqrt5\approx6.708$. The answer needs two orthonormal bases, one for the inputs and one for the outputs, and such a pair exists for every matrix, square or not. This is the singular value decomposition. The second half of the chapter treats functions of two vectors such as $(x,y)\mapsto\langle Ax,Ay\rangle$, and shows that the same spectral theorem puts every Hermitian one into diagonal form. The matrix $A$ is the running example (an original choice).

Conventions. $F$ is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. On $F^n$, $\langle x,y\rangle=y^*x$, where ${}^*$ is the conjugate transpose, defined for matrices of any shape. A square matrix $U$ is unitary if $U^*U=I$ (a real unitary matrix is called orthogonal). A matrix $H$ is Hermitian if $H^*=H$. A basis is orthonormal if its vectors have norm $1$ and are pairwise orthogonal. We use the spectral theorem of Chapter 25: a Hermitian matrix has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with real eigenvalues (real vectors if the matrix is real).

1. The matrix $A^*A$ 03:03

A rectangular $A\in F^{m\times n}$ has no eigenvalues, but $A^*A$ is a square $n\times n$ matrix.

Lemma 1.1. $A^*A$ is Hermitian, $x^*A^*Ax=\|Ax\|^2\ge0$ for all $x$, and its eigenvalues are real and nonnegative.

Proof. $(A^*A)^*=A^*A$, and $x^*A^*Ax=(Ax)^*(Ax)$. If $A^*Az=\lambda z$ with $z\ne0$, then $\|Az\|^2=\lambda\|z\|^2$, so $\lambda\ge0$. $\square$

A Hermitian matrix with $x^*Hx\ge0$ for all $x$ is called positive semidefinite.

2. The decomposition theorem 06:25

Theorem 2.1. Let $A\in F^{m\times n}$ have rank $r$. There are unitary $U\in F^{m\times m}$ and $V\in F^{n\times n}$ with

$$A=UDV^*,$$

where $D\in F^{m\times n}$ has $D_{jj}=\sigma_j$ for $j\le r$, all other entries zero, and $\sigma_1\ge\dots\ge\sigma_r\gt0$.

Proof. By the spectral theorem and Lemma 1.1, $F^n$ has an orthonormal basis $v_1,\dots,v_n$ with $A^*Av_j=\lambda_jv_j$ and $\lambda_j\ge0$. Number them so that $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots$, and let $s$ be the number of positive ones. Put $x_j=Av_j$. Then

$$\langle x_j,x_k\rangle=v_k^*A^*Av_j=\lambda_j\,v_k^*v_j=\lambda_j\delta_{jk},$$

where $\delta_{jk}$ is $1$ for $j=k$ and $0$ otherwise. So $x_j=0$ for $j\gt s$, and with $\sigma_j=\sqrt{\lambda_j}$ the vectors $u_j=x_j/\sigma_j$ $(j\le s)$ are orthonormal in $F^m$. The vectors $Av_j$ span the image of $A$, and the nonzero ones are independent; therefore $s=r$, and in particular $r\le\min(m,n)$. Extend $u_1,\dots,u_r$ to an orthonormal basis $u_1,\dots,u_m$ of $F^m$ (extend to a basis and apply Gram–Schmidt). Let $U,V$ have the columns $u_j,v_j$. The equations $Av_j=\sigma_ju_j$ $(j\le r)$ and $Av_j=0$ $(j\gt r)$ say $AV=UD$. Since $V^{-1}=V^*$, $A=UDV^*$. $\square$

The hinge is the displayed line: orthogonal eigenvectors of $A^*A$ have orthogonal images under $A$.

Definition 2.2. The numbers $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r$ are the singular values of $A$. The columns of $V$ are right and those of $U$ left singular vectors.

The singular values are determined by $A$: if $A=UDV^*$ is any such factorization, then $A^*A=V(D^*D)V^*$, so the $\sigma_j^2$ are the nonzero eigenvalues of $A^*A$ with their multiplicities. The matrices $U,V$ are not unique; for instance $(U,V)$ may be replaced by $(-U,-V)$. Singular values are in general not eigenvalues of $A$.

Shapes 20:52. For $m\gt n$ the matrix $D$ has zero rows below the diagonal block; for $m\lt n$ it has zero columns to the right; for $m=n$ it is an ordinary diagonal matrix.

On the running example. $A^{\mathsf T}A=\begin{pmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $45,5$ with unit eigenvectors $v_1=(1,1)/\sqrt2$, $v_2=(1,-1)/\sqrt2$. So $\sigma_1=3\sqrt5$, $\sigma_2=\sqrt5$, and $u_j=Av_j/\sigma_j$ gives $u_1=(1,3)/\sqrt{10}$, $u_2=(3,-1)/\sqrt{10}$. The unit circle is mapped to an ellipse with semi-axes $3\sqrt5$ along $u_1$ and $\sqrt5$ along $u_2$. Note $\sigma_1\sigma_2=15=|\det A|$.

3. A rank-one example in full 30:40

Let $$B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1-i\\1+i&2\\1&1-i\end{pmatrix},\qquad B^*B=4\begin{pmatrix}1&1-i\\1+i&2\end{pmatrix}.$$

The eigenvalues of $B^*B$ are $12$ and $0$, with orthogonal eigenvectors $(1,1+i)$ and $(1-i,-1)$, each of squared norm $3$. So $r=1$, $\sigma_1=2\sqrt3$ and $V$ has these vectors divided by $\sqrt3$ as columns. Then $Bv_1=\sqrt3\,(1,1+i,1)$, so $u_1=\frac12(1,1+i,1)$.

Completing $U$ 34:19. The list $u_1,e_2,e_3$ is a basis of $\mathbb{C}^3$. Gram–Schmidt gives

$$x_2=\tfrac14(-1+i,\;2,\;-1+i),\qquad x_3=\tfrac12(-1,0,1),$$

each of squared norm $\tfrac12$. So $u_2=\sqrt2\,x_2$ and $u_3=\sqrt2\,x_3$, and $U^*BV$ has the single nonzero entry $2\sqrt3$ in position $(1,1)$. The choice of $u_2,u_3$ is arbitrary within the orthogonal complement of $u_1$.

4. Sesquilinear forms 03:56

Definition 4.1. Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$. A sesquilinear form on $V$ is a function $f:V\times V\to F$ with

$$f(c\alpha+\beta,\gamma)=cf(\alpha,\gamma)+f(\beta,\gamma),\qquad f(\alpha,c\beta+\gamma)=\bar c\,f(\alpha,\beta)+f(\alpha,\gamma).$$

It is linear in the first argument and conjugate-linear in the second. Over $\mathbb{R}$ it is bilinear. Sums and scalar multiples of forms are forms, so the forms make up a vector space. An inner product is a form; so is $f(x,y)=\langle Ax,Ay\rangle$.

5. Forms are operators 10:59

From here to the end, $V$ is a finite-dimensional inner product space.

Theorem 5.1. For every form $f$ there is a unique operator $T_f$ with $f(\alpha,\beta)=\langle T_f\alpha,\beta\rangle$. The map $f\mapsto T_f$ is a linear isomorphism from the space of forms onto the space of operators on $V$.

Proof. For fixed $\beta$, $\alpha\mapsto f(\alpha,\beta)$ is a linear functional. By the representation theorem of Chapter 23 there is a unique $U\beta$ with $f(\alpha,\beta)=\langle\alpha,U\beta\rangle$. The identity

$$\langle\alpha,U(c\beta+\gamma)\rangle=f(\alpha,c\beta+\gamma)=\bar c\,f(\alpha,\beta)+f(\alpha,\gamma)=\langle\alpha,cU\beta+U\gamma\rangle$$

shows that $U$ is linear. Put $T_f=U^*$; then $f(\alpha,\beta)=\langle\alpha,U\beta\rangle=\langle T_f\alpha,\beta\rangle$. If $T'$ also works, then $\langle(T_f-T')\alpha,\beta\rangle=0$ for all $\alpha,\beta$, so $T'=T_f$. Linearity of $f\mapsto T_f$ follows from uniqueness, since $cT_f+T_g$ represents $cf+g$. The map is injective because $T_f=0$ forces $f=0$, and surjective because every operator $T$ defines the form $\langle T\alpha,\beta\rangle$. $\square$

An inner product on forms 27:47. Transporting $\operatorname{tr}(ST^*)$ through the isomorphism, $\langle f,g\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(T_fT_g^*)$ is an inner product on forms. For any orthonormal basis $(\alpha_j)$,

$$\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{j,k}f(\alpha_k,\alpha_j)\,\overline{g(\alpha_k,\alpha_j)},$$

because the matrix of $T_f$ has the entries $\langle T_f\alpha_k,\alpha_j\rangle=f(\alpha_k,\alpha_j)$ in row $j$, column $k$. So the right side does not depend on the chosen orthonormal basis.

6. The matrix of a form 34:05

Definition 6.1. For an ordered basis $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ the matrix of $f$ is given by $M_{jk}=f(\alpha_k,\alpha_j)$ (note the order).

With coordinate columns $X,Y$ of $\alpha,\beta$ this gives $f(\alpha,\beta)=Y^*MX$. If $G$ is the Gram matrix of $\mathcal{B}$, then $\langle T_f\alpha,\beta\rangle=Y^*G\,[T_f]_{\mathcal{B}}X$, so

$$M=G\,[T_f]_{\mathcal{B}} .$$

In an orthonormal basis $G=I$ and the two matrices coincide. In another basis they usually differ: for $f$ equal to the inner product itself, $T_f=I$ but $M=G$. They also change differently. If $P$ expresses a new basis in the old one, the matrix of the form becomes $P^*MP$, while the matrix of an operator becomes $P^{-1}[T]P$. The two rules agree when $P$ is unitary.

7. Hermitian forms and principal axes 37:47

Definition 7.1. A form is Hermitian if $f(\alpha,\beta)=\overline{f(\beta,\alpha)}$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ this means symmetric.

Proposition 7.2. $f$ is Hermitian if and only if $T_f$ is self-adjoint.

Proof. $\overline{f(\beta,\alpha)}=\overline{\langle T_f\beta,\alpha\rangle}=\langle\alpha,T_f\beta\rangle$. So $f$ is Hermitian exactly when $\langle T_f\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,T_f\beta\rangle$ for all $\alpha,\beta$. $\square$

Theorem 7.3 (principal axes) 41:13. For a Hermitian form $f$ there is an orthonormal basis of $V$ in which the matrix of $f$ is diagonal with real entries.

Proof. $T_f$ is self-adjoint, so there is an orthonormal basis with $T_f\alpha_k=c_k\alpha_k$ and $c_k$ real. Then $f(\alpha_k,\alpha_j)=\langle c_k\alpha_k,\alpha_j\rangle=c_k\delta_{jk}$. $\square$

In these coordinates $f(\alpha,\alpha)=\sum_kc_k|x_k|^2$. Consequently $f$ is an inner product on $V$ exactly when all $c_k$ are positive: if they are, the sum is positive for $\alpha\ne0$; and $f(\alpha_k,\alpha_k)=c_k$ shows necessity.

On the running example. The form $f(x,y)=\langle Ax,Ay\rangle$ has $T_f=A^{\mathsf T}A$. Its principal axes are the right singular vectors and its diagonal entries are $\sigma_1^2=45$, $\sigma_2^2=5$:

$$\|Ax\|^2=25x_1^2+40x_1x_2+25x_2^2=45s^2+5t^2,\qquad s=\frac{x_1+x_2}{\sqrt2},\ t=\frac{x_1-x_2}{\sqrt2}.$$

The singular value decomposition of $A$ is therefore the principal axis theorem for $\|Ax\|^2$, together with the images of the axes.

8. Executable check

The chapter laboratory uses integer, rational and Gaussian-integer arithmetic where the data allow it and floating point with a stated tolerance where square roots occur. It verifies $A^{\mathsf T}A$ and the singular vectors of the running example, the factorization of $B$ with its completed unitary matrix, the identity $M=G[T_f]$ in a non-orthonormal basis, the basis independence of the form inner product on an example, and the exercise data. It does not check the general theorems.

Exercises

  1. (Computation.) Find a singular value decomposition of $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$.
  2. (Normal matrices.) Show that the singular values of a normal matrix are the absolute values of its nonzero eigenvalues.
  3. (Extremes.) For $A\in F^{m\times n}$ of rank $r\ge1$ show that the maximum of $\|Ax\|$ over $\|x\|=1$ is $\sigma_1$. Find the minimum.
  4. (Adjoint.) Show that $A$ and $A^*$ have the same singular values.
  5. (Principal axes.) Bring the curve $5x_1^2-6x_1x_2+5x_2^2=1$ to principal axes and find the lengths of its semi-axes.
  6. (Boundary.) For the real form $f(x,y)=x_1y_2-x_2y_1$ show that no basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ gives it a diagonal matrix. What happens for the same formula $x_1\bar y_2-x_2\bar y_1$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$?
Fully worked solutions

  1. Call the matrix $N$. $N^{\mathsf T}N=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $2,0$ with unit eigenvectors $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ and $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$. So $\sigma_1=\sqrt2$ and $u_1=Nv_1/\sqrt2=(1,0)$; take $u_2=(0,1)$. Then $U=I$, $D=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt2,0)$, $V=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$.

  2. Over $\mathbb{C}$ (a real matrix is also a complex one with the same $A^*A$), the spectral theorem gives an orthonormal basis with $Av_j=c_jv_j$ and $A^*v_j=\bar c_jv_j$. Then $A^*Av_j=|c_j|^2v_j$. So the nonzero eigenvalues of $A^*A$, with multiplicity, are the $|c_j|^2\ne0$, and the singular values are the $|c_j|$.

  3. Write $x=\sum_jy_jv_j$ with $\sum|y_j|^2=1$. By the proof of Theorem 2.1 the images $Av_j$ are orthogonal with $\|Av_j\|=\sigma_j$ (zero for $j\gt r$). So $\|Ax\|^2=\sum_{j\le r}\sigma_j^2|y_j|^2\le\sigma_1^2$, with equality at $x=v_1$. The minimum is attained at $v_n$: it is $\sigma_n$ if $r=n$ and $0$ if $r\lt n$.

  4. From $A=UDV^*$ follows $A^*=VD^*U^*$. This is a factorization of the same type for $A^*$, since $D^*$ has the real diagonal entries $\sigma_j$. By the uniqueness of singular values, they are the singular values of $A^*$.

  5. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix}5&-3\\-3&5\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $2$ on $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ and $8$ on $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$. In the coordinates $s,t$ of Section 7 the curve is $2s^2+8t^2=1$, an ellipse with semi-axes $1/\sqrt2$ along $(1,1)$ and $1/\sqrt8$ along $(1,-1)$.

  6. A diagonal matrix $M$ in a real basis would make $f(\alpha,\beta)=Y^{\mathsf T}MX$ symmetric. But $f(e_1,e_2)=1$ and $f(e_2,e_1)=-1$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the standard matrix is $M=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=T_f$, which is normal, with eigenvalues $\pm i$ and orthogonal eigenvectors. So there is an orthonormal basis making the matrix diagonal, with imaginary entries; the form is not Hermitian, but $if$ is.

"""Chapter 26 laboratory: singular values and forms.

Integer and Gaussian-integer identities are checked exactly (Python
complex is exact for these small values).  Where square roots occur the
check uses floating point with numpy.allclose.  The general theorems are
proved in the chapter.
"""
from fractions import Fraction as Fr

import numpy as np


def running_example():
    a = np.array([[3, 0], [4, 5]])
    assert np.array_equal(a.T @ a, [[25, 20], [20, 25]])
    for v, lam in (([1, 1], 45), ([1, -1], 5)):
        assert np.array_equal(a.T @ a @ v, lam * np.array(v))
    v = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2)
    u = np.array([[1, 3], [3, -1]]) / np.sqrt(10)
    d = np.diag([3 * np.sqrt(5), np.sqrt(5)])
    assert np.allclose(u.T @ u, np.eye(2))
    assert np.allclose(u @ d @ v.T, a)
    assert np.allclose(np.linalg.svd(a)[1], np.diag(d))
    assert np.isclose(d[0, 0] * d[1, 1], 15)
    for x1, x2 in ((1, 0), (2, -3), (5, 7)):
        s2, t2 = Fr((x1 + x2) ** 2, 2), Fr((x1 - x2) ** 2, 2)
        y = a @ [x1, x2]
        q = 25 * x1 ** 2 + 40 * x1 * x2 + 25 * x2 ** 2
        assert int(y @ y) == q == 45 * s2 + 5 * t2
    print('running example: sigma^2 = 45, 5; U D V^T = A')


def rank_one_example():
    b = np.array([[1, 1 - 1j], [1 + 1j, 2], [1, 1 - 1j]])
    h = b.conj().T @ b
    assert np.array_equal(h, 4 * np.array([[1, 1 - 1j], [1 + 1j, 2]]))
    v1 = np.array([1, 1 + 1j])
    v0 = np.array([1 - 1j, -1])
    assert np.array_equal(h @ v1, 12 * v1)
    assert np.array_equal(h @ v0, 0 * v0)
    assert np.vdot(v1, v0) == 0
    assert np.array_equal(b @ v1, 3 * np.array([1, 1 + 1j, 1]))
    u1 = np.array([1, 1 + 1j, 1]) / 2
    x2 = np.array([-1 + 1j, 2, -1 + 1j]) / 4
    x3 = np.array([-1, 0, 1]) / 2
    e2, e3 = np.array([0, 1, 0]), np.array([0, 0, 1])
    assert np.allclose(x2, e2 - np.vdot(u1, e2) * u1)
    w = e3 - np.vdot(u1, e3) * u1
    w = w - np.vdot(x2, e3) / np.vdot(x2, x2) * x2
    assert np.allclose(x3, w)
    u = np.column_stack([u1, np.sqrt(2) * x2, np.sqrt(2) * x3])
    v = np.column_stack([v1, v0]) / np.sqrt(3)
    d = np.zeros((3, 2))
    d[0, 0] = 2 * np.sqrt(3)
    assert np.allclose(u.conj().T @ u, np.eye(3))
    assert np.allclose(u.conj().T @ b @ v, d)
    print('rank-one example: sigma = 2 sqrt 3, U completed')


def forms():
    # f(a, b) = <T a, b> in the basis (1,0), (1,1)
    t = np.array([[1, 2], [0, 3]])
    p = np.array([[1, 1], [0, 1]])
    pinv = np.array([[1, -1], [0, 1]])

    def f(x, y):
        return np.vdot(y, t @ x)

    m = np.array([[f(p[:, k], p[:, j]) for k in range(2)]
                  for j in range(2)])
    gram = p.T @ p
    op = pinv @ t @ p
    assert np.array_equal(m, gram @ op)
    assert np.array_equal(m, p.T @ t @ p)
    assert not np.array_equal(m, op)
    s = np.array([[0, 1j], [2, 1]])
    c, sn = Fr(3, 5), Fr(4, 5)
    basis = np.array([[c, -sn], [sn, c]], dtype=float)
    total = sum(np.vdot(basis[:, j], s @ basis[:, k])
                * np.conj(np.vdot(basis[:, j], t @ basis[:, k]))
                for j in range(2) for k in range(2))
    assert np.isclose(total, np.trace(s @ t.conj().T))
    print('form matrices and basis independence verified')


def exercises():
    n = np.array([[1, 1], [0, 0]])
    v = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2)
    assert np.allclose(np.diag([np.sqrt(2), 0]) @ v.T, n)
    q = np.array([[5, -3], [-3, 5]])
    assert np.array_equal(q @ [1, 1], [2, 2])
    assert np.array_equal(q @ [1, -1], [8, -8])
    m = np.array([[0, -1], [1, 0]])
    assert np.array_equal(m @ [1, -1j], 1j * np.array([1, -1j]))
    assert np.array_equal(1j * m, (1j * m).conj().T)
    print('exercise data verified')


def main():
    running_example()
    rank_one_example()
    forms()
    exercises()
    print('all chapter 26 checks passed')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
