Given the three vectors

$$ \alpha_1=(1,0,1),\qquad\alpha_2=(1,1,0),\qquad\alpha_3=(0,1,1) $$

of $\mathbb{Q}^3$, how can we find the coordinates of an arbitrary vector $v$ in the basis $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)$ without solving a new system for each $v$? Each coordinate is a number that depends linearly on $v$. So the answer should be three linear rules $f_1,f_2,f_3$, each taking a vector to a number. For $v=(x,y,z)$ they turn out to be

$$ f_1(v)=\tfrac12(x-y+z),\quad f_2(v)=\tfrac12(x+y-z),\quad f_3(v)=\tfrac12(-x+y+z). $$

For $v=(3,1,2)$ they give $2,1,0$, and indeed $2\alpha_1+\alpha_2=(3,1,2)$. These three rules are the running example of the chapter (an original construction). Linear rules with values in the field are called linear functionals. They form a vector space of their own, the dual space; the rules above form its basis dual to $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)$. The same language describes evaluation of polynomials, integrals, traces, and the equations that cut out a subspace.

Throughout, $F$ is a field and $V$ a vector space over $F$. Finite dimension is stated where used. A subspace, basis, dimension and linear map are as in Chapter 4 and Chapter 7.

1. Linear functionals 00:25

Definition 1.1. A linear functional on $V$ is a linear map $f:V\to F$, where $F$ is regarded as a one-dimensional vector space over itself:

$$ f(c\alpha+\beta)=cf(\alpha)+f(\beta)\qquad(\alpha,\beta\in V,\ c\in F). $$

Coordinate formulas. Fix scalars $c_1,\dots,c_n$ and define $f:F^n\to F$ by $f(x)=c_1x_1+\cdots+c_nx_n$. This is linear by distributivity. Every linear functional on $F^n$ has this form: with $e_i$ the unit vectors, $x=\sum x_ie_i$, so $f(x)=\sum x_if(e_i)$. The coefficient $c_i$ is the value $f(e_i)$.

2. Further examples 11:14

Trace. On the space $F^{n\times n}$ of square matrices, $\operatorname{tr}A=\sum_{i=1}^nA_{ii}$ is linear, since $\operatorname{tr}(cA+B)=c\operatorname{tr}A+\operatorname{tr}B$.

Evaluation. Let $V$ be the space of all polynomials over $F$ (infinite-dimensional) and $t\in F$. The map $\mathrm{ev}_t(p)=p(t)$ is linear.

Integration. Let $V=C[a,b]$ be the real vector space of continuous functions on $[a,b]$. Then $L(g)=\int_a^bg(t)\,dt$ is a linear functional, by linearity of the integral.

A non-example: $g\mapsto\int_a^bg(t)^2\,dt$ is not linear, since scaling $g$ by $2$ multiplies the value by $4$.

3. The dual space 17:34

Definition 3.1. The dual space of $V$ is $V^*=\mathcal{L}(V,F)$, the space of all linear functionals on $V$, with pointwise addition and scalar multiplication (Chapter 8).

If $\dim V=n$, then $\dim V^*=n\cdot1=n$ by the dimension formula for $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$. A concrete basis comes next.

4. The dual basis 21:46

Let $\delta_{ij}$ denote $1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise.

Theorem 4.1. Let $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ be an ordered basis of $V$.

  1. There is a unique list $\mathcal{B}^*=(f_1,\dots,f_n)$ in $V^*$ with $f_i(\alpha_j)=\delta_{ij}$, and it is an ordered basis of $V^*$, the dual basis of $\mathcal{B}$.
  2. For every $f\in V^*$, $f=\sum_{i=1}^nf(\alpha_i)f_i$.
  3. For every $\alpha\in V$, $\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^nf_i(\alpha)\alpha_i$.

Proof. A linear map is determined by its values on a basis, and these may be chosen freely (Chapter 7). So for each $i$ there is exactly one $f_i$ with $f_i(\alpha_j)=\delta_{ij}$. For independence suppose $\sum c_if_i=0$, the zero functional. Evaluate at $\alpha_j$: $\sum_ic_i\delta_{ij}=c_j=0$. So $f_1,\dots,f_n$ are $n$ independent elements of the $n$-dimensional space $V^*$, hence a basis. For part 2, evaluate the right side at $\alpha_j$: $\sum_if(\alpha_i)\delta_{ij}=f(\alpha_j)$. Both sides are linear and agree on a basis, so they are equal. For part 3, write $\alpha=\sum x_j\alpha_j$ and apply $f_i$: $f_i(\alpha)=\sum_jx_j\delta_{ij}=x_i$. $\square$

So the dual basis is precisely the list of coordinate functions: the $i$th coordinate of $\alpha$ is $f_i(\alpha)$ and, dually, the $i$th coordinate of $f$ in $\mathcal{B}^*$ is $f(\alpha_i)$:

$$ [\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=\big(f_1(\alpha),\dots,f_n(\alpha)\big)^{\mathsf T},\qquad[f]_{\mathcal{B}^*}=\big(f(\alpha_1),\dots,f(\alpha_n)\big)^{\mathsf T}. $$

Here the superscript $\mathsf T$ turns a row into a column.

Running example. To find $f_i$, we need $f_i(\alpha_j)=\delta_{ij}$. Writing the $\alpha_j$ as columns of a matrix $M$ and the coefficient rows of the $f_i$ as rows of a matrix $N$, the condition reads $NM=I$. So the rows of $M^{-1}$ are the coefficients of the dual basis, which gives the formulas of the opening. For $f(x,y,z)=2x-y+3z$ the coordinates in $\mathcal{B}^*$ are the values $f(\alpha_1)=5$, $f(\alpha_2)=1$, $f(\alpha_3)=2$; so $f=5f_1+f_2+2f_3$.

The standard basis is dual to itself in coefficient form: the dual basis of $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ is the list of coordinate functionals $x\mapsto x_i$.

Boundary: infinite dimension. On the space $F[x]$ of all polynomials, let $f_j(p)$ be the coefficient of $x^j$. Then $f_j(x^k)=\delta_{jk}$, but the $f_j$ do not span the dual space. A finite combination of $f_0,\dots,f_N$ vanishes on $x^{N+1}$, while $\mathrm{ev}_1(x^{N+1})=1$. So $\mathrm{ev}_1$ is not a finite combination of them, for any $N$. Theorem 4.1 needs finite dimension.

5. Evaluation functionals and interpolation 00:25

Let $P_2$ be the space of polynomials of degree at most $2$ over $F$, and let $t_1,t_2,t_3\in F$ be distinct. Define $L_i(p)=p(t_i)$. We show that $(L_1,L_2,L_3)$ is a basis of $P_2^*$. Since $\dim P_2^*=3$, independence suffices. Suppose $c_1L_1+c_2L_2+c_3L_3=0$. Applying this to $1,x,x^2$ gives

$$ \begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\t_1&t_2&t_3\\t_1^2&t_2^2&t_3^2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\\c_3\end{pmatrix}=0 . $$

Row operations turn this matrix into an upper triangular one with diagonal $1$, $t_2-t_1$ and $(t_3-t_1)(t_3-t_2)$. All are nonzero since the $t_i$ are distinct, so the matrix is invertible and $c_1=c_2=c_3=0$.

The basis of $P_2$ to which $(L_1,L_2,L_3)$ is dual 11:07. We want polynomials $p_1,p_2,p_3\in P_2$ with $L_i(p_j)=\delta_{ij}$. Write $p_j=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2$; the three conditions are a linear system with the transpose of the matrix above, which is invertible, so $p_j$ exists and is unique. We can also write it down:

$$ p_j(x)=\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{x-t_k}{t_j-t_k}, $$

the Lagrange polynomial: it equals $1$ at $t_j$ and $0$ at the other nodes. Theorem 4.1(3), applied to the basis $(p_1,p_2,p_3)$, now reads

$$ p=p(t_1)\,p_1+p(t_2)\,p_2+p(t_3)\,p_3\qquad(p\in P_2).\tag{5.1} $$

A polynomial of degree at most $2$ is recovered from its values at three points. For $t=(0,1,3)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$,

$$ p_1=1-\tfrac43x+\tfrac13x^2,\qquad p_2=\tfrac32x-\tfrac12x^2,\qquad p_3=-\tfrac16x+\tfrac16x^2, $$

and for $p=2-x+4x^2$ the values are $2,5,35$, so $p=2p_1+5p_2+35p_3$. The determinant of the matrix is $(t_2-t_1)(t_3-t_1)(t_3-t_2)=6$.

The argument extends to $n+1$ distinct points and $P_n$, using the same product formula. It needs $F$ to have at least $n+1$ elements: over $\mathbb{F}_2$ there are only two points, and the functions $x$ and $x^2$ coincide. If two nodes coincide, the functionals are equal and cannot be independent.

6. Null spaces of functionals: hyperspaces 19:25

Let $f\in V^*$ with $\dim V=n$. The range of $f$ is a subspace of the one-dimensional space $F$. So either $f=0$, with null space $V$, or $f$ has rank $1$ and, by rank-nullity, $\dim\ker f=n-1$.

Definition 6.1. A subspace of dimension $n-1$ of an $n$-dimensional space is a hyperspace (also called a hyperplane).

So the kernel of a nonzero functional is a hyperspace. Conversely every hyperspace $H$ is such a kernel: extend a basis $(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{n-1})$ of $H$ by $\alpha_n$ and take the dual basis; then $\ker f_n=H$. In the running example, $\ker f_3$ is the plane spanned by $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$.

7. Annihilators 24:02

Definition 7.1. For a subset $S\subseteq V$, the annihilator of $S$ is

$$ S^0=\{f\in V^*: f(\alpha)=0\text{ for all }\alpha\in S\}. $$

$S^0$ is a subspace of $V^*$ (not of $V$), even if $S$ is only a set: if $f,g$ vanish on $S$, so does $cf+g$. Also $S^0=(\operatorname{span}S)^0$, because a functional that vanishes on $S$ vanishes on every combination of elements of $S$.

Theorem 7.2 28:26. Let $\dim V=n$ and let $W$ be a subspace of $V$. Then $\dim W+\dim W^0=n$.

Proof. Let $(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k)$ be a basis of $W$, extend it to a basis $(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ of $V$, and let $(f_1,\dots,f_n)$ be the dual basis. We show that $(f_{k+1},\dots,f_n)$ is a basis of $W^0$. Each $f_j$ with $j\gt k$ vanishes on $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k$, hence on $W$. They are independent, being part of a basis. For spanning, let $f\in W^0$. By Theorem 4.1(2), $f=\sum_{i=1}^nf(\alpha_i)f_i$, and $f(\alpha_i)=0$ for $i\le k$. So $f=\sum_{i\gt k}f(\alpha_i)f_i$. Hence $\dim W^0=n-k$. $\square$

The hinge is Theorem 4.1(2): the coefficients of $f$ are its values, so vanishing on $W$ kills exactly the first $k$ coefficients. For a set $S$ that is not a subspace, the formula is applied to its span.

8. Every subspace is an intersection of hyperspaces 00:25

Theorem 8.1. Let $\dim V=n$ and $\dim W=k$. Then $W$ is the intersection of $n-k$ hyperspaces, and it is not an intersection of fewer.

Proof. With the dual basis of the proof of Theorem 7.2, put $H_j=\ker f_j$ for $j\gt k$. Then $W\subseteq\bigcap H_j$. Conversely, let $\alpha\in\bigcap H_j$. By Theorem 4.1(3), $\alpha=\sum f_i(\alpha)\alpha_i$, and $f_i(\alpha)=0$ for $i\gt k$, so $\alpha$ is a combination of $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k$, that is, $\alpha\in W$. (For $k=n$ the empty intersection is $V$.) For the second claim, take hyperspaces $\ker g_1,\dots,\ker g_r$. The linear map $\alpha\mapsto(g_1\alpha,\dots,g_r\alpha)$ from $V$ to $F^r$ has rank at most $r$, so its kernel $\bigcap\ker g_i$ has dimension at least $n-r$. If the intersection is $W$, then $k\ge n-r$, so $r\ge n-k$. $\square$

A worked example 02:32. Let $W\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^5$ be spanned by

$$ (1,2,0,1,1),\quad(0,1,1,-1,2),\quad(0,0,1,1,-1),\quad(1,4,1,-2,6). $$

Row reduction of these as rows gives the nonzero rows $(1,0,0,5,-5)$, $(0,1,0,-2,3)$, $(0,0,1,1,-1)$, so $\dim W=3$ and $W$ is an intersection of $5-3=2$ hyperspaces. A functional $g(x)=c_1x_1+\cdots+c_5x_5$ annihilates $W$ exactly when it vanishes on these three rows:

$$ c_1+5c_4-5c_5=0,\quad c_2-2c_4+3c_5=0,\quad c_3+c_4-c_5=0 . $$

Taking $(c_4,c_5)=(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ gives the basis of $W^0$:

$$ g_1=-5x_1+2x_2-x_3+x_4,\qquad g_2=5x_1-3x_2+x_3+x_5 . $$

So $W=\ker g_1\cap\ker g_2$. Check on the fourth spanning vector: $g_1=-5+8-1-2=0$ and $g_2=5-12+1+6=0$. A single hyperspace has dimension $4$, so one equation is not enough.

9. From functionals back to subspaces 15:12

Conversely, given functionals $g_1,\dots,g_m$ on $F^n$, $g_i(x)=\sum_jA_{ij}x_j$, the subspace annihilated by all of them is the solution space of $AX=0$. Row reduction tells how many are independent: if $A$ has rank $r$, the span of the $g_i$ has dimension $r$ and the solution space has dimension $n-r$. For

$$ A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1\\0&1&1&-1\\1&3&1&0\end{pmatrix} $$

the third row is the sum of the first two, so $r=2$ and the subspace has dimension $4-2=2$, spanned by $(2,-1,1,0)$ and $(-3,1,0,1)$. The third functional adds no condition.

Exercises

  1. (Computation.) Find the dual basis of $\big((1,1),(1,-1)\big)$ in $\mathbb{Q}^2$. Where does the answer need $2\neq0$?
  2. (Quadrature.) On $P_2(\mathbb{Q})$, express the functional $I(p)=\int_0^1p(t)\,dt$ in the basis $L_1,L_2,L_3$ of evaluations at $0,\tfrac12,1$.
  3. (Representation.) Show that every linear functional $f$ on $F^{n\times n}$ has the form $f(A)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)$ for exactly one $B\in F^{n\times n}$.
  4. (Proof.) Let $f,g\in V^*$ with $f\neq0$ and $\ker f\subseteq\ker g$. Show that $g=cf$ for some $c\in F$.
  5. (Counting.) How many hyperspaces does $\mathbb{F}_p^n$ have?
  6. (Annihilator.) Find a basis of the annihilator of $W=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,0,0),(0,1,1,0)\}$ in $\mathbb{Q}^4$, and check Theorem 7.2.
Fully worked solutions

  1. Let $M$ have the columns $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$. Its inverse is $\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$. So $f_1(x,y)=(x+y)/2$ and $f_2(x,y)=(x-y)/2$. Check: $f_1(1,1)=1$, $f_1(1,-1)=0$, $f_2(1,1)=0$, $f_2(1,-1)=1$. The division by $2$ needs $2\neq0$. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ the two vectors are equal, so they are not a basis at all.

  2. By Theorem 4.1(2) applied to the basis $(p_1,p_2,p_3)$ of $P_2$ dual to $(L_1,L_2,L_3)$, the coefficients are $I(p_j)$. With nodes $0,\tfrac12,1$: $p_1=(2x-1)(x-1)=2x^2-3x+1$, $p_2=4x(1-x)=4x-4x^2$, $p_3=x(2x-1)=2x^2-x$. Their integrals over $[0,1]$ are $\frac23-\frac32+1=\frac16$, $2-\frac43=\frac23$, and $\frac23-\frac12=\frac16$. So $I=\frac16L_1+\frac23L_2+\frac16L_3$, which is Simpson's rule, exact on $P_2$.

  3. Let $E_{ij}$ be the matrix units. $\operatorname{tr}(BE_{ij})=B_{ji}$. So if $f(A)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)$ for all $A$, then necessarily $B_{ji}=f(E_{ij})$; this gives uniqueness. For existence define $B$ by this formula. Then $A\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(BA)$ and $f$ are linear and agree on the basis $\{E_{ij}\}$, so they are equal.

  4. Choose $v$ with $f(v)=1$, which exists since $f\neq0$ (scale a vector on which $f$ is nonzero). For any $\alpha\in V$, $\alpha-f(\alpha)v$ lies in $\ker f$, since $f$ of it is $f(\alpha)-f(\alpha)=0$. So it lies in $\ker g$: $g(\alpha)=f(\alpha)g(v)$. Hence $g=cf$ with $c=g(v)$. No finite dimension is needed.

  5. Each hyperspace is the kernel of a nonzero functional, and by Exercise 4 two nonzero functionals have the same kernel exactly when one is a nonzero multiple of the other (if $\ker f=\ker g$ then $g=cf$, and $c\neq0$ since $g\neq0$). There are $p^n-1$ nonzero functionals and each kernel arises from $p-1$ of them. So there are $(p^n-1)/(p-1)$ hyperspaces; for $p=2$, $n=2$ there are $3$ lines.

  6. A functional $c_1x_1+\cdots+c_4x_4$ annihilates $W$ when $c_1+c_2=0$ and $c_2+c_3=0$, with $c_4$ free. Taking $c_2=-1$, $c_4=0$ gives $(1,-1,1,0)$, and $c_2=0$, $c_4=1$ gives $(0,0,0,1)$. So $\dim W^0=2$, and $\dim W+\dim W^0=2+2=4$.

"""Chapter 10 laboratory: linear functionals, dual bases, annihilators.

Exact arithmetic over Q with fractions.Fraction.  A vector in Q^n is a
list of n Fractions.  A linear functional on Q^n is stored by its row of
coefficients, so f(x) = sum_i c_i x_i.  A matrix is a list of rows.  Every
routine returns new objects and never changes its input.

The laboratory checks, in exact arithmetic and on one fixed example each:
  1. the dual basis of a basis of Q^3 is given by the rows of P^{-1};
  2. coordinates and functional expansions (Theorem 10.2);
  3. the trace functional and its dual basis on 2x2 matrices;
  4. evaluation functionals on polynomials of degree at most 2, and the
     dual basis they induce (the Lagrange polynomials);
  5. the boundary cases: repeated nodes, F_2, infinite dimension;
  6. dim W + dim W^0 = dim V and W as an intersection of hyperspaces;
  7. the converse problem: the common null space of given functionals.
It does not prove anything for general fields or dimensions.
"""
from fractions import Fraction


def Q(rows):
    """Matrix over Q from integer or Fraction entries."""
    return [[Fraction(x) for x in row] for row in rows]


def matmul(A, B):
    return [[sum((A[i][k] * B[k][j] for k in range(len(B))), Fraction(0))
             for j in range(len(B[0]))] for i in range(len(A))]


def transpose(A):
    return [list(col) for col in zip(*A)]


def identity(n):
    return [[Fraction(int(i == j)) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def rref(A):
    """Return (R, pivots): reduced row echelon form and pivot columns."""
    R = [row[:] for row in A]
    m, n = len(R), len(R[0])
    pivots = []
    r = 0
    for c in range(n):
        if r == m:
            break
        p = next((i for i in range(r, m) if R[i][c] != 0), None)
        if p is None:
            continue
        R[r], R[p] = R[p], R[r]
        piv = R[r][c]
        R[r] = [x / piv for x in R[r]]
        for i in range(m):
            if i != r and R[i][c] != 0:
                f = R[i][c]
                R[i] = [a - f * b for a, b in zip(R[i], R[r])]
        pivots.append(c)
        r += 1
    return R, pivots


def rank(A):
    return len(rref(A)[1])


def nullspace(A):
    """Basis of {x : A x = 0} as a list of vectors."""
    R, pivots = rref(A)
    n = len(A[0])
    free = [c for c in range(n) if c not in pivots]
    basis = []
    for f in free:
        x = [Fraction(0)] * n
        x[f] = Fraction(1)
        for row, p in zip(R, pivots):
            x[p] = -row[f]
        basis.append(x)
    return basis


def inverse(P):
    """Inverse of a square matrix; raises ValueError if singular."""
    n = len(P)
    aug = [P[i][:] + identity(n)[i] for i in range(n)]
    R, pivots = rref(aug)
    if pivots[:n] != list(range(n)):
        raise ValueError('matrix is singular')
    return [row[n:] for row in R]


def apply(f, x):
    """Value of the functional with coefficient row f at the vector x."""
    return sum((a * b for a, b in zip(f, x)), Fraction(0))


def dual_basis(basis):
    """Coefficient rows (f_1, ..., f_n) with f_i(basis_j) = delta_ij.

    The basis vectors are the columns of P; the rows of P^{-1} are the
    functionals, because P^{-1} P = I.
    """
    P = transpose(basis)
    return inverse(P)


def coords(basis, x):
    """Coordinates of x relative to the ordered basis, via the dual basis."""
    return [apply(f, x) for f in dual_basis(basis)]


def poly_eval(p, t):
    """Value at t of the polynomial with coefficient list p = (p0, p1, ...)."""
    return sum((c * t ** k for k, c in enumerate(p)), Fraction(0))


def check_dual_basis_q3():
    alpha = Q([[1, 0, 1], [1, 1, 0], [0, 1, 1]])
    duals = dual_basis(alpha)
    print('dual basis of B in Q^3 (rows):')
    for row in duals:
        print('  ', [str(c) for c in row])
    for i, f in enumerate(duals):
        for j, a in enumerate(alpha):
            assert apply(f, a) == (1 if i == j else 0)
    v = Q([[3, 1, 2]])[0]
    c = coords(alpha, v)
    print('coordinates of (3,1,2):', [str(x) for x in c])
    assert c == [2, 1, 0]
    rebuilt = [sum(c[j] * alpha[j][k] for j in range(3)) for k in range(3)]
    assert rebuilt == v
    # Theorem 10.2: f = sum f(alpha_j) f_j for f(x, y, z) = 2x - y + 3z
    f = Q([[2, -1, 3]])[0]
    comp = [apply(f, a) for a in alpha]
    print('f = 2x - y + 3z has coordinates in B*:', [str(x) for x in comp])
    assert comp == [5, 1, 2]
    combo = [sum(comp[i] * duals[i][k] for i in range(3)) for k in range(3)]
    assert combo == f
    # The standard basis is its own dual basis: c_i = f(e_i).
    e = identity(3)
    assert dual_basis(e) == e
    assert [apply(f, ei) for ei in e] == f
    # Annihilator of W = span(alpha_1, alpha_2) is spanned by f_3.
    W = [alpha[0], alpha[1]]
    ann = nullspace(W)
    print('annihilator of span(alpha1, alpha2):',
          [[str(x) for x in r] for r in ann])
    assert len(ann) == 1
    assert ann[0] == [c * ann[0][0] / duals[2][0] for c in duals[2]]
    # A hyperplane is the kernel of one nonzero functional (rank 1).
    assert rank([duals[2]]) == 1 and len(nullspace([duals[2]])) == 2


def check_trace():
    # tr on 2x2 matrices, coordinates (a11, a12, a21, a22).
    tr = Q([[1, 0, 0, 1]])[0]
    A = Q([[1, 2, 3, 4]])[0]
    B = Q([[5, -1, 0, 7]])[0]
    c = Fraction(3)
    lin = [c * a + b for a, b in zip(A, B)]
    assert apply(tr, lin) == c * apply(tr, A) + apply(tr, B)
    # AB and BA need not be equal, but tr(AB) = tr(BA): not a functional
    # identity of one variable, so only the linear check is a claim here.
    ker = nullspace([tr])
    print('trace on 2x2 matrices: rank 1, nullity', len(ker))
    assert len(ker) == 3


def check_evaluation(nodes=(0, 1, 3)):
    t = [Fraction(x) for x in nodes]
    # Row i of the Vandermonde matrix is L_i applied to 1, x, x^2.
    V = [[ti ** k for k in range(3)] for ti in t]
    Vinv = inverse(V)
    print('Vandermonde inverse for nodes', nodes)
    for row in Vinv:
        print('  ', [str(x) for x in row])
    # Column j of Vinv holds the coefficients of p_j (L_i(p_j) = delta_ij).
    polys = [[Vinv[k][j] for k in range(3)] for j in range(3)]
    for i in range(3):
        for j in range(3):
            assert poly_eval(polys[j], t[i]) == (1 if i == j else 0)
    print('dual basis polynomials p_j (coefficients p0, p1, p2):')
    for p in polys:
        print('  ', [str(x) for x in p])
    # They are the Lagrange polynomials prod_{k != j} (x - t_k)/(t_j - t_k).
    for j in range(3):
        for x in [Fraction(-2), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(5)]:
            lag = Fraction(1)
            for k in range(3):
                if k != j:
                    lag *= (x - t[k]) / (t[j] - t[k])
            assert poly_eval(polys[j], x) == lag
    # Expansion p = sum p(t_j) p_j for p = 2 - x + 4x^2.
    p = Q([[2, -1, 4]])[0]
    rebuilt = [sum(poly_eval(p, t[j]) * polys[j][k] for j in range(3))
               for k in range(3)]
    print('p(t_j) for p = 2 - x + 4x^2:',
          [str(poly_eval(p, tj)) for tj in t])
    assert rebuilt == p
    # det of the Vandermonde matrix is prod_{i<j} (t_j - t_i).
    det = Fraction(1)
    for i in range(3):
        for j in range(i + 1, 3):
            det *= t[j] - t[i]
    print('prod (t_j - t_i) =', det)
    assert det == 6 and rank(V) == 3
    # Boundary: a repeated node makes the evaluation functionals dependent.
    bad = [[x ** k for k in range(3)] for x in Q([[0, 1, 1]])[0]]
    assert rank(bad) == 2
    try:
        inverse(bad)
        raise AssertionError('singular matrix was inverted')
    except ValueError:
        pass
    # Boundary: over F_2 the functions 1, x, x^2 on {0, 1} have rank 2.
    rows = [[1, 1], [0, 1], [0, 1]]   # (p(0), p(1)) for 1, x, x^2 over F_2
    r = 0
    for c in range(2):
        p = next((i for i in range(r, 3) if rows[i][c] % 2), None)
        if p is None:
            continue
        rows[r], rows[p] = rows[p], rows[r]
        for i in range(3):
            if i != r and rows[i][c] % 2:
                rows[i] = [(a + b) % 2 for a, b in zip(rows[i], rows[r])]
        r += 1
    print('rank of (1, x, x^2) as functions on F_2:', r)
    assert r == 2


def check_infinite():
    # On R[x], f_j(p) = coefficient of x^j are independent, but ev_1 is
    # not a finite combination: on x^(N+1) every f_j (j <= N) is zero
    # while ev_1(x^(N+1)) = 1.
    ok = 0
    for N in range(0, 21):
        mono = [Fraction(0)] * (N + 2)
        mono[N + 1] = Fraction(1)
        assert poly_eval(mono, Fraction(1)) == 1
        assert all(mono[j] == 0 for j in range(N + 1))
        ok += 1
    print('ev_1 separated from span(f_0..f_N) for N = 0..20:', ok, 'cases')


def check_annihilator():
    U = Q([[1, 2, 0, 1, 1],
           [0, 1, 1, -1, 2],
           [0, 0, 1, 1, -1],
           [1, 4, 1, -2, 6]])
    n = 5
    R, piv = rref(U)
    k = len(piv)
    print('RREF of the four spanning vectors of W (nonzero rows):')
    for row in R[:k]:
        print('  ', [str(x) for x in row])
    print('dim W =', k)
    ann = nullspace(U)
    print('dim W0 =', len(ann))
    assert k == 3 and len(ann) == n - k
    assert k + len(ann) == n
    print('basis of W0 (one functional per free column):')
    for row in ann:
        print('  ', [str(x) for x in row])
    # Every functional in ann kills every spanning vector.
    for f in ann:
        for u in U:
            assert apply(f, u) == 0
    # W equals the intersection of the two kernels: the common null space
    # of ann has dimension n - 2 = 3 = dim W and contains W.
    common = nullspace(ann)
    assert len(common) == k
    assert rank(common + U) == k
    # Fewer than n - k hyperspaces do not cut out W.
    assert len(nullspace([ann[0]])) == n - 1 != k
    # S^0 for a non-subspace S equals (span S)^0.
    S = [U[0], U[1]]
    assert len(nullspace(S)) == n - 2
    comb = [a + b for a, b in zip(U[0], U[1])]
    assert all(apply(f, comb) == 0 for f in nullspace(S))


def check_converse():
    # m = 3 functionals on Q^4 given by the rows of A; the third is the
    # sum of the first two, so r = 2 and dim W = 4 - 2 = 2.
    A = Q([[1, 2, 0, 1],
           [0, 1, 1, -1],
           [1, 3, 1, 0]])
    r = rank(A)
    W = nullspace(A)
    print('converse problem: r =', r, ', dim W =', len(W))
    for row in W:
        print('  ', [str(x) for x in row])
    assert r == 2 and len(W) == 4 - r
    for w in W:
        assert all(apply(f, w) == 0 for f in A)
    # Dropping the dependent functional changes nothing.
    assert len(nullspace(A[:2])) == len(W)
    assert rank(W + nullspace(A[:2])) == len(W)
