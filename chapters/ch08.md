# The algebra of linear transformations: sums, composition, invertibility, and isomorphism

Take the rational matrices

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1\\1&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix},
$$

and let $T,U:\mathbb{Q}^3\to\mathbb{Q}^2$ and $R:\mathbb{Q}^2\to\mathbb{Q}^2$ be the maps $X\mapsto AX$, $X\mapsto BX$ and $X\mapsto CX$. These maps are the running examples of the chapter (original constructions). We can add $T$ and $U$, rescale them, and follow $T$ by $R$. Which of these operations give linear maps again? Can $R$ be undone? Can $T$ be undone? And when are two vector spaces "the same" for the purposes of linear algebra? The answers organise the set of all linear maps into a vector space of its own, with a product on top, and they identify every $n$-dimensional space with $F^n$.

Throughout, $F$ is a field, and $V,W,Z$ are vector spaces over $F$. A map $T:V\to W$ is *linear* if $T(c\alpha+\beta)=cT\alpha+T\beta$ ([Chapter 7](ch07.md)); a linear map $V\to V$ is a *linear operator* on $V$. The *kernel* $\ker T$ is the set of vectors sent to zero, and the *range* $\operatorname{im}T$ is the set of images.

## 1. Sums and scalar multiples [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4DXAx2Qc3DM&t=25s)

**Definition 1.1.** For linear $T,U:V\to W$ and $c\in F$, define maps $T+U$ and $cT$ from $V$ to $W$ by

$$
(T+U)\alpha=T\alpha+U\alpha,\qquad(cT)\alpha=c\,(T\alpha).
$$

**Theorem 1.2.** $T+U$ and $cT$ are linear.

**Proof.** For $\alpha,\beta\in V$ and $d\in F$,

$$
(T+U)(d\alpha+\beta)=dT\alpha+T\beta+dU\alpha+U\beta=d\,(T+U)\alpha+(T+U)\beta,
$$

where the first step uses linearity of $T$ and of $U$ and the second regroups in $W$. Likewise

$$
(cT)(d\alpha+\beta)=c(dT\alpha+T\beta)=d\,(cT)\alpha+(cT)\beta .
$$

The last step uses $cd=dc$ in $F$. $\square$

Commutativity of the field is used: for scalars that do not commute, $cT$ need not be linear. For the running example, $T+U$ is $X\mapsto(A+B)X$ with $A+B=\begin{pmatrix}3&1&1\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix}$, and $2T$ has the matrix $2A$.

## 2. The space of linear maps [08:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4DXAx2Qc3DM&t=528s)

Write $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ for the set of all linear maps $V\to W$.

**Theorem 2.1.** With the operations of Definition 1.1, $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ is a vector space over $F$.

**Proof.** The set of all functions $V\to W$ is a vector space under pointwise operations: each axiom is checked at each $\alpha\in V$, where it is an axiom of $W$ ([Chapter 3](ch03.md)). $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ is a subset of it that contains the zero map and, by Theorem 1.2, is closed under sums and scalar multiples. So it is a subspace. $\square$

The zero vector is the zero map, and the additive inverse of $T$ is $(-1)T$. Neither $V$ nor $W$ needs to be finite-dimensional here.

## 3. Its dimension is $mn$ [15:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4DXAx2Qc3DM&t=912s)

**Theorem 3.1.** Let $\dim V=n$ and $\dim W=m$. Then $\dim\mathcal{L}(V,W)=mn$.

**Proof.** Fix ordered bases $(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ of $V$ and $(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_m)$ of $W$. A linear map is determined by, and can be prescribed arbitrarily on, the basis of $V$ ([Chapter 7](ch07.md)). So for $1\le p\le m$ and $1\le q\le n$ there is a unique linear map $E^{pq}$ with

$$
E^{pq}\alpha_j=\delta_{jq}\beta_p,
$$

where $\delta_{jq}$ is $1$ if $j=q$ and $0$ otherwise. So $E^{pq}$ sends $\alpha_q$ to $\beta_p$ and every other basis vector to zero. We show that the $mn$ maps $E^{pq}$ form a basis.

*Spanning.* Let $T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Write $T\alpha_j=\sum_{i=1}^mA_{ij}\beta_i$ with $A_{ij}\in F$, and let $S=\sum_{p,q}A_{pq}E^{pq}$. Then

$$
S\alpha_j=\sum_{p,q}A_{pq}\delta_{jq}\beta_p=\sum_pA_{pj}\beta_p=T\alpha_j .
$$

$S$ and $T$ agree on a basis, so $S=T$.

*Independence.* If $\sum_{p,q}A_{pq}E^{pq}=0$, apply both sides to $\alpha_j$: $\sum_pA_{pj}\beta_p=0$. Independence of the $\beta_p$ gives $A_{pj}=0$ for all $p$, and $j$ was arbitrary. $\square$

The coefficient $A_{pq}$ of $T$ along $E^{pq}$ is entry $(p,q)$ of the table of coordinates of the images $T\alpha_q$. This is the matrix of $T$ of [Chapter 9](ch09.md).

**The six maps $\mathbb{Q}^3\to\mathbb{Q}^2$** [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QIt8CporAVU&t=25s). With the standard bases of $\mathbb{Q}^3$ and $\mathbb{Q}^2$, the map $E^{pq}$ is $X\mapsto M_{pq}X$, where $M_{pq}$ is the $2\times3$ matrix with $1$ in position $(p,q)$ and $0$ elsewhere. For $T$ we get the coordinates $(1,1,0,0,1,1)$ along $E^{11},E^{12},E^{13},E^{21},E^{22},E^{23}$, which are the entries of $A$ read row by row. The basis depends on the chosen bases: with $\beta'=((1,0),(1,1))$ in $\mathbb{Q}^2$, the map $E^{21}$ built from $\beta'$ sends the first unit vector to $(1,1)$, not to $(0,1)$.

## 4. Composition [08:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QIt8CporAVU&t=535s)

For $T:V\to W$ and $U:W\to Z$, the *composition* $UT:V\to Z$ is $(UT)\alpha=U(T\alpha)$.

**Theorem 4.1.** If $T$ and $U$ are linear, then $UT$ is linear.

**Proof.** $UT(c\alpha+\beta)=U(cT\alpha+T\beta)=cU(T\alpha)+U(T\beta)=c(UT)\alpha+(UT)\beta$. $\square$

The order is forced by the domains. For $T:\mathbb{Q}^3\to\mathbb{Q}^2$ and $R:\mathbb{Q}^2\to\mathbb{Q}^2$ the composition $RT$ is defined, with matrix

$$
CA=\begin{pmatrix}1&3&2\\1&4&3\end{pmatrix},
$$

but $TR$ is not, since $R$ produces a vector in $\mathbb{Q}^2$ and $T$ needs one in $\mathbb{Q}^3$.

## 5. Linear operators form an algebra [15:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QIt8CporAVU&t=907s)

Let $\mathcal{L}(V)=\mathcal{L}(V,V)$ be the space of linear operators on $V$. If $\dim V=n$, then $\dim\mathcal{L}(V)=n^2$ by Theorem 3.1. In $\mathcal{L}(V)$ any two elements can be composed.

**Theorem 5.1.** For $U,T,T_1,T_2\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ and $c\in F$, with $I$ the identity operator:

1. $IU=UI=U$;
2. $U(T_1T_2)=(UT_1)T_2$;
3. $U(T_1+T_2)=UT_1+UT_2$ and $(T_1+T_2)U=T_1U+T_2U$;
4. $c(UT)=(cU)T=U(cT)$.

**Proof.** Each identity is checked on a vector $\alpha$. Part 1 and part 2 hold for any functions. For part 3, $U(T_1+T_2)\alpha=U(T_1\alpha+T_2\alpha)=UT_1\alpha+UT_2\alpha$ by linearity of $U$, whereas $(T_1+T_2)U\alpha=T_1U\alpha+T_2U\alpha$ is the definition of the sum. For part 4, both $c(UT)\alpha$ and $(cU)T\alpha$ equal $cU(T\alpha)$, and $U(cT\alpha)=cU(T\alpha)$ by linearity of $U$. $\square$

Note that only the first identity of part 3 uses linearity of the outer map. A vector space with a bilinear, associative multiplication satisfying these identities is called a *linear algebra* over $F$ (with identity, here). So $\mathcal{L}(V)$ is a linear algebra.

Composition differs from multiplication of numbers in two ways. It is not commutative: with $N$ the operator with matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$,

$$
CN=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad NC=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

And there are *zero divisors*: $N\neq0$ but $N^2=0$.

## 6. Invertible linear maps [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PpvRhe2QpjA&t=25s)

**Definition 6.1.** A function $T:V\to W$ is *invertible* if there is a function $S:W\to V$ with $ST=I_V$ and $TS=I_W$, where $I_V,I_W$ are the identity maps. Then $S$ is unique and written $T^{-1}$.

Uniqueness: if $S$ and $S'$ both work, $S=S(TS')=(ST)S'=S'$. A function is invertible exactly when it is *injective* (one-to-one) and *surjective* (onto). The two identities involve different identity maps, and both are needed.

**Theorem 6.2.** If $T:V\to W$ is linear and invertible, then $T^{-1}:W\to V$ is linear.

**Proof.** Take $\beta_1,\beta_2\in W$ and $c\in F$, and let $\alpha_i=T^{-1}\beta_i$. Then $T(c\alpha_1+\alpha_2)=c\beta_1+\beta_2$ by linearity. Apply $T^{-1}$ to both sides: $c\alpha_1+\alpha_2=T^{-1}(c\beta_1+\beta_2)$. So $T^{-1}(c\beta_1+\beta_2)=cT^{-1}\beta_1+T^{-1}\beta_2$. $\square$

**One-sided inverses.** The map $T$ of the running example is surjective but not injective: $\ker T$ is spanned by $(1,-1,1)$. Take

$$
G=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then $AG=I_2$, so $TG=I$ with $G$ viewed as the map $X\mapsto GX$. But $GA$ has rank $2$, so $GT\neq I_3$. Thus $T$ has a right inverse and no left inverse. In infinite dimension the same can happen for operators: on $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, differentiation $D$ and integration $J$ from $0$ satisfy $DJ=I$, while $JDp=p-p(0)$.

## 7. Nonsingular maps [10:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PpvRhe2QpjA&t=645s)

**Definition 7.1.** A linear $T:V\to W$ is *nonsingular* if $T\alpha=0$ implies $\alpha=0$, that is, $\ker T=\{0\}$.

Since $T\alpha_1=T\alpha_2$ means $T(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)=0$, nonsingular is the same as injective.

**Theorem 7.2.** A linear $T:V\to W$ is nonsingular if and only if it maps every linearly independent set to a linearly independent set, with distinct vectors having distinct images.

**Proof.** Let $T$ be nonsingular and $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k$ independent. Suppose $\sum c_iT\alpha_i=0$. By linearity $T\big(\sum c_i\alpha_i\big)=0$, so $\sum c_i\alpha_i=0$ by nonsingularity, so all $c_i=0$ by independence. Hence the images are independent, in particular distinct. For infinite independent sets, apply this to each finite subset. Conversely, if $\alpha\neq0$, then $\{\alpha\}$ is independent, so $\{T\alpha\}$ is independent, which means $T\alpha\neq0$. So $\ker T=\{0\}$. $\square$

For $T$ above, the independent set $\{(1,-1,1)\}$ is mapped to $\{0\}$, which is dependent.

**Theorem 7.3.** Let $\dim V=\dim W=n$ and $T:V\to W$ linear. The following are equivalent: (a) $T$ is invertible; (b) $T$ is nonsingular; (c) $T$ is surjective; (d) $T$ maps a basis of $V$ to a basis of $W$.

**Proof.** (a) implies (b) since invertible maps are injective. (b) implies (d): the images of a basis are $n$ independent vectors in the $n$-dimensional space $W$, hence a basis. (d) implies (c): the range contains a basis of $W$. (c) implies (a): by rank-nullity ([Chapter 7](ch07.md)), $\operatorname{nullity}T=n-\operatorname{rank}T=0$, so $T$ is injective and surjective. $\square$

**Invertibility from a polynomial.** For $R$, a direct computation gives $C^2=4C-I$. So $R^2-4R+I=0$, that is, $R(4I-R)=I=(4I-R)R$. Hence $R$ is invertible with $R^{-1}=4I-R$, whose matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Such a relation always exists in finite dimension, because the $n^2+1$ powers $I,R,\dots,R^{n^2}$ lie in the $n^2$-dimensional space $\mathcal{L}(V)$ and are therefore dependent. [Chapter 13](ch13.md) uses this fact.

Invertible operators do not form a subspace: $I$ and $-I$ are invertible, and their sum $0$ is not.

## 8. Isomorphisms [21:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PpvRhe2QpjA&t=1295s)

**Definition 8.1.** An *isomorphism* from $V$ onto $W$ is a linear, invertible map $T:V\to W$. If one exists, $V$ is *isomorphic* to $W$.

By Theorem 6.2, $T^{-1}$ is an isomorphism from $W$ onto $V$, so the relation is symmetric; it is reflexive (identity) and transitive (composition of isomorphisms is an isomorphism, with inverse $T^{-1}U^{-1}$).

**Theorem 8.2.** Every $n$-dimensional vector space over $F$ is isomorphic to $F^n$.

**Proof.** Fix an ordered basis $(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ and define $\psi:V\to F^n$ by $\psi(\alpha)=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, where $\alpha=\sum x_i\alpha_i$. Coordinates exist and are unique, so $\psi$ is well defined. If $\beta=\sum y_i\alpha_i$, then $c\alpha+\beta=\sum(cx_i+y_i)\alpha_i$, so $\psi(c\alpha+\beta)=c\psi(\alpha)+\psi(\beta)$. $\psi$ is injective since $\psi(\alpha)=0$ forces $\alpha=\sum0\cdot\alpha_i=0$, and surjective since $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is the image of $\sum x_i\alpha_i$. $\square$

**Corollary 8.3.** Finite-dimensional spaces over $F$ are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension.

**Proof.** If $\dim V=\dim W=n$, both are isomorphic to $F^n$, and the relation is symmetric and transitive. Conversely an isomorphism maps a basis to an independent set (Theorem 7.2) that spans, since it is surjective. So the dimensions agree. $\square$

An isomorphism preserves every statement formulated with sums and scalar multiples: independence, spanning, subspaces and dimension. So a question about an abstract $n$-dimensional space can be moved to $F^n$, where row reduction answers it. For example, the polynomials $1+x$, $x+x^2$, $1+x^2$ form a basis of the space of polynomials of degree at most $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$: their coordinate columns $(1,1,0)$, $(0,1,1)$, $(1,0,1)$ have determinant $2\neq0$. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ the three add to zero. The isomorphism depends on the basis; no basis is forced by the space itself. Also, the field matters: $\mathbb{C}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$ as a real space, but not to $\mathbb{C}^2$ as a complex space.

## Exercises

1. (Computation.) Find the inverse of the operator on $\mathbb{Q}^3$ given by $x\mapsto(x_1+x_2,\;x_2+x_3,\;x_3)$.
2. (Composition.) If $T:V\to W$ and $U:W\to Z$ are linear, prove that $\ker T\subseteq\ker UT$ and $\operatorname{im}UT\subseteq\operatorname{im}U$. When $UT$ is invertible and $V,W,Z$ have the same finite dimension, show that $T$ and $U$ are invertible.
3. (Counting.) How many linear maps $\mathbb{F}_p^2\to\mathbb{F}_p^3$ are there, and how many are nonsingular?
4. (Annihilating relation.) Let $S$ on $\mathbb{Q}^3$ have matrix with rows $(2,0,0),(0,2,0),(0,0,3)$. Find a relation $S^2=aS+bI$ and use it to find $S^{-1}$.
5. (Commutator.) Let $V=\mathbb{Q}[x]$, $D$ differentiation and $M$ multiplication by $x$. Show that $DM-MD=I$, and prove that no operators on a finite-dimensional rational space satisfy $ST-TS=I$.
6. (Isomorphism.) Exhibit an isomorphism between the space of $2\times2$ symmetric rational matrices and $\mathbb{Q}^3$, and give the image of $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&5\end{pmatrix}$.
