# Coordinates and change of ordered basis

Take the polynomial $p=2+3x+5x^2$ with rational coefficients. Its coefficients $(2,3,5)$ describe it relative to the monomials $1,x,x^2$. Suppose that we care about the behaviour of $p$ near $x=1$, for instance in a numerical method that expands about that point. Then the natural building blocks are $1$, $x-1$ and $(x-1)^2$, and

$$
p=10+13(x-1)+5(x-1)^2 .
$$

Check the constant term at $x=0$: $10-13+5=2$. The polynomial has not changed; only the list of numbers that records it has. This chapter finds the rule that converts one list into the other for any finite-dimensional vector space and any two bases. The rule is multiplication by an invertible matrix, and, conversely, every invertible matrix is such a rule. The polynomial $p$ and the two bases of this paragraph are the running example (an original construction).

Throughout, $F$ is a field and $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$ with $\dim V=n\ge1$. Matrices in $F^{n\times n}$ have entries in $F$, and $I$ is the identity matrix, with entries $\delta_{ij}$ ($1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise). A matrix $P$ is *invertible* if $PQ=QP=I$ for some matrix $Q$, written $P^{-1}$.

## 1. Ordered bases and coordinate matrices [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rA7m8jQKtQ4&t=25s)

An *ordered basis* $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ is a basis of $V$ listed in a definite order. By [Chapter 4](ch04.md), every $\alpha\in V$ has exactly one expression $\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^nx_i\alpha_i$ with $x_i\in F$. The column

$$
[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\ \vdots\\ x_n\end{pmatrix}\in F^n
$$

is the *coordinate matrix* of $\alpha$ relative to $\mathcal{B}$. The map $\alpha\mapsto[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}$ is a bijection from $V$ onto $F^n$, and it preserves linear combinations:

$$
[c\alpha+\beta]_{\mathcal{B}}=c[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}+[\beta]_{\mathcal{B}}\qquad(c\in F,\ \alpha,\beta\in V).
$$

Here $F^n$ is written as the space of columns. In particular $\alpha=\mathbf{0}$ exactly when $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=0$, and $[\alpha_j]_{\mathcal{B}}$ is the unit column $e_j$ with $1$ in position $j$ and $0$ elsewhere.

The order matters. Exchanging $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ gives the same basis as a set but exchanges the first two coordinates of every vector.

For the running example, with $\mathcal{B}=(1,x,x^2)$ we have $[p]_{\mathcal{B}}=(2,3,5)^{\mathsf T}$, where $\mathsf T$ denotes transpose, so that a row of numbers followed by $\mathsf T$ means the column with those entries. With $\mathcal{B}'=(1,x-1,(x-1)^2)$ we have $[p]_{\mathcal{B}'}=(10,13,5)^{\mathsf T}$.

## 2. The change-of-basis theorem [04:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rA7m8jQKtQ4&t=275s)

Let $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ and $\mathcal{B}'=(\alpha_1',\dots,\alpha_n')$ be two ordered bases of $V$. Each new basis vector has coordinates in the old basis. So there are scalars $P_{ij}$ with

$$
\alpha_j'=\sum_{i=1}^nP_{ij}\alpha_i\qquad(j=1,\dots,n).\tag{2.1}
$$

The matrix $P=(P_{ij})$ has as its $j$th column the coordinate matrix $[\alpha_j']_{\mathcal{B}}$. Note the position of the indices: the sum runs over the *first* index of $P_{ij}$. We write $P=P_{\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}'}$ and read it as "from $\mathcal{B}'$-coordinates to $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates".

**Theorem 2.1.** For every $\alpha\in V$,

$$
[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=P\,[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'} .
$$

Moreover $P$ is invertible, and $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}=P^{-1}[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}$.

**Proof.** Let $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}=(x_1',\dots,x_n')^{\mathsf T}$. Substitute (2.1) into the expansion of $\alpha$ and exchange the order of the two finite sums:

$$
\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^nx_j'\alpha_j'=\sum_{j=1}^nx_j'\sum_{i=1}^nP_{ij}\alpha_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(\sum_{j=1}^nP_{ij}x_j'\Big)\alpha_i .
$$

The coefficients of the $\alpha_i$ are unique, so the $i$th coordinate of $\alpha$ in $\mathcal{B}$ is $\sum_jP_{ij}x_j'$, which is entry $i$ of $P[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}$. This proves the formula.

For invertibility, suppose $PX'=0$ and let $\alpha=\sum_jx_j'\alpha_j'$, so $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}=X'$. By the formula $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=PX'=0$, so $\alpha=\mathbf{0}$, and then $X'=[\mathbf{0}]_{\mathcal{B}'}=0$. So $PX=0$ has only the zero solution. By [Chapter 2](ch02.md), a square matrix with this property is row-equivalent to $I$, and so it is a product of elementary matrices, hence invertible. Multiplying the formula on the left by $P^{-1}$ gives the last claim. $\square$

The hinge is that invertibility needs no computation with $P$. It follows because both coordinate maps are bijections: a column killed by $P$ describes a vector that is zero in one basis, hence zero, hence zero in the other.

**Uniqueness of $P$.** Only one matrix satisfies $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=P[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}$ for all $\alpha$: taking $\alpha=\alpha_j'$ gives $[\alpha_j']_{\mathcal{B}}=Pe_j$, the $j$th column of $P$.

The direction of the formula is the most common source of error. The columns of $P$ express *new* vectors in the *old* basis, but $P$ converts *new* coordinates into *old* ones. To go from old to new, one needs $P^{-1}$.

## 3. The running example worked out

The new basis is $\mathcal{B}'=(1,x-1,(x-1)^2)$ and the old one is $\mathcal{B}=(1,x,x^2)$. The columns of $P$ are the old coordinates of $1$, of $x-1=-1+x$, and of $(x-1)^2=1-2x+x^2$:

$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&1\\0&1&-2\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&2\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

One can check $P^{-1}$ column by column: it expresses $1$, $x=1+(x-1)$ and $x^2=1+2(x-1)+(x-1)^2$ in the new basis. Therefore

$$
[p]_{\mathcal{B}'}=P^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}10\\13\\5\end{pmatrix},
$$

which is the expansion in the opening paragraph. Using $P$ instead of $P^{-1}$ gives $P(2,3,5)^{\mathsf T}=(4,-7,5)^{\mathsf T}$, which is wrong: it would claim $p=4-7(x-1)+5(x-1)^2$, whose value at $x=1$ is $4$, while $p(1)=10$.

The new coordinates have a meaning: $10=p(1)$, $13=p'(1)$ and $5=p''(1)/2$, where $p'$ and $p''$ are the first and second derivatives. This is Taylor's formula for a quadratic polynomial.

**A functional in new coordinates.** Let $f(q)=q(1)$ be the value at $1$. In old coordinates $f(q)=aX$ with the row $a=(1,1,1)$. Since $X=PX'$, we get $f(q)=(aP)X'$. Here $aP=(1,0,0)$: in the basis centred at $1$, evaluation at $1$ reads off the first coordinate. Rows of coefficients transform by $P$ on the right, while columns of coordinates transform by $P^{-1}$ on the left. [Chapter 10](ch10.md) makes this precise.

## 4. Composing changes of basis

Let $\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}',\mathcal{B}''$ be ordered bases, $P=P_{\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}'}$ and $Q=P_{\mathcal{B}'\leftarrow\mathcal{B}''}$. Then for all $\alpha$,

$$
[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=P[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}=PQ[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}''}.
$$

By uniqueness, $P_{\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}''}=PQ$. In particular, taking $\mathcal{B}''=\mathcal{B}$ shows that $P_{\mathcal{B}'\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}=P^{-1}$, in agreement with Theorem 2.1. With $\mathcal{B}''=(1,x-2,(x-2)^2)$, the change from $\mathcal{B}'$ to $\mathcal{B}''$ is another shift by one, so $Q=P$, and the change from $\mathcal{B}$ to $\mathcal{B}''$ is

$$
P^2=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&4\\0&1&-4\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},
$$

whose columns are the coefficients of $1$, $x-2$ and $(x-2)^2=4-4x+x^2$.

## 5. Example in $F^3$ [16:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rA7m8jQKtQ4&t=1017s)

Let $V=F^3$, let $\mathcal{E}=(e_1,e_2,e_3)$ be the standard basis, and let $\mathcal{B}'=\big((1,1,1),(1,1,0),(1,0,0)\big)$. Then $[(x,y,z)]_{\mathcal{E}}=(x,y,z)^{\mathsf T}$. The columns of $P=P_{\mathcal{E}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}'}$ are the standard coordinates of the new vectors, that is, the vectors themselves:

$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\1&1&0\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Solving $c_1(1,1,1)+c_2(1,1,0)+c_3(1,0,0)=(x,y,z)$ from the last coordinate upward gives $c_1=z$, $c_2=y-z$, $c_3=x-y$. So $[(x,y,z)]_{\mathcal{B}'}=(z,\,y-z,\,x-y)^{\mathsf T}$. Check the theorem:

$$
P\begin{pmatrix}z\\y-z\\x-y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}z+(y-z)+(x-y)\\ z+(y-z)\\ z\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}.
$$

When neither basis is standard, write $M$ for the matrix whose columns are the standard coordinates of the vectors of $\mathcal{B}_1$, and $M'$ for that of $\mathcal{B}_2$. Then $P_{\mathcal{E}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}_1}=M$ and $P_{\mathcal{E}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}_2}=M'$, so by Section 4,

$$
P_{\mathcal{B}_1\leftarrow\mathcal{B}_2}=M^{-1}M'.
$$

In practice one does not invert $M$: row reducing the augmented matrix $[M\mid M']$ to $[I\mid P]$ performs left multiplication by $M^{-1}$ on both parts. For $\mathcal{B}_1=\big((1,1,0),(0,1,1),(1,0,1)\big)$ and $\mathcal{B}_2=\big((1,1,1),(0,1,1),(0,0,1)\big)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ the result is

$$
P_{\mathcal{B}_1\leftarrow\mathcal{B}_2}=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&0&-1/2\\1/2&1&1/2\\1/2&0&1/2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $v=(4,-2,6)$ one finds $[v]_{\mathcal{B}_2}=(4,-6,8)^{\mathsf T}$ and $[v]_{\mathcal{B}_1}=(-2,0,6)^{\mathsf T}$, and the first row of $P$ gives $\tfrac12\cdot4-\tfrac12\cdot8=-2$. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ the list $\mathcal{B}_1$ is not a basis, since its three vectors add to zero; the entries $1/2$ signal that the computation needs $2\neq0$.

## 6. Every invertible matrix is a change of basis [25:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rA7m8jQKtQ4&t=1521s)

Theorem 2.1 produces an invertible matrix from two bases. The converse produces a second basis from one basis and an invertible matrix.

**Theorem 6.1.** Let $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ be an ordered basis of $V$ and $P\in F^{n\times n}$ invertible. There is exactly one ordered basis $\mathcal{B}'$ of $V$ with

$$
[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=P[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}\quad\text{for all }\alpha\in V.
$$

It is given by $\alpha_j'=\sum_iP_{ij}\alpha_i$.

**Proof.** *Uniqueness.* If $\mathcal{B}'$ satisfies the formula, then $[\alpha_j']_{\mathcal{B}}=Pe_j$, so $\alpha_j'=\sum_iP_{ij}\alpha_i$.

*Existence.* Define $\alpha_j'$ by this formula. Let $Q=P^{-1}$. For each $k$,

$$
\sum_{j=1}^nQ_{jk}\alpha_j'=\sum_{j=1}^nQ_{jk}\sum_{i=1}^nP_{ij}\alpha_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(\sum_{j=1}^nP_{ij}Q_{jk}\Big)\alpha_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{ik}\alpha_i=\alpha_k.
$$

So every $\alpha_k$ lies in the span of $\alpha_1',\dots,\alpha_n'$, and these $n$ vectors span $V$. A spanning list of $n=\dim V$ vectors is a basis ([Chapter 4](ch04.md)). Now Theorem 2.1 applies to $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{B}'$, and the matrix it produces has $j$th column $[\alpha_j']_{\mathcal{B}}=Pe_j$. So it is $P$. $\square$

The hinge is the identity $PQ=I$, read as a statement about vectors: the entries of $Q$ are the weights that rebuild each old vector from the new ones.

**Example.** For the monomial basis and the invertible matrix

$$
P_c=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&1\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad P_c^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&2\\1&1&-1\\-1&2&1\end{pmatrix},
$$

the new basis is $(1+x^2,\;2+x,\;x+x^2)$, read from the columns, and $[p]_{\text{new}}=P_c^{-1}(2,3,5)^{\mathsf T}=(2,0,3)^{\mathsf T}$. Indeed $2(1+x^2)+0\cdot(2+x)+3(x+x^2)=2+3x+5x^2$.

**Boundary.** If $P$ is not invertible, the formula $\alpha_j'=\sum_iP_{ij}\alpha_i$ still defines vectors, but they are not a basis. For $P$ with both columns $(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ we get $\alpha_1'=\alpha_2'=\alpha_1+\alpha_2$.

**Counting.** Theorems 2.1 and 6.1 say that, once one ordered basis is fixed, $\mathcal{B}'\mapsto P_{\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}'}$ is a bijection from the set of ordered bases of $V$ onto the set of invertible $n\times n$ matrices. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ with $n=2$ there are $6$ of each, and with $n=3$ there are $168$.

## 7. What is invariant under a change of basis

A vector does not depend on a basis; its coordinates do. A statement about vectors must therefore give the same truth value in every basis. For example, a list $v_1,\dots,v_k$ is linearly independent if and only if the columns $[v_i]_{\mathcal{B}}$ are, for one and hence for every ordered basis $\mathcal{B}$, because the coordinate map is a linear bijection and the columns in two bases differ by multiplication by an invertible $P$. By contrast, statements such as "the first coordinate is zero" depend on the basis: $(x,y,z)$ has first standard coordinate $x$ and first $\mathcal{B}'$-coordinate $z$.

## Exercises

1. (Computation.) In $P_2(\mathbb{Q})$ find the change-of-basis matrix from $\mathcal{C}=(1,x+1,(x+1)^2)$ to $\mathcal{B}=(1,x,x^2)$ and use it to expand $p=2+3x+5x^2$ in powers of $x+1$.
2. (Rotation.) In $\mathbb{R}^2$ let $\mathcal{B}'=\big((\cos\theta,\sin\theta),(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\big)$. Find $P_{\mathcal{E}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}'}$ and the $\mathcal{B}'$-coordinates of $(x,y)$.
3. (Direction.) Let $\mathcal{B}$ be an ordered basis of $V$ and $\mathcal{B}'$ obtained from it by replacing $\alpha_1$ with $2\alpha_1$, where $2\neq0$ in $F$. How do the coordinates of a vector change? Explain the result in words.
4. (Proof.) Prove that the ordered bases $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{B}'$ give $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}$ for every $\alpha$ if and only if $\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}'$.
5. (Field dependence.) For which primes $p$ is $\big((1,1,0),(0,1,1),(1,0,1)\big)$ a basis of $\mathbb{F}_p^3$? For $p=3$, find the coordinates of $(1,0,0)$.
6. (Counting.) Let $p$ be a prime. Count the invertible $2\times2$ matrices over $\mathbb{F}_p$ with the help of Theorem 6.1.
