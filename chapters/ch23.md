# Linear functionals and adjoint operators on inner product spaces

Let $P_2$ be the real polynomials of degree at most $2$ with $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1fg$. Reading the value $f(0)$ is a linear operation. Can it also be done by integrating $f$ against one fixed polynomial $g$? It can: for every $f\in P_2$,

$$f(0)=\int_0^1f(x)\,(30x^2-36x+9)\,dx .$$

The same question for the derivative operator $D$ asks for an operator $D^*$ with $\langle Df,g\rangle=\langle f,D^*g\rangle$. This chapter proves that on a finite-dimensional inner product space every linear functional is an inner product with a fixed vector, deduces that every operator has such a partner, and shows that both statements can fail in infinite dimension. The space $P_2$ is the running example.

**Conventions.** $V$ is an inner product space over $F=\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, with $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ linear in the first argument and conjugate-linear in the second ([Chapter 21](ch21.md)). A *linear functional* is a linear map $f:V\to F$; an *operator* is a linear map $T:V\to V$. An *orthonormal basis* is a basis of pairwise orthogonal vectors of norm $1$; every finite-dimensional inner product space has one. $S^{\perp}$ is the set of vectors orthogonal to all of $S$, and $A^*=\overline{A}^{\mathsf T}$ is the conjugate transpose of a matrix.

## 1. Representing linear functionals [02:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DBoDKvOMl3o&t=159s)

For fixed $\beta\in V$, the rule $f_\beta(\alpha)=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$ is a linear functional, by linearity in the first argument.

**Theorem 1.1** (representation theorem). Let $V$ be finite-dimensional and $f$ a linear functional on $V$. There is exactly one $\beta\in V$ with $f(\alpha)=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$ for all $\alpha$. For every orthonormal basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$,

$$\beta=\sum_{j=1}^n\overline{f(e_j)}\,e_j .$$

*Proof.* Define $\beta$ by the formula. Then $\langle e_k,\beta\rangle=\sum_jf(e_j)\langle e_k,e_j\rangle=f(e_k)$. So $f_\beta$ and $f$ agree on a basis, hence everywhere. If $\gamma$ also represents $f$, then $\langle\alpha,\beta-\gamma\rangle=0$ for all $\alpha$; taking $\alpha=\beta-\gamma$ gives $\beta=\gamma$. $\square$

**Where the formula comes from** [09:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DBoDKvOMl3o&t=548s). If some $\beta=\sum y_je_j$ works, then $f(e_k)=\langle e_k,\beta\rangle=\overline{y_k}$. So $y_k=\overline{f(e_k)}$ is forced. The conjugate appears because $\beta$ sits in the conjugate-linear slot. For the same reason the assignment $\beta\mapsto f_\beta$ is a bijection from $V$ onto the dual space $V^*$ (the space of linear functionals) that is *conjugate-linear*: $f_{c\beta+\gamma}=\bar c\,f_\beta+f_\gamma$.

## 2. The geometry of the representing vector [18:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DBoDKvOMl3o&t=1100s)

Let $V$ be finite-dimensional, let $f\ne0$ and put $N=\ker f$. Then $V=N\oplus N^{\perp}$ ([Chapter 22](ch22.md)), and $\dim N^{\perp}=\dim V-\dim N=1$. Take any nonzero $\gamma\in N^{\perp}$. The orthogonal projection of $\alpha$ on $N^{\perp}$ is $P\alpha=\frac{\langle\alpha,\gamma\rangle}{\|\gamma\|^2}\gamma$, and $\alpha-P\alpha\in N$. Hence

$$f(\alpha)=f(P\alpha)=\frac{\langle\alpha,\gamma\rangle}{\|\gamma\|^2}f(\gamma)=\Big\langle\alpha,\ \frac{\overline{f(\gamma)}}{\|\gamma\|^2}\,\gamma\Big\rangle .$$

So $\beta=\overline{f(\gamma)}\,\gamma/\|\gamma\|^2$. The representing vector is perpendicular to the kernel; its direction is fixed by the kernel and its length and phase by the values of $f$.

## 3. The running example: evaluation at a point [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OR00X0UhdmE&t=25s)

Gram–Schmidt on $(1,x,x^2)$ [06:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OR00X0UhdmE&t=375s) gives $1,\ x-\tfrac12,\ x^2-x+\tfrac16$ with squared norms $1,\tfrac1{12},\tfrac1{180}$. So an orthonormal basis is

$$e_1=1,\qquad e_2=\sqrt{12}\,\big(x-\tfrac12\big),\qquad e_3=\sqrt{180}\,\big(x^2-x+\tfrac16\big).$$

For $\varphi(f)=f(t_0)$ Theorem 1.1 gives $g=\sum_je_j(t_0)\,e_j$, that is,

$$g(x)=1+12\,(t_0-\tfrac12)(x-\tfrac12)+180\,(t_0^2-t_0+\tfrac16)(x^2-x+\tfrac16).$$

At $t_0=0$ this is $g(x)=9-36x+30x^2$, the polynomial of the opening. As a check, $\int_0^1g=1$, $\int_0^1xg=0$ and $\int_0^1x^2g=0$, which are the values of $1,x,x^2$ at $0$.

## 4. No representing vector in infinite dimension [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lN_tuUFxovo&t=25s)

Let $V=\mathbb{C}[x]$, all complex polynomials, with $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1f\bar g$. Fix $z\in\mathbb{C}$ and let $L(f)=f(z)$, a nonzero linear functional.

**Proposition 4.1.** No $g\in V$ satisfies $L(f)=\langle f,g\rangle$ for all $f$.

*Proof.* Suppose $g$ does. Let $h=x-z$ and let $\bar h=x-\bar z$, the polynomial with conjugated coefficients. Apply $L$ to $f=h\,\bar h\,g$, which vanishes at $z$:

$$0=\langle h\bar hg,\,g\rangle=\int_0^1|h(x)|^2\,|g(x)|^2\,dx .$$

The integrand is continuous and nonnegative, so it vanishes on $[0,1]$. Since $h$ has at most one zero there, $g$ vanishes at infinitely many points and is the zero polynomial. Then $L=0$, a contradiction. $\square$

The theorem fails here although every individual space $P_n$ has a representing polynomial $g_n$: the $g_n$ do not stabilize at any single polynomial.

## 5. Existence of the adjoint [09:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lN_tuUFxovo&t=582s)

**Definition 5.1** [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ycvgCq8Dx0Q&t=25s). An operator $T$ on $V$ *has an adjoint* if there is an operator $T^*$ on $V$ with

$$\langle T\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,T^*\beta\rangle\qquad\text{for all }\alpha,\beta\in V.$$

There is at most one such operator, in any dimension: if $\langle\alpha,U\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,U'\beta\rangle$ for all $\alpha$, then $U\beta=U'\beta$.

**Theorem 5.2.** On a finite-dimensional inner product space every operator has an adjoint.

*Proof.* Fix $\beta$. The rule $\varphi(\alpha)=\langle T\alpha,\beta\rangle$ is a linear functional. By Theorem 1.1 there is a unique $\beta'$ with $\varphi(\alpha)=\langle\alpha,\beta'\rangle$; define $T^*\beta=\beta'$. It remains to prove linearity. For all $\alpha$, $\beta,\gamma$ and $c\in F$,

$$\langle\alpha,T^*(c\beta+\gamma)\rangle=\langle T\alpha,c\beta+\gamma\rangle=\bar c\,\langle T\alpha,\beta\rangle+\langle T\alpha,\gamma\rangle=\langle\alpha,cT^*\beta+T^*\gamma\rangle .$$

Hence $T^*(c\beta+\gamma)=cT^*\beta+T^*\gamma$. $\square$

The hinge is that the two conjugations cancel: $c$ leaves the second slot as $\bar c$ and re-enters it as $c$.

## 6. Matrices of the adjoint [19:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lN_tuUFxovo&t=1163s)

**Theorem 6.1.** Let $\mathcal{B}=(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ be an orthonormal basis and $A=[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$. Then $A_{kj}=\langle Te_j,e_k\rangle$, and $[T^*]_{\mathcal{B}}=A^*$.

*Proof.* By definition of the matrix, $Te_j=\sum_kA_{kj}e_k$; taking the inner product with $e_k$ gives the first formula. Let $B=[T^*]_{\mathcal{B}}$. Then

$$B_{kj}=\langle T^*e_j,e_k\rangle=\overline{\langle e_k,T^*e_j\rangle}=\overline{\langle Te_k,e_j\rangle}=\overline{A_{jk}} .\qquad\square$$

**A general basis.** The word *orthonormal* cannot be dropped. Let $\mathcal{B}$ be any basis, $G$ its Gram matrix, so that $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=Y^*GX$ in coordinates, and $A=[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$, $B=[T^*]_{\mathcal{B}}$. The defining identity reads $Y^*GAX=(BY)^*GX$ for all $X,Y$, so $GA=B^*G$. Since $G$ is Hermitian and invertible,

$$[T^*]_{\mathcal{B}}=G^{-1}A^*G .$$

**The derivative on $P_2$.** In the orthonormal basis, $De_1=0$, $De_2=2\sqrt3\,e_1$ and $De_3=2\sqrt{15}\,e_2$. So $D^*$ has the transposed matrix: $D^*e_1=2\sqrt3\,e_2$, $D^*e_2=2\sqrt{15}\,e_3$, $D^*e_3=0$. In particular $D^*1=12x-6$. In the basis $(1,x,x^2)$ the matrices are

$$[D]=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&2\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad[D^*]=\begin{pmatrix}-6&2&3\\12&-24&-26\\0&30&30\end{pmatrix}.$$

The second is far from the transpose of the first, because this basis is not orthonormal.

**Transpose and adjoint.** The transpose $T^{\mathsf t}$ of [Chapter 11](ch11.md) acts on functionals by $T^{\mathsf t}f=f\circ T$. The defining identity of the adjoint says $f_\beta\circ T=f_{T^*\beta}$, that is, $T^{\mathsf t}f_\beta=f_{T^*\beta}$. So $T^*$ is the transpose carried back to $V$ through the conjugate-linear bijection $\beta\mapsto f_\beta$.

## 7. Examples

**Column vectors** [03:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ycvgCq8Dx0Q&t=232s). On $\mathbb{C}^n$ with $\langle X,Y\rangle=Y^*X$ and $TX=AX$: $\langle AX,Y\rangle=Y^*AX=(A^*Y)^*X$, so $T^*Y=A^*Y$.

**The adjoint depends on the inner product.** On $\mathbb{C}^2$ let $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. For the standard inner product $T^*$ has the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. For $\langle X,Y\rangle=Y^*\operatorname{diag}(1,2)X$ the formula $G^{-1}A^*G$ gives $\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1/2&0\end{pmatrix}$.

**Left multiplication** [07:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ycvgCq8Dx0Q&t=458s). On $\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ with $\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(B^*A)$ let $T(A)=MA$. Then $\langle MA,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(B^*MA)=\operatorname{tr}\big((M^*B)^*A\big)=\langle A,M^*B\rangle$. So $T^*(B)=M^*B$.

**Orthogonal projections** [16:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OR00X0UhdmE&t=1006s). Let $E$ be the orthogonal projection on a finite-dimensional subspace $W$ of any inner product space. Since $E\alpha\perp\beta-E\beta$ and $\alpha-E\alpha\perp E\beta$,

$$\langle E\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle E\alpha,E\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,E\beta\rangle .$$

So $E^*=E$. For $W$ spanned by $w=(3,12,-1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ [22:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OR00X0UhdmE&t=1362s), $E\alpha=\frac{\langle\alpha,w\rangle}{154}w$, with standard matrix

$$\frac1{154}\begin{pmatrix}9&36&-3\\36&144&-12\\-3&-12&1\end{pmatrix},$$

which is symmetric, as Theorem 6.1 requires.

## 8. Infinite dimension: an adjoint may or may not exist [14:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ycvgCq8Dx0Q&t=845s)

Let $V=\mathbb{C}[x]$ with the integral inner product.

**Multiplication** has an adjoint. For a fixed polynomial $p$ and $Tg=pg$, $\langle pg,h\rangle=\int_0^1g\,\overline{\bar ph}=\langle g,\bar ph\rangle$. So $T^*h=\bar ph$.

**Differentiation has none** [21:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ycvgCq8Dx0Q&t=1262s). Integration by parts gives

$$\langle Df,g\rangle=f(1)\overline{g(1)}-f(0)\overline{g(0)}-\langle f,Dg\rangle .$$

So a vector $u$ with $\langle Df,g\rangle=\langle f,u\rangle$ for all $f$ exists exactly when the boundary functional $\varphi(f)=f(1)\overline{g(1)}-f(0)\overline{g(0)}$ is representable. Suppose $\varphi(f)=\langle f,h\rangle$. Every $f=x(x-1)h$ has $\varphi(f)=0$, so $\int_0^1x(x-1)|h|^2=0$. The integrand has one sign, so $h=0$. Hence $\varphi=0$, and the choices $f=x$ and $f=1-x$ give $g(1)=g(0)=0$. Therefore $D^*g$ can be defined only for $g$ vanishing at both ends (where it is $-Dg$), and $D$ has no adjoint on $V$. On $P_2$, in contrast, $D^*$ exists by Theorem 5.2; the adjoint depends on the space as well as on the inner product.

## 9. Algebra of adjoints [26:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ycvgCq8Dx0Q&t=1570s)

**Theorem 9.1.** Let $V$ be finite-dimensional, $T,U$ operators and $c\in F$. Then

$$(T+U)^*=T^*+U^*,\quad(cT)^*=\bar c\,T^*,\quad(TU)^*=U^*T^*,\quad(T^*)^*=T .$$

*Proof.* By uniqueness it suffices to check the defining identity. First, $\langle(T+U)\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,T^*\beta\rangle+\langle\alpha,U^*\beta\rangle$. Second, $\langle cT\alpha,\beta\rangle=c\langle\alpha,T^*\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,\bar cT^*\beta\rangle$. Third, $\langle TU\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle U\alpha,T^*\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,U^*T^*\beta\rangle$. Fourth, $\langle T^*\alpha,\beta\rangle=\overline{\langle\beta,T^*\alpha\rangle}=\overline{\langle T\beta,\alpha\rangle}=\langle\alpha,T\beta\rangle$. $\square$

**Proposition 9.2.** If $T$ has an adjoint (for instance if $V$ is finite-dimensional), then $\ker T^*=(\operatorname{im}T)^{\perp}$.

*Proof.* $T^*\beta=0$ if and only if $\langle\alpha,T^*\beta\rangle=0$ for all $\alpha$, that is, $\langle T\alpha,\beta\rangle=0$ for all $\alpha$. $\square$

On $P_2$: $\operatorname{im}D=P_1$, and $\ker D^*$ is spanned by $e_3$, which is orthogonal to $P_1$.

## 10. Self-adjoint operators and the Cartesian decomposition [30:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ycvgCq8Dx0Q&t=1830s)

**Definition 10.1.** An operator is *self-adjoint* (also *Hermitian*) if $T^*=T$.

In an orthonormal basis this says $A^*=A$. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional complex inner product space. Every operator splits as

$$T=U_1+iU_2,\qquad U_1=\frac{T+T^*}{2},\quad U_2=\frac{T-T^*}{2i},$$

with $U_1,U_2$ self-adjoint by Theorem 9.1. The splitting is unique: if $T=U_1+iU_2$ with self-adjoint parts, then $T^*=U_1-iU_2$, and the two equations determine $U_1,U_2$. The operators $U_1,U_2$ play the roles of the real and imaginary parts of a complex number, and $T$ itself need not be self-adjoint. Over $\mathbb{R}$ there is no $i$; the analogous splitting is $T=\tfrac12(T+T^*)+\tfrac12(T-T^*)$, a self-adjoint part plus a *skew-adjoint* part ($S^*=-S$). Self-adjoint operators are the ones that [Chapter 25](ch25.md) diagonalizes by orthonormal bases.

## Exercises

1. (Computation.) Find $g\in P_2$ with $f'(0)=\langle f,g\rangle$ for all $f\in P_2$.
2. (Range and kernel.) On a finite-dimensional space show that $\operatorname{im}T^*=(\ker T)^{\perp}$ and that $T$ and $T^*$ have the same rank.
3. (Invariant subspaces.) Let $V$ be finite-dimensional and $TW\subseteq W$. Show that $T^*W^{\perp}\subseteq W^{\perp}$.
4. (Trace inner product.) On $\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ with $\operatorname{tr}(B^*A)$ find the adjoint of $T(A)=AM$.
5. (Products.) For self-adjoint $T,U$ show that $TU$ is self-adjoint exactly when $TU=UT$, and give self-adjoint $2\times2$ matrices whose product is not.
6. (Complex versus real.) Show that on a finite-dimensional complex space $T$ is self-adjoint if and only if $\langle T\alpha,\alpha\rangle$ is real for all $\alpha$, and that this fails over $\mathbb{R}$.
