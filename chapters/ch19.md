# The primary decomposition theorem

Let $T$ be the operator on $\mathbb{Q}^5$ whose standard matrix is block diagonal,

$$M=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\0&C\end{pmatrix},\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\1&2&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad C=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

This is the running example of the chapter. The block $A$ is the example worked in the first recording used here; the block $C$, a quarter turn, is added so that the example also has a factor with no root in $\mathbb{Q}$. The operator has two defects that stop diagonalization. The eigenvalue $2$ is repeated but has only one independent eigenvector. And on the last two coordinates there is no eigenvalue at all over $\mathbb{Q}$. Even so, the space splits into three invariant pieces on which $T$ behaves in three different ways. The *primary decomposition theorem* says that every operator on a finite-dimensional space splits like this, with one piece for each irreducible factor of its minimal polynomial.

**Conventions.** $F$ is a field, $V$ is a vector space over $F$, and $T$ is a linear operator on $V$. $F[x]$ is the ring of polynomials over $F$, and $p(T)$ is $p$ evaluated at $T$. All polynomials in $T$ commute. For finite-dimensional $V$, the *minimal polynomial* $m_T$ is the monic polynomial of least degree with $m_T(T)=0$; every $p$ with $p(T)=0$ is a multiple of it ([Chapter 13](ch13.md)). A polynomial of positive degree is *irreducible* over $F$ if it is not a product of two polynomials of positive degree. Every monic polynomial of positive degree is a product of monic irreducible polynomials, uniquely up to order. Polynomials are *relatively prime* if no polynomial of positive degree divides all of them. A subspace $W$ is *$T$-invariant* if $T(W)\subseteq W$, and then $T_W$ denotes the restriction.

## 1. The question [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eyIs2irRboE&t=25s)

When $V=W_1\oplus\cdots\oplus W_k$ with each $W_i$ invariant, $T$ is the direct sum of its restrictions $T_i=T_{W_i}$ ([Chapter 18](ch18.md)). The question now runs the other way. Given only $T$, is there such a decomposition, and can the pieces be described?

Eigenspaces give an answer when $T$ is diagonalizable. Two things can go wrong. First, the field may be too small: $T$ may have no eigenvalue in $F$. One can enlarge the field to an *algebraically closed* field, one in which every nonconstant polynomial has a root, such as $\mathbb{C}$. Second, and this no field change repairs, there may be too few eigenvectors.

## 2. Kernels of powers [06:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eyIs2irRboE&t=374s)

The block $A$ shows the second defect and the repair. Its characteristic polynomial is $(x-2)^2(x+1)$, because $A$ is triangular. Solving $(A+I)X=0$ gives the eigenvector $e_3$ for $-1$, where $e_1,e_2,e_3$ are the standard basis vectors. Solving $(A-2I)X=0$ gives only multiples of $e_2$. So there are two independent eigenvectors in a three-dimensional space. Now square:

$$A-2I=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&-3\end{pmatrix},\qquad (A-2I)^2=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&9\end{pmatrix}.$$

The null space of $(A-2I)^2$ is $\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)$. Together with $\ker(A+I)=\operatorname{span}(e_3)$ it fills the space:

$$F^3=\ker(A-2I)^2\oplus\ker(A+I).$$

Raising the factor to the power with which it occurs in the minimal polynomial recovers the missing direction.

## 3. The theorem [14:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eyIs2irRboE&t=872s)

**Theorem 3.1 (primary decomposition).** Let $V$ be finite-dimensional over $F$, let $T$ be a linear operator on $V$, and factor its minimal polynomial as

$$m_T=p_1^{r_1}p_2^{r_2}\cdots p_k^{r_k},$$

with $p_i$ distinct monic irreducible polynomials over $F$ and $r_i\ge1$. Let $W_i=\ker p_i(T)^{r_i}$. Then

1. $V=W_1\oplus\cdots\oplus W_k$;
2. each $W_i$ is nonzero and $T$-invariant;
3. the minimal polynomial of $T_i=T_{W_i}$ is $p_i^{r_i}$.

No hypothesis is placed on $F$. The polynomials $p_i$ need not be linear. The subspaces $W_i$ are the *primary components* of $T$.

The proof has one idea: write the identity operator as a sum of polynomials in $T$, each of which survives on one component and vanishes on the others. Sections 4 to 6 carry it out.

## 4. A Bezout identity produces projections [02:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qra533N0-nw&t=174s)

Write $m=m_T$ and put $f_i=m/p_i^{r_i}=\prod_{j\ne i}p_j^{r_j}$, the part of $m$ that remains after removing the $i$th factor.

**Lemma 4.1.** There are $g_1,\dots,g_k\in F[x]$ with $g_1f_1+\cdots+g_kf_k=1$.

*Proof.* The set $J$ of all sums $\sum a_if_i$ with $a_i\in F[x]$ is an ideal of $F[x]$. Every nonzero ideal of $F[x]$ consists of the multiples of one monic polynomial $d$ ([Chapter 15](ch15.md)). Since each $f_i$ lies in $J$, $d$ divides every $f_i$. If $d$ had positive degree, some irreducible $q$ would divide $d$, hence every $f_i$, hence $m$. So $q=p_l$ for some $l$. But $p_l$ does not divide $f_l$, which is a product of powers of the other irreducibles. So $d=1$, and $1\in J$. $\square$

The polynomials $f_i$ have no common factor as a family, even though any two of them may share factors when $k\ge3$. The identity is called a *Bezout identity*.

**The projections.** Let $h_i=g_if_i$ and $E_i=h_i(T)$.

**Proposition 4.2.** $E_1+\cdots+E_k=I$, $E_iE_j=0$ for $i\ne j$, $E_i^2=E_i$, and each $E_i$ commutes with $T$.

*Proof.* The first equation is Lemma 4.1 evaluated at $T$. For $i\ne j$, the product $f_if_j$ contains every factor $p_l^{r_l}$, since $f_i$ lacks only the $i$th and $f_j$ contains it. So $m$ divides $h_ih_j$, and $E_iE_j=(h_ih_j)(T)=0$. Multiplying $\sum_iE_i=I$ by $E_j$ now gives $E_j^2=E_j$. Finally $E_i$ is a polynomial in $T$. $\square$

## 5. The ranges are the primary components [09:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qra533N0-nw&t=590s)

**Proposition 5.1.** $\operatorname{im}E_j=W_j$ for every $j$.

*Proof.* Let $\alpha\in\operatorname{im}E_j$, so $\alpha=E_j\alpha$. Then $p_j(T)^{r_j}\alpha=(p_j^{r_j}g_jf_j)(T)\alpha=(g_jm)(T)\alpha=0$, because $p_j^{r_j}f_j=m$. So $\alpha\in W_j$.

Conversely, let $\alpha\in W_j$. For $i\ne j$ the polynomial $f_i$ contains the factor $p_j^{r_j}$, so $E_i\alpha=(g_if_i)(T)\alpha=0$. Then $\alpha=\sum_iE_i\alpha=E_j\alpha$. $\square$

**Proof of parts 1 and 2 of Theorem 3.1.** By Proposition 4.2 and [Chapter 17](ch17.md), $V$ is the direct sum of the ranges of the $E_i$, which by Proposition 5.1 are the $W_i$. Each $W_i$ is invariant: if $p_i(T)^{r_i}\alpha=0$ then $p_i(T)^{r_i}T\alpha=Tp_i(T)^{r_i}\alpha=0$. Each $W_i$ is nonzero: if $W_i=0$, then $p_i(T)^{r_i}$ is injective, and $p_i(T)^{r_i}f_i(T)=m(T)=0$ forces $f_i(T)=0$. That contradicts the minimality of $m$, because $\deg f_i<\deg m$. $\square$

## 6. The minimal polynomial of each piece [16:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qra533N0-nw&t=1000s)

**Proof of part 3.** Let $s$ be the minimal polynomial of $T_j$. Since $p_j(T_j)^{r_j}=0$ on $W_j$ by the definition of $W_j$, $s$ divides $p_j^{r_j}$. For the other direction we show $(sf_j)(T)=0$. Write $\alpha=\sum_i\alpha_i$ with $\alpha_i\in W_i$. For $i\ne j$, $f_j$ contains $p_i^{r_i}$, so $f_j(T)\alpha_i=0$. For $i=j$,

$$(sf_j)(T)\alpha_j=f_j(T)\,s(T)\alpha_j=0,$$

since $s(T)\alpha_j=s(T_j)\alpha_j=0$. Hence $m$ divides $sf_j$. Cancelling $f_j$ from $m=p_j^{r_j}f_j$ shows that $p_j^{r_j}$ divides $s$. Both are monic, so $s=p_j^{r_j}$. $\square$

The hinge of the whole theorem is Lemma 4.1. It turns the coprimeness of the factors, a statement about polynomials, into projections, a statement about vectors.

**On the running example.** The minimal polynomial of $M$ is

$$m=(x-2)^2(x+1)(x^2+1)=x^5-3x^4+x^3+x^2+4.$$

The factor $x^2+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. The cofactors are $f_1=(x+1)(x^2+1)$, $f_2=(x-2)^2(x^2+1)$ and $f_3=(x-2)^2(x+1)$. A Bezout identity is $g_1f_1+g_2f_2+g_3f_3=1$ with

$$g_1=\frac{49-17x}{225},\qquad g_2=\frac1{18},\qquad g_3=\frac{x+7}{50}.$$

The $g_i$ can be found one factor at a time. For example, modulo $x^2+1$ we have $f_3\equiv7-x$, and $(7-x)(7+x)=49-x^2\equiv50$, so $g_3=(x+7)/50$. Evaluating $h_i=g_if_i$ at $M$ gives the three coordinate projections onto $\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)$, $\operatorname{span}(e_3)$ and $\operatorname{span}(e_4,e_5)$. So

$$W_1=\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2),\qquad W_2=\operatorname{span}(e_3),\qquad W_3=\operatorname{span}(e_4,e_5),$$

of dimensions $2,1,2$, with restrictions having minimal polynomials $(x-2)^2$, $x+1$ and $x^2+1$. In this example the answer could be read from the block form. The point is that the formula $E_i=h_i(M)$ needs no block form: for $M'=PMP^{-1}$ with any invertible $P$, the same polynomials give $h_i(M')=Ph_i(M)P^{-1}$, the projections of the transformed components.

## 7. Two consequences

**Invariant subspaces split along the components.**

**Corollary 7.1.** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 3.1, every $T$-invariant subspace $U$ satisfies $U=(U\cap W_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus(U\cap W_k)$.

*Proof.* Each $E_i$ is a polynomial in $T$, so $E_iU\subseteq U$, and $E_iU\subseteq W_i$. Every $u\in U$ equals $\sum_iE_iu$ with $E_iu\in U\cap W_i$. The sum is direct because the $W_i$ are independent. $\square$

**The split case.** Suppose every $p_i$ is linear, $p_i=x-c_i$; over $\mathbb{C}$ this is always so. Then the $c_i$ are the distinct eigenvalues, and $W_i=\ker(T-c_iI)^{r_i}$ contains the eigenspace of $c_i$. These are called *generalized eigenspaces*.

**Corollary 7.2.** In the split case, $\dim W_i$ is the algebraic multiplicity of $c_i$ in the characteristic polynomial $c_T$.

*Proof.* The operator $T_i-c_iI$ on $W_i$ satisfies $(T_i-c_iI)^{r_i}=0$. So the only eigenvalue of $T_i$ is $c_i$, and $T_i$ is triangulable ([Chapter 16](ch16.md)) with every diagonal entry $c_i$. Hence $c_{T_i}(x)=(x-c_i)^{\dim W_i}$. In a basis adapted to the decomposition the matrix of $T$ is block diagonal, so $c_T=\prod_ic_{T_i}$. $\square$

## 8. A first look at the diagonalizable and nilpotent parts [21:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eyIs2irRboE&t=1315s)

An operator $N$ is *nilpotent* if $N^r=0$ for some positive integer $r$. Suppose $m_T=\prod(x-c_i)^{r_i}$ splits, with the projections $E_i$ above. Put

$$D=c_1E_1+\cdots+c_kE_k,\qquad N=T-D=\sum_i(T-c_iI)E_i.$$

The operator $D$ is diagonalizable ([Chapter 18](ch18.md), since each $E_i\ne0$ and the $c_i$ are distinct). Because the $E_i$ commute with $T$ and annihilate each other,

$$N^r=\sum_i(T-c_iI)^rE_i .$$

The $i$th term vanishes when $r\ge r_i$, since $E_i$ maps into $W_i$. So $N^r=0$ for $r\ge\max r_i$. Therefore $T=D+N$ with $D$ diagonalizable, $N$ nilpotent, and, both being polynomials in $T$, $DN=ND$. [Chapter 20](ch20.md) proves that these two properties determine $D$ and $N$.

**On the running example.** Over $\mathbb{Q}$ the polynomial $x^2+1$ does not split, so apply the construction to the block $A$ alone. There $D=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,-1)$ and $N$ has the single nonzero entry $1$ in position $(2,1)$, with $N^2=0$. For the whole of $M$ one can still keep $D=2E_1-E_2+ME_3$ and $N=(M-2I)E_1$. Then $N^2=0$ and $DN=ND$, but $D$ is not diagonalizable over $\mathbb{Q}$, since on $W_3$ it is the quarter turn. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it is.

## 9. Where the hypotheses bind

**The exponent matters.** With exponent $1$ instead of $2$, $\ker(M-2I)$ is only a line, and $\ker(M-2I)+W_2+W_3$ has dimension $4$, not $5$.

**Coprimeness matters.** The polynomials $(x-2)^2$ and $(x-2)(x+1)$ share a factor. Their kernels on $\mathbb{Q}^5$ have dimension $2$ each, but their sum has dimension $3$, because both contain $e_2$.

**Finite dimension.** The proof used finite dimension only to obtain a nonzero polynomial that annihilates $T$. Theorem 3.1 therefore holds on any vector space for an operator that satisfies some nonzero polynomial. It fails to apply to differentiation on $F[x]$ in characteristic $0$, which satisfies none.

**The field.** The decomposition depends on $F$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the factor $x^2+1$ becomes $(x-i)(x+i)$, and $W_3$ splits into two lines.

## Exercises

1. (Computation.) For the block $A$, find polynomials $h_1,h_2$ with $h_1+h_2=1$ that give the projections onto $\ker(A-2I)^2$ and $\ker(A+I)$.
2. (Complex splitting.) Over $\mathbb{C}$, compute the two projections that split the block $C$ along the factors $x-i$ and $x+i$.
3. (Invariant subspaces.) Use Corollary 7.1 to list all $T$-invariant subspaces of $\mathbb{Q}^5$ for the running example.
4. (Two factors.) Let $q_1,q_2$ be relatively prime and $q_1(T)q_2(T)=0$. Prove that $V=\ker q_1(T)\oplus\ker q_2(T)$ and that $\ker q_1(T)=\operatorname{im}q_2(T)$, with no dimension hypothesis.
5. (Involutions.) Let $F$ have characteristic different from $2$ and $T^2=I$. Find the projections of Theorem 3.1 as polynomials in $T$. What happens in characteristic $2$?
6. (Field dependence.) Let $S$ be the cyclic shift of $F^4$, $Se_1=e_2$, $Se_2=e_3$, $Se_3=e_4$, $Se_4=e_1$. Find the primary decomposition for $F=\mathbb{Q}$, for $F=\mathbb{F}_5$ and for $F=\mathbb{F}_2$.
