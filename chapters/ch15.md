# Invariant subspaces, conductors, and triangulability

Let $T$ be the operator on $\mathbb{Q}^4$ whose matrix in the standard basis $(e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4)$ is

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1&0\\-2&4&0&1\\-3&3&0&2\\-1&1&-1&3\end{pmatrix}.$$

This matrix is the running example of the chapter. It is an original construction. Its characteristic polynomial is $\det(xI-A)=(x-2)^3(x-3)$, as Section 5 shows without expanding the $4\times4$ determinant. The operator is not diagonalizable: the eigenvectors for $2$ and for $3$ span only a plane, so no basis of $\mathbb{Q}^4$ consists of eigenvectors. Most operators with repeated eigenvalues behave in this way or can. We therefore need a weaker simple form than a diagonal matrix.

The idea is to look for subspaces that $T$ does not leave. If such a subspace $W$ exists, then part of the behaviour of $T$ can be studied inside $W$, where the dimension is smaller, and the matrix of $T$ acquires a block of zeros. A chain of such subspaces, one in each dimension, gives a triangular matrix. This chapter builds the two tools for finding the chain: invariant subspaces, and a polynomial called the conductor that measures how far a vector is from an invariant subspace.

**Conventions.** $F$ is a field, $V$ is a vector space over $F$, and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ is a linear operator on $V$, that is, a linear map from $V$ to $V$. Finite dimension is stated where it is used. $F[x]$ is the ring of polynomials with coefficients in $F$. For $p=\sum_kp_kx^k\in F[x]$ we write $p(T)=\sum_kp_kT^k$, with $T^0=I$ the identity. Since powers of $T$ commute, $(pq)(T)=p(T)q(T)=q(T)p(T)$. When $\dim V=n$ is finite, the *minimal polynomial* $m_T$ is the monic polynomial of least degree with $m_T(T)=0$, and it divides every $p$ with $p(T)=0$ ([Chapter 13](ch13.md)). The *characteristic polynomial* is $c_T(x)=\det(xI-T)$. A scalar $c\in F$ is an *eigenvalue* if $Tv=cv$ for some $v\neq0$; the *eigenspace* is $E_c=\ker(T-cI)$. The eigenvalues in $F$ are exactly the roots in $F$ of $c_T$, and also exactly the roots in $F$ of $m_T$ ([Chapter 12](ch12.md), [Chapter 14](ch14.md)). The Cayley–Hamilton theorem says $c_T(T)=0$ ([Chapter 14](ch14.md)). The same notation is used for a square matrix $M$, viewed as the operator $v\mapsto Mv$.

## 1. Invariant subspaces [01:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K-RIfSg3uWI&t=100s)

**Definition 1.1.** A subspace $W\subseteq V$ is *invariant under $T$*, or *$T$-invariant*, if $T\alpha\in W$ for every $\alpha\in W$. In set notation, $T(W)\subseteq W$.

The definition asks only that $T$ send $W$ into $W$. It does not ask that $T$ fix any vector, and it does not ask that $T(W)$ equal $W$. The zero operator maps every subspace to $0$, so every subspace is invariant under it.

**Lemma 1.2.** Suppose $W=\operatorname{span}(w_1,\dots,w_k)$. Then $W$ is $T$-invariant if and only if $Tw_j\in W$ for each $j$.

*Proof.* If $W$ is invariant, each $Tw_j$ lies in $W$ because $w_j$ does. Conversely, a general vector of $W$ is $\alpha=\sum_ja_jw_j$, and $T\alpha=\sum_ja_jTw_j$ is a linear combination of vectors of $W$. $\square$

The lemma turns an infinite condition into $k$ membership tests, each of which is a linear system. For the running example take $w_1=(1,1,0,0)$ and $w_2=(0,1,1,0)$ and let $W=\operatorname{span}(w_1,w_2)$. (Vectors are columns; we write them as tuples to save space.) Direct multiplication gives

$$Aw_1=(2,2,0,0)=2w_1,\qquad Aw_2=(1,4,3,0)=w_1+3w_2.$$

Both images lie in $W$, so $W$ is $T$-invariant. By contrast, $Ae_1=(2,-2,-3,-1)$ is not a multiple of $e_1$, so the line through $e_1$ is not invariant.

## 2. First examples: trivial subspaces, kernel, range, and polynomials [03:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K-RIfSg3uWI&t=194s)

Four subspaces are invariant under every operator $T$.

- The zero subspace: $T0=0$.
- The whole space $V$: $T$ maps $V$ into $V$.
- The kernel $\ker T$: if $T\alpha=0$ then $T(T\alpha)=T0=0$, so $T\alpha\in\ker T$.
- The range $\operatorname{im}T$: if $\beta\in\operatorname{im}T$ then $T\beta$ is $T$ applied to a vector, hence lies in $\operatorname{im}T$.

These can coincide with the first two. For an invertible operator, $\ker T=0$ and $\operatorname{im}T=V$, and they give nothing new. For the running example $\det A=24\neq0$, the value of $c_T$ at $0$, so more work is needed to find useful invariant subspaces.

**Polynomials and the derivative.** Let $V=F[x]$, which is infinite-dimensional, and let $D$ be the formal derivative, $D\big(\sum_ka_kx^k\big)=\sum_{k\ge1}ka_kx^{k-1}$. Let $P_n$ be the subspace of polynomials of degree at most $n$, including the zero polynomial. Differentiation never raises the degree, so $D(P_n)\subseteq P_n$. Each $P_n$ is invariant, and we get an infinite chain $P_0\subset P_1\subset P_2\subset\cdots$ of invariant subspaces.

The invariance depends on the operator, not only on the subspace. Let $M$ be multiplication by $x$, so $Mp=xp$. Then $x^n\in P_n$ but $Mx^n=x^{n+1}\notin P_n$. So $P_n$ is not $M$-invariant.

## 3. Commuting operators and eigenspaces [09:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K-RIfSg3uWI&t=596s)

**Theorem 3.1.** Let $T,U\in\mathcal{L}(V)$ with $TU=UT$. Then $\ker U$ and $\operatorname{im}U$ are $T$-invariant. No assumption on $\dim V$ is needed.

*Proof.* Let $\alpha\in\operatorname{im}U$, say $\alpha=U\beta$. Then $T\alpha=TU\beta=U(T\beta)$, which lies in $\operatorname{im}U$. Let $\alpha\in\ker U$. Then $U(T\alpha)=T(U\alpha)=T0=0$, so $T\alpha\in\ker U$. $\square$

The hinge is the exchange $TU=UT$: it moves $T$ past $U$ so that the defining property of the subspace can be used. Without commutation the conclusion can fail. On $F^2$ let $T(a,b)=(0,a)$ and $U(a,b)=(a,0)$. Then $\operatorname{im}U$ is the first coordinate axis, and $T(1,0)=(0,1)$ leaves it.

**Corollary 3.2.** For every $p\in F[x]$, the subspaces $\ker p(T)$ and $\operatorname{im}p(T)$ are $T$-invariant. In particular every eigenspace $E_c$ is $T$-invariant.

*Proof.* $p(T)$ commutes with $T$, so Theorem 3.1 applies with $U=p(T)$. For $E_c$ take $p=x-c$. Directly: $\alpha\in E_c$ gives $T\alpha=c\alpha\in E_c$. $\square$

A subspace of an eigenspace is also invariant, since each of its vectors is only rescaled. A line $\operatorname{span}(v)$ with $v\neq0$ is invariant exactly when $Tv=cv$ for some $c$, that is, exactly when $v$ is an eigenvector. So the invariant lines are the lines spanned by eigenvectors.

**On the running example.** Write $N=A-2I$. Row reduction gives $E_2=\ker N=\operatorname{span}(w_1)$ and $E_3=\ker(A-3I)=\operatorname{span}(1,2,1,0)$. Since $(1,2,1,0)=w_1+w_2$, the subspace of Section 1 is $W=E_2\oplus E_3$. Corollary 3.2 also supplies the kernels of the powers of $N$. They have dimensions $1,2,3$ for $N,N^2,N^3$, with $\ker N^2=\operatorname{span}(w_1,(0,0,1,1))$. So $A$ has more invariant subspaces than its eigenvectors alone show.

**Sums and intersections.** If $W_1$ and $W_2$ are $T$-invariant, then so are $W_1+W_2$ and $W_1\cap W_2$ (Exercise 2). A complement of an invariant subspace need not be invariant, and an invariant complement need not exist at all.

## 4. Restriction and the block form of the matrix [16:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K-RIfSg3uWI&t=964s)

**Definition 4.1.** Let $W$ be $T$-invariant. The *restriction* of $T$ to $W$ is the map $T_W:W\to W$ with $T_W\alpha=T\alpha$.

The restriction is a linear operator on $W$ precisely because the values $T\alpha$ lie in $W$. For a subspace that is not invariant, $T$ restricted to $W$ is still a linear map $W\to V$, but it is not an operator on $W$ and has no characteristic polynomial.

Now let $\dim V=n$ and $\dim W=k$ with $1\le k<n$. Choose an ordered basis $\mathcal{B}_W=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k)$ of $W$ and extend it to an ordered basis $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ of $V$ ([Chapter 4](ch04.md)). We call such a basis *adapted* to $W$. Recall that column $j$ of $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$ holds the coordinates of $T\alpha_j$.

**Theorem 4.2.** Let $\mathcal{B}$ be adapted to a subspace $W$ of dimension $k$. Then $W$ is $T$-invariant if and only if

$$[T]_{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}B&C\\0&D\end{pmatrix},$$

with $B\in F^{k\times k}$, $C\in F^{k\times(n-k)}$, $D\in F^{(n-k)\times(n-k)}$, and a zero block of size $(n-k)\times k$. In that case $B=[T_W]_{\mathcal{B}_W}$.

*Proof.* For $j\le k$, write $T\alpha_j=\sum_{i=1}^na_{ij}\alpha_i$. Because the coordinates in a basis are unique and $W=\operatorname{span}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k)$, the vector $T\alpha_j$ lies in $W$ if and only if $a_{ij}=0$ for every $i>k$. By Lemma 1.2, invariance is the statement $T\alpha_j\in W$ for all $j\le k$, which is therefore the statement that the lower left block vanishes. When it holds, $T\alpha_j=\sum_{i\le k}a_{ij}\alpha_i$, so the entries $a_{ij}$ with $i,j\le k$ are the matrix of $T_W$. $\square$

The columns $j>k$ are unrestricted: $T\alpha_j$ may have components both in $W$, recorded in $C$, and along the added vectors, recorded in $D$. If in addition $\operatorname{span}(\alpha_{k+1},\dots,\alpha_n)$ is $T$-invariant, the same argument gives $C=0$, and the matrix is block diagonal.

**On the running example.** Extend $(w_1,w_2)$ by $w_3=(0,0,1,1)$ and $w_4=(0,0,0,1)$. Let $P$ have the columns $w_1,\dots,w_4$; it is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal, hence invertible. Computing $Aw_3=(1,1,2,2)$ and $Aw_4=(0,1,2,3)$ and solving for coordinates gives

$$P^{-1}AP=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1&0\\0&3&0&1\\0&0&2&1\\0&0&0&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad D=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.$$

For instance, the third column says $Aw_3=w_1+2w_3$, and indeed $(1,1,0,0)+(0,0,2,2)=(1,1,2,2)$.

## 5. What the blocks say about the two polynomials [23:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K-RIfSg3uWI&t=1427s)

**Theorem 5.1.** Let $\dim V$ be finite, let $W$ be a $T$-invariant subspace with $0\neq W\neq V$, and let $B$ and $D$ be the diagonal blocks of Theorem 4.2. Then

1. $c_T(x)=\det(xI-B)\,\det(xI-D)$, so $c_{T_W}$ divides $c_T$;
2. $m_{T_W}$ divides $m_T$, and $m_D$, the minimal polynomial of the matrix $D$, divides $m_T$;
3. $m_T$ divides the product $m_{T_W}\,m_D$.

*Proof.* Let $M=[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$. For part 1, $xI-M$ is block upper triangular with diagonal blocks $xI-B$ and $xI-D$. The determinant of a block upper triangular matrix is the product of the determinants of the diagonal blocks: in the permutation expansion of the determinant, a nonzero term cannot send any of the first $k$ columns to a row below the $k$-th, so each contributing permutation permutes the first $k$ indices among themselves and the remaining indices among themselves, and the sum factors. Since $B$ represents $T_W$, the first factor is $c_{T_W}$.

For part 2, block multiplication shows that a product of two block upper triangular matrices, with the same block sizes, is block upper triangular, and that its diagonal blocks are the products of the diagonal blocks. Hence $M^r$ has diagonal blocks $B^r$ and $D^r$ for every $r\ge0$. Taking linear combinations,

$$p(M)=\begin{pmatrix}p(B)&C_p\\0&p(D)\end{pmatrix}\qquad(p\in F[x])$$

for some matrix $C_p$. Take $p=m_T$. Then $p(M)=0$, so $p(B)=0$ and $p(D)=0$. A polynomial that annihilates $B$ is a multiple of $m_B=m_{T_W}$, and the same holds for $D$.

For part 3, put $q=m_{T_W}$ and $s=m_D$. The displayed formula gives

$$q(M)=\begin{pmatrix}0&X\\0&Z\end{pmatrix},\qquad s(M)=\begin{pmatrix}Y&U\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$$

for some blocks $X,Z,Y,U$. In the product $q(M)\,s(M)$, every one of the four blocks is a sum of two products, and each product has a zero factor: the first column of $q(M)$ is zero and the second row of $s(M)$ is zero. So $(qs)(T)=0$, and $m_T$ divides $qs$. $\square$

The hinge of part 2 is that the upper right block, however complicated, never feeds back into the diagonal blocks. That block does affect $m_T$. This is why part 3 gives only divisibility into the product, and why $m_T$ need not equal the least common multiple of $m_{T_W}$ and $m_D$.

The matrix $D$ depends on the vectors chosen to extend the basis, but Theorem 5.1 holds for every choice.

**On the running example.** The block $B$ has the distinct eigenvalues $2,3$ and so $m_B=(x-2)(x-3)$. The block $D$ satisfies $D-2I\neq0$ and $(D-2I)^2=0$, so $m_D=(x-2)^2$. Part 1 gives $c_T=(x-2)(x-3)\cdot(x-2)^2=(x-2)^3(x-3)$, as claimed in the opening. Part 2 says $m_T$ is divisible by $(x-2)(x-3)$ and by $(x-2)^2$, hence by $(x-2)^2(x-3)$. Part 3 says it divides $(x-2)^3(x-3)$. Both possibilities pass these tests, so we compute: $(A-2I)^2(A-3I)$ is not zero (its first row is $(1,-1,1,-1)$). Therefore $m_T=(x-2)^3(x-3)$. The least common multiple $(x-2)^2(x-3)$ is a proper divisor of $m_T$, and the block $C$ is the reason.

## 6. An operator with no invariant line [30:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K-RIfSg3uWI&t=1854s)

Not every operator has a nontrivial invariant subspace. Let $R$ be the operator on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, the rotation by a quarter turn.

**Proposition 6.1.** The only subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^2$ invariant under $R$ are $0$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$.

*Proof.* Both are invariant. Any other subspace $W$ has dimension $1$, say $W=\operatorname{span}(\beta)$ with $\beta\neq0$. If $W$ were invariant, then $R\beta\in W$, so $R\beta=c\beta$ for some $c\in\mathbb{R}$. Then $c$ would be an eigenvalue. But $c_R(x)=x^2+1$ has no real root. $\square$

The field is the whole issue. The same matrix acting on $\mathbb{C}^2$ has the eigenvalues $i$ and $-i$. Since $R(1,-i)=(i,1)=i\,(1,-i)$ and $R(1,i)=(-i,1)=-i\,(1,i)$, the two lines $\operatorname{span}(1,-i)$ and $\operatorname{span}(1,i)$ are invariant. In general, an operator on a two-dimensional space has an invariant line if and only if its characteristic polynomial has a root in $F$, by the remark after Corollary 3.2.

## 7. The conductor of a vector into an invariant subspace [01:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wTr4FJ6ogvM&t=103s)

A vector $v$ outside an invariant subspace $W$ can often be pushed into $W$ by applying a suitable polynomial in $T$. The collection of all such polynomials records the position of $v$ relative to $W$.

**Definition 7.1.** Let $W$ be a $T$-invariant subspace and $v\in V$. Put

$$S_T(v;W)=\{g\in F[x]:\ g(T)v\in W\}.$$

**The set is large.** It contains the zero polynomial. If $\dim V$ is finite, it contains nonzero polynomials: $m_T(T)v=0\in W$, so $m_T\in S_T(v;W)$, and by Cayley–Hamilton $c_T\in S_T(v;W)$ as well.

**Invariance passes to polynomials.** If $T(W)\subseteq W$, then $T^2(W)=T(T(W))\subseteq T(W)\subseteq W$, and by induction $T^k(W)\subseteq W$ for every $k\ge0$. A subspace is closed under linear combinations, so $f(T)W\subseteq W$ for every $f\in F[x]$.

**Definition 7.2.** An *ideal* of $F[x]$ is a nonempty subset $J$ that is closed under addition and under multiplication by arbitrary polynomials: $g,h\in J$ and $f\in F[x]$ imply $g+h\in J$ and $fg\in J$.

Since $F[x]$ is commutative, there is no distinction between left and right ideals. Taking $f$ constant shows that an ideal is a subspace of the vector space $F[x]$ over $F$.

**Theorem 7.3.** If $W$ is $T$-invariant, then $S_T(v;W)$ is an ideal of $F[x]$.

*Proof.* It contains $0$. If $g,h\in S_T(v;W)$, then $(g+h)(T)v=g(T)v+h(T)v\in W$ because $W$ is a subspace. If $g\in S_T(v;W)$ and $f\in F[x]$, then $(fg)(T)v=f(T)\big(g(T)v\big)$. The vector $g(T)v$ lies in $W$, and $f(T)$ maps $W$ into $W$. $\square$

Invariance is used only in the last step, and it cannot be dropped. On $F^2$ let $T(a,b)=(0,a)$, let $W=\operatorname{span}(e_1)$, which is not invariant, and let $v=e_1$. Then $1\in S_T(v;W)$ because $v\in W$. But $x\cdot1=x$ is not, because $Tv=e_2\notin W$. The set is still a subspace of $F[x]$, but not an ideal.

## 8. The monic generator and its properties [16:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wTr4FJ6ogvM&t=974s)

**Lemma 8.1.** Every ideal $J\neq0$ of $F[x]$ contains exactly one monic polynomial $g$ of least degree, and $J$ consists of all multiples of $g$.

*Proof.* Among the nonzero elements of $J$ pick one of least degree and divide by its leading coefficient; the result $g$ is monic and still in $J$. For $h\in J$, the division algorithm gives $h=qg+r$ with $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg g$. Then $r=h-qg\in J$, so minimality forces $r=0$. If $g'$ is another monic element of least degree, then $g-g'\in J$ has smaller degree, so $g=g'$. $\square$

**Definition 8.2.** Suppose $S_T(v;W)\neq0$. Its monic generator is the *$T$-conductor of $v$ into $W$*. We denote it $g_W$ when $v$ is fixed. When $W=0$ it is called the *$T$-annihilator of $v$*.

The word "conductor" is used for the polynomial. Some authors use it for the whole ideal.

**Theorem 8.3.** Let $\dim V$ be finite, let $W$ be $T$-invariant, and let $g_W$ be the conductor of $v$ into $W$.

1. $g_W$ divides $m_T$, and hence divides $c_T$.
2. $g_W=1$ if and only if $v\in W$.
3. If $W_1\subseteq W_2$ are $T$-invariant, then $g_{W_2}$ divides $g_{W_1}$.
4. $\deg g_W$ is the least $d\ge0$ with $T^dv\in W+\operatorname{span}(v,Tv,\dots,T^{d-1}v)$, and $\deg g_W\le\dim V-\dim W$.

*Proof.* (1) $m_T$ belongs to the ideal, so it is a multiple of $g_W$. Cayley–Hamilton says that $m_T$ divides $c_T$. (2) $g_W=1$ means $1\in S_T(v;W)$, that is, $v=1(T)v\in W$. (3) If $g(T)v\in W_1$ then $g(T)v\in W_2$, so $S_T(v;W_1)\subseteq S_T(v;W_2)$. In particular $g_{W_1}$ lies in the second ideal and is a multiple of its generator. (4) A monic polynomial $x^d-\sum_{i<d}a_ix^i$ lies in $S_T(v;W)$ exactly when $T^dv-\sum_{i<d}a_iT^iv\in W$. So the least degree of a monic member is the least $d$ in the statement. For the bound, let $d=\deg g_W$ and suppose $\sum_{i<d}b_iT^iv\in W$. The polynomial $\sum_{i<d}b_ix^i$ lies in the ideal and has degree below $d$, so it is zero. Hence $v,Tv,\dots,T^{d-1}v$ are linearly independent, and their span meets $W$ only in $0$. Therefore $d+\dim W\le\dim V$. $\square$

Part 3 compares the two extreme cases. Taking $W_1=0$ and $W_2=V$ gives the largest and smallest conductors: the $T$-annihilator of $v$ at one end and $1$ at the other. Part 4 is an algorithm: test $d=0,1,2,\dots$, each test being one linear system.

**On the running example.** Take $v=e_4$. Then

$$Ne_4=(0,1,2,1),\quad N^2e_4=(2,3,1,0)=2w_1+w_2,\quad N^3e_4=(1,2,1,0)=w_1+w_2,\quad N^4e_4=N^3e_4,$$

where $N=A-2I$. The subspace $W$ consists of the vectors $(a,a+b,b,0)$, so $e_4\notin W$. A monic polynomial of degree $1$ can be written $(x-2)+t$ with $t\in\mathbb{Q}$, and it sends $e_4$ to $Ne_4+te_4=(0,1,2,1+t)$. Membership in $W$ would need $t=-1$, then $a=0$, then $b=1$ from the second coordinate and $b=2$ from the third. This is impossible. So no polynomial of degree at most $1$ works, and $N^2e_4\in W$ shows $g_W=(x-2)^2$. The degree $2$ equals the bound $4-2$.

The same vector has conductor $(x-2)^2(x-3)$ into the line $\operatorname{span}(w_1)$ and $T$-annihilator $(x-2)^3(x-3)$. Along the chain $0\subset\operatorname{span}(w_1)\subset W\subset V$ the conductors are

$$(x-2)^3(x-3),\qquad(x-2)^2(x-3),\qquad(x-2)^2,\qquad1,$$

and each is divisible by the next, as part 3 requires.

**Boundary: infinite dimension.** For multiplication by $x$ on $F[x]$, with $W=0$ and $v=1$, we have $g(M)1=g$. This is zero only for $g=0$. So $S_M(1;0)=0$, and there is no conductor. The finite-dimensional hypothesis is what supplies a nonzero member.

## 9. Triangulable operators [22:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wTr4FJ6ogvM&t=1374s)

A square matrix $M=(a_{ij})$ is *upper triangular* if $a_{ij}=0$ whenever $i>j$.

**Definition 9.1.** Let $\dim V=n$. The operator $T$ is *triangulable* (over $F$) if some ordered basis $\mathcal{B}$ of $V$ makes $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$ upper triangular.

**Proposition 9.2.** Let $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ and $U_j=\operatorname{span}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_j)$. The matrix $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$ is upper triangular if and only if every $U_j$ is $T$-invariant.

*Proof.* Upper triangularity says that $T\alpha_j\in U_j$ for every $j$. If every $U_j$ is invariant, this holds since $\alpha_j\in U_j$. Conversely, suppose $T\alpha_i\in U_i$ for all $i$. For $i\le j$ we have $U_i\subseteq U_j$, so $T\alpha_i\in U_j$, and Lemma 1.2 shows that $U_j$ is invariant. $\square$

So a triangularizing basis is the same thing as a chain of invariant subspaces $U_1\subset U_2\subset\cdots\subset U_n=V$ with $\dim U_j=j$, together with a choice of one new basis vector at each step. A necessary condition is visible at once. If $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$ is upper triangular, then $c_T=\prod_j(x-a_{jj})$, a product of linear factors over $F$. The rotation of Section 6 has $c_R=x^2+1$, so it is not triangulable over $\mathbb{R}$. It is diagonalizable, hence triangulable, over $\mathbb{C}$.

We say that a monic polynomial *splits over $F$* if it is a product $(x-c_1)^{r_1}\cdots(x-c_k)^{r_k}$ with $c_i\in F$ distinct and $r_i\ge1$. The converse of the necessary condition, in terms of $m_T$, is proved in [Chapter 16](ch16.md). Its engine is the next lemma.

## 10. The linear-conductor lemma [25:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wTr4FJ6ogvM&t=1549s)

To extend a chain of invariant subspaces by one dimension, we need a vector $\alpha\notin W$ with $T\alpha\in W+\operatorname{span}(\alpha)$. In the language of conductors, we need $\alpha$ whose conductor into $W$ has degree one.

**Lemma 10.1.** Let $\dim V$ be finite and suppose $m_T=(x-c_1)^{r_1}\cdots(x-c_k)^{r_k}$ splits over $F$. Let $W$ be a $T$-invariant subspace with $W\neq V$. Then there exist $\alpha\notin W$ and an eigenvalue $c$ of $T$ with $(T-cI)\alpha\in W$.

*Proof.* Pick $\beta\notin W$ and let $g$ be the conductor of $\beta$ into $W$. By Theorem 8.3, $g$ divides $m_T$, so by unique factorization $g=(x-c_1)^{e_1}\cdots(x-c_k)^{e_k}$ with $0\le e_i\le r_i$. Since $\beta\notin W$, $g\neq1$, so some $e_j\ge1$. Write $g=(x-c_j)h$ and set $\alpha=h(T)\beta$. The polynomial $h$ is monic of degree $\deg g-1$, so it is not in $S_T(\beta;W)$, which means $\alpha=h(T)\beta\notin W$. And $(T-c_jI)\alpha=g(T)\beta\in W$. Finally $c_j$ is a root of $m_T$, hence an eigenvalue. $\square$

The linear conductor belongs to the new vector $\alpha$, not to the vector $\beta$ with which we started. The conductor of $\alpha$ into $W$ is $x-c_j$, because $x-c_j$ lies in $S_T(\alpha;W)$ and the only monic polynomial of lower degree, $1$, does not lie in it, as $\alpha\notin W$. The hinge is the minimality of $g$, which guarantees that stopping one factor early leaves the vector outside $W$.

The lemma does not say that $\alpha$ is an eigenvector. It says that $T\alpha=c\alpha+w$ with $w\in W$. It becomes an eigenvector when $W=0$.

**On the running example.** With $W$ as before and $\beta=e_4$, we found $g=(x-2)^2$. Here $c=2$ and $h=x-2$, so $\alpha=Ne_4=(0,1,2,1)$. Its fourth coordinate is nonzero, so $\alpha\notin W$, and $(A-2I)\alpha=N^2e_4=2w_1+w_2\in W$. The subspace $W+\operatorname{span}(\alpha)$ is therefore a three-dimensional invariant subspace.

**Iterating.** Start with $W=0$, apply the lemma, add the new vector, and repeat. Each stage produces an invariant subspace one dimension larger. For the running example one such run produces the basis

$$(1,1,0,0),\quad(-3,-3,1,1),\quad(-1,-2,-3,-1),\quad(1,0,0,0),$$

in which the matrix of $T$ has the rows $(2,1,0,0)$, $(0,2,1,0)$, $(0,0,2,1)$ and $(0,0,0,3)$. The diagonal lists $2$ three times and $3$ once, matching the exponents in $c_T$. The argument that this always works is written out in [Chapter 16](ch16.md).

**Where the hypothesis binds.** For the rotation $R$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $W=0$, the lemma would produce a real eigenvector, and none exists. There $m_R=x^2+1$ does not split, and the conductor of every nonzero vector into $0$ is $x^2+1$.

## 11. Invariance seen from the dual space

[Chapter 11](ch11.md) introduced, for $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$, the transpose $T^{\mathsf t}$ on the dual space $V^*$ by $T^{\mathsf t}f=f\circ T$, and the annihilator $W^0=\{f\in V^*: f(w)=0\text{ for all }w\in W\}$. With a basis and its dual basis, the matrix of $T^{\mathsf t}$ is the matrix transpose of the matrix of $T$. Invariance passes to annihilators.

**Proposition 11.1.** If $W$ is $T$-invariant, then $W^0$ is $T^{\mathsf t}$-invariant. If $\dim V$ is finite, the converse holds.

*Proof.* Let $f\in W^0$ and $w\in W$. Then $(T^{\mathsf t}f)(w)=f(Tw)=0$ because $Tw\in W$. So $T^{\mathsf t}f\in W^0$. For the converse, let $w\in W$. For every $f\in W^0$ we have $T^{\mathsf t}f\in W^0$, so $f(Tw)=(T^{\mathsf t}f)(w)=0$. In finite dimension, a vector annihilated by all of $W^0$ lies in $W$ ([Chapter 11](ch11.md)). So $Tw\in W$. $\square$

For the running example, $W^0$ is spanned by the row vectors $f_1=(1,-1,1,0)$ and $f_2=(0,0,0,1)$, and $T^{\mathsf t}$ sends a row vector $f$ to $fA$. We find $f_1A=(1,-1,1,1)=f_1+f_2$ and $f_2A=(-1,1,-1,3)=-f_1+3f_2$. Both lie in $W^0$. The matrix of $T^{\mathsf t}$ on $W^0$ in this basis is $\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$, with characteristic polynomial $x^2-4x+4=(x-2)^2$. This is the characteristic polynomial of the block $D$.

1. With $N=A-2I$ we have $e_4$, $Ne_4=(0,1,2,1)$, $N^2e_4=(2,3,1,0)$ and $N^3e_4=(1,2,1,0)$. The last vector spans $E_3$, so $(x-2)^3\in S_T(e_4;E_3)$. The conductor divides $(x-2)^3$, so it is $(x-2)^j$ with $j\le3$. None of $e_4$, $Ne_4=(0,1,2,1)$, $N^2e_4=(2,3,1,0)$ is a multiple of $(1,2,1,0)$, so $j$ is not $0$, $1$ or $2$. So the conductor is $(x-2)^3$, of degree $3=\dim V-\dim E_3$, which attains the bound. Since $E_3\subseteq W$, part 3 says that the conductor into $W$, namely $(x-2)^2$, divides $(x-2)^3$, which is true. Since $0\subseteq E_3$, the conductor $(x-2)^3$ divides the $T$-annihilator $(x-2)^3(x-3)$, also true.

2. Let $\alpha=\alpha_1+\alpha_2$ with $\alpha_i\in W_i$. Then $T\alpha=T\alpha_1+T\alpha_2$ with $T\alpha_i\in W_i$, so $T\alpha\in W_1+W_2$. If $\alpha\in W_1\cap W_2$, then $T\alpha$ lies in $W_1$ and in $W_2$. For the second part, suppose $\mathbb{Q}^4=W\oplus U$ with $U$ invariant. Then $\dim U=2$. The restriction $T_U$ has a minimal polynomial that divides $m_T=(x-2)^3(x-3)$ by Theorem 5.1(2), and its degree is at least $1$ because $U\neq0$. So it has a root $c\in\{2,3\}$, and $T_U$ has an eigenvector $u\neq0$ in $U$. Then $u$ is an eigenvector of $T$, so $u\in E_2\cup E_3\subseteq W$. This contradicts $W\cap U=0$. So no invariant complement exists. In the language of Section 4, no choice of $w_3,w_4$ makes the block $C$ vanish.

3. In every case $0$ and $F^2$ are invariant, and any other invariant subspace is a line spanned by an eigenvector. For $\operatorname{diag}(2,3)$ the eigenspaces are the two coordinate axes, so there are exactly four invariant subspaces: $0$, $\operatorname{span}(e_1)$, $\operatorname{span}(e_2)$, $F^2$. For $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$, the only eigenvalue is $2$, and $M-2I$ sends $(a,b)$ to $(b,0)$, with kernel $\operatorname{span}(e_1)$. So there are exactly three: $0$, $\operatorname{span}(e_1)$, $F^2$. For $2I$ every vector is an eigenvector, so every subspace of $F^2$ is invariant: $0$, $F^2$, and all lines. The number of lines is $|F|+1$ for a finite field and infinite otherwise.

4. The characteristic polynomial is $x^2+1$. Over $\mathbb{F}_5$ its roots are $2$ and $3$, since $4+1=5=0$ and $9+1=10=0$. The equation $(M-cI)(a,b)=0$ reads $-b=ca$ and $a=cb$. For $c=2$ this gives $b=-2a=3a$, the line through $(1,3)$; check $M(1,3)=(-3,1)=(2,1)=2(1,3)$. For $c=3$ it gives $b=2a$, the line through $(1,2)$; check $M(1,2)=(-2,1)=(3,1)=3(1,2)$. So there are four invariant subspaces, and $((1,3),(1,2))$ is a basis of eigenvectors: the operator is diagonalizable. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ we have $x^2+1=(x+1)^2$ and the only eigenvalue is $1$. The matrix $M-I$ has rows $(1,1)$ and $(1,1)$ since $-1=1$, with kernel $\operatorname{span}(1,1)$. So the invariant subspaces are $0$, $\operatorname{span}(1,1)$, $\mathbb{F}_2^2$. The operator is not diagonalizable, but it is triangulable: in the basis $((1,1),(1,0))$ we have $M(1,1)=(1,1)$ and $M(1,0)=(0,1)=(1,1)+(1,0)$, so the matrix has rows $(1,1)$ and $(0,1)$.

5. $D^2x^4=12x^2$ and $D^3x^4=24x$. Let $g$ be monic of degree $j\le2$ and let $x^b$ be the lowest power of $x$ that occurs in $g$, so $b\le2$. The terms $D^ix^4$ have the distinct degrees $4-i$, so $g(D)x^4$ has degree exactly $4-b\ge2$. So $g(D)x^4\notin P_1$. Since $D^3x^4=24x\in P_1$, the conductor is $x^3$. Now let $W\neq0$ be finite-dimensional and $D$-invariant, and let $n$ be the largest degree of a nonzero element of $W$; it exists because a basis of $W$ bounds all degrees. Pick $p\in W$ of degree $n$. The polynomials $p,Dp,\dots,D^np$ lie in $W$. Over $\mathbb{Q}$, $D^kp$ has degree exactly $n-k$, because the leading coefficient is multiplied by the nonzero integer $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$. Polynomials of degrees $n,n-1,\dots,0$ form a basis of $P_n$. So $P_n\subseteq W\subseteq P_n$. The zero subspace is the remaining case. Over a field of characteristic $p>0$ the argument fails: the derivative of $x^p$ is $0$, so $\operatorname{span}(x^p)$ is invariant.

6. Concatenate a basis of $W$ and a basis of $U$. Both subspaces are invariant, so by Theorem 4.2 applied twice the matrix is block diagonal with blocks $B=[T_W]$ and $E=[T_U]$. Then $\det(xI-M)=\det(xI-B)\det(xI-E)$, which is the first formula. For every polynomial $p$, $p(M)$ is block diagonal with blocks $p(B)$ and $p(E)$. So $p(M)=0$ if and only if $p(B)=0$ and $p(E)=0$, that is, if and only if $p$ is a common multiple of $m_B$ and $m_E$. The monic generator of the common multiples is the least common multiple. For the counterexample take $M=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ and $W=\operatorname{span}(e_1)$. The diagonal blocks are both $(2)$, with least common multiple $x-2$. But $M-2I\neq0$ and $(M-2I)^2=0$, so $m_M=(x-2)^2$.
