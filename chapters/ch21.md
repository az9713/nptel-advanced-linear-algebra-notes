# Inner products, norms, and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

How close is the function $x$ to a constant, and what is the angle between the functions $1$ and $x$ on the interval $[0,1]$? The vector space axioms cannot answer. They give sums and scalar multiples, but no lengths and no angles. Take the space $P_2$ of real polynomials of degree at most $2$, with the rule

$$\langle p,q\rangle=\int_0^1p(t)\,q(t)\,dt .$$

This space with this rule is the running example of the chapter (an original use of a rule that appears in the recordings for continuous functions). We shall find that $1$ and $x$ meet at an angle of exactly $30^\circ$, that the constant closest to $x$ in the resulting distance is $\tfrac12$, and that the basis $(1,x,x^2)$ can be replaced by one whose members are mutually perpendicular.

**Conventions.** Throughout this chapter $F$ is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and $V$ is a vector space over $F$. For $c\in\mathbb{C}$, $\bar c$ is the complex conjugate, $\operatorname{Re}c$ and $\operatorname{Im}c$ the real and imaginary parts, and $|c|=\sqrt{c\bar c}$. For a matrix $A$, $A^{*}=\overline{A}^{\mathsf T}$ is the conjugate transpose. Over $\mathbb{R}$ every bar can be erased.

## 1. The definition of an inner product [02:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRCjsemJ9nk&t=167s)

The dot product $x_1y_1+x_2y_2+x_3y_3$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ measures lengths and angles. We keep its essential properties as axioms.

**Definition 1.1.** An *inner product* on $V$ is a function $V\times V\to F$, written $(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$, such that for all $\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in V$ and $c\in F$:

1. $\langle\alpha+\beta,\gamma\rangle=\langle\alpha,\gamma\rangle+\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle$;
2. $\langle c\alpha,\beta\rangle=c\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$;
3. $\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle=\overline{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}$;
4. $\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle>0$ if $\alpha\ne0$.

Axiom 3 implies that $\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle$ equals its own conjugate, so it is real, and axiom 4 makes sense. Axioms 1 to 3 give

$$\langle\alpha,c\beta+\gamma\rangle=\bar c\,\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle+\langle\alpha,\gamma\rangle .$$

So the inner product is linear in the first argument and *conjugate-linear* in the second. This book uses that convention throughout; physics texts often reverse it.

**Why the conjugate is there.** Suppose we asked for symmetry $\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$ over $\mathbb{C}$. Then $\langle i\alpha,i\alpha\rangle=i^2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle=-\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle$, so $\alpha$ and $i\alpha$ could not both have positive squared length. For instance the rule $x_1y_1+x_2y_2$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ gives $0$ at $x=y=(1,i)$ although $x\ne0$.

## 2. Standard examples [08:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRCjsemJ9nk&t=497s)

**The standard inner product** on $F^n$ is

$$\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^nx_j\overline{y_j}=y^{*}x .$$

Linearity in $x$ and the conjugation rule are visible, and $\langle x,x\rangle=\sum|x_j|^2$ is positive for $x\ne0$.

**Matrices** [13:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRCjsemJ9nk&t=816s). On $F^{n\times n}$ put $\langle A,B\rangle=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\overline{B_{ij}}$. This is the standard inner product of $F^{n^2}$ applied to the entries. It can be written with the *trace*, the sum of the diagonal entries: $\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(AB^{*})$, because the $(i,i)$ entry of $AB^{*}$ is $\sum_kA_{ik}\overline{B_{ik}}$.

**Continuous functions** [22:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRCjsemJ9nk&t=1379s). On the space $C[0,1]$ of continuous $F$-valued functions on $[0,1]$ put

$$\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1f(t)\overline{g(t)}\,dt .$$

The integral is linear, and conjugation passes through it. For positivity, let $f\ne0$. Then $|f(t_0)|>0$ at some point, and by continuity $|f|^2$ stays above a positive number on an interval around $t_0$ inside $[0,1]$. So $\int|f|^2>0$. Continuity matters: a function that is nonzero at one point only has integral $0$. Restricting to $P_2$ gives the running example. There $\langle1,1\rangle=1$, $\langle1,x\rangle=\tfrac12$ and $\langle x,x\rangle=\tfrac13$.

**A rule that fails.** On $P_2$ the rule $p(0)q(0)+p(1)q(1)$ satisfies axioms 1 to 3, but the polynomial $x^2-x$ vanishes at $0$ and $1$. It is nonzero with $\langle p,p\rangle=0$, so axiom 4 fails.

## 3. Inner products pulled back along a map [25:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRCjsemJ9nk&t=1529s)

**Proposition 3.1.** Let $W$ have an inner product and let $T:V\to W$ be linear and injective. Then $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_T=\langle T\alpha,T\beta\rangle$ is an inner product on $V$.

*Proof.* Axioms 1 to 3 follow from the linearity of $T$ and the axioms in $W$. If $\alpha\ne0$ then $T\alpha\ne0$ by injectivity, so $\langle T\alpha,T\alpha\rangle>0$. $\square$

Injectivity is used only in the last step, and it cannot be dropped: for $T(x_1,x_2)=x_1$, the vector $(0,1)$ gets squared length $0$.

**Coordinates.** If $V$ is finite-dimensional with ordered basis $\mathcal{B}$, the coordinate map $\alpha\mapsto[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}\in F^n$ is an injective linear map. So $V$ carries the inner product $\sum x_i\overline{y_i}$, where $x,y$ are the coordinates of $\alpha,\beta$. In this inner product the basis vectors have length $1$ and are mutually perpendicular. Every finite-dimensional real or complex space therefore has inner products, many of them. On $P_2$ with $\mathcal{B}=(1,x,x^2)$ this rule gives $\langle1,x\rangle=0$, while the integral gives $\tfrac12$. The inner product is extra structure that we choose.

## 4. Norm and polarization [00:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xYXFAnXDED0&t=8s)

**Definition 4.1.** The *norm* of $\alpha$ is $\|\alpha\|=\sqrt{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}$. The function $\alpha\mapsto\|\alpha\|^2$ is the *quadratic form* of the inner product.

Expanding with axioms 1 to 3,

$$\|\alpha\pm\beta\|^2=\|\alpha\|^2\pm2\operatorname{Re}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle+\|\beta\|^2 .$$

Adding the two cases gives the **parallelogram law**

$$\|\alpha+\beta\|^2+\|\alpha-\beta\|^2=2\|\alpha\|^2+2\|\beta\|^2 .$$

Subtracting them gives $\operatorname{Re}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\tfrac14\big(\|\alpha+\beta\|^2-\|\alpha-\beta\|^2\big)$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ this recovers the whole inner product from lengths.

**The complex case** [08:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xYXFAnXDED0&t=535s). Since $\langle\alpha,i\beta\rangle=-i\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$, we have $\operatorname{Im}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\langle\alpha,i\beta\rangle$. So the imaginary part is also determined by lengths, and

$$\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\frac14\sum_{n=1}^4i^n\,\|\alpha+i^n\beta\|^2 .$$

This is the **polarization identity**. Consequently two inner products with the same norm are equal, over either field.

**Not every length comes from an inner product.** On $\mathbb{R}^2$ the lengths $|x_1|+|x_2|$ and $\max(|x_1|,|x_2|)$ violate the parallelogram law at $\alpha=(1,0)$, $\beta=(0,1)$: the left side minus the right side is $4$ for the first and $-2$ for the second. So no inner product produces them. (A theorem of analysis, not proved here, says conversely that a norm satisfying the parallelogram law comes from an inner product.)

## 5. The matrix of an inner product [11:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xYXFAnXDED0&t=714s)

Let $\dim V=n$ and $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ an ordered basis. Define $G\in F^{n\times n}$ by

$$G_{jk}=\langle\alpha_k,\alpha_j\rangle .$$

Note the order of the indices. If $\alpha=\sum x_k\alpha_k$ and $\beta=\sum y_j\alpha_j$, then by linearity in the first argument and conjugate-linearity in the second,

$$\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\sum_{j,k}x_k\overline{y_j}\langle\alpha_k,\alpha_j\rangle=\sum_{j,k}\overline{y_j}G_{jk}x_k=Y^{*}GX,$$

where $X,Y$ are the coordinate columns. $G$ is the *matrix of the inner product* in $\mathcal{B}$, also called the *Gram matrix* of $\mathcal{B}$.

**Definition 5.1.** A matrix $G$ is *Hermitian* if $G^{*}=G$ and *positive definite* if it is Hermitian and $X^{*}GX>0$ for every $X\ne0$.

**Theorem 5.2.** (a) The Gram matrix of an inner product is positive definite, hence invertible. (b) Conversely [23:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xYXFAnXDED0&t=1424s), if $G$ is positive definite, then $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=Y^{*}GX$ is an inner product on $V$ whose Gram matrix in $\mathcal{B}$ is $G$.

*Proof.* (a) Axiom 3 gives $\overline{G_{jk}}=\overline{\langle\alpha_k,\alpha_j\rangle}=G_{kj}$, so $G$ is Hermitian. For $X\ne0$ the vector $\alpha$ is nonzero, so $X^{*}GX=\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle>0$. If $GX=0$ then $X^{*}GX=0$, so $X=0$; hence $G$ is invertible. (b) $Y^{*}GX$ is linear in $X$. Its conjugate is $(Y^{*}GX)^{*}=X^{*}G^{*}Y=X^{*}GY$, which is axiom 3. Axiom 4 is positive definiteness. Finally $e_j^{*}Ge_k=G_{jk}$. $\square$

So, after a basis is fixed, inner products correspond exactly to positive definite matrices. Invertible and Hermitian is not enough: $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ is both, and gives $(0,1)$ squared length $-1$.

**On the running example.** In $(1,x,x^2)$,

$$H=\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2&1/3\\1/2&1/3&1/4\\1/3&1/4&1/5\end{pmatrix}.$$

## 6. The Hilbert matrix [27:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xYXFAnXDED0&t=1646s)

The *Hilbert matrix* of size $n$ has the entries $A_{jk}=1/(j+k-1)$. Is it invertible? A direct elimination for general $n$ is unpleasant. Instead, observe that on the space $P_{n-1}$ with the integral inner product,

$$\langle x^{k-1},x^{j-1}\rangle=\int_0^1t^{j+k-2}\,dt=\frac1{j+k-1}.$$

So $A$ is the Gram matrix of the basis $(1,x,\dots,x^{n-1})$. By Theorem 5.2(a) it is positive definite and therefore invertible, for every $n$. The matrix $H$ above is the case $n=3$; its determinant is $1/2160$.

## 7. Inner product spaces and the two inequalities [10:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NYo2wbePAnI&t=616s)

An *inner product space* is a real or complex vector space together with one chosen inner product. A finite-dimensional real one is called *Euclidean*, and a complex one *unitary*.

**Theorem 7.1.** In an inner product space, for all $\alpha,\beta$ and $c\in F$:

1. $\|c\alpha\|=|c|\,\|\alpha\|$;
2. $\|\alpha\|>0$ if $\alpha\ne0$;
3. $|\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle|\le\|\alpha\|\,\|\beta\|$ (**Cauchy–Schwarz**), with equality exactly when $\alpha,\beta$ are linearly dependent;
4. $\|\alpha+\beta\|\le\|\alpha\|+\|\beta\|$ (**triangle inequality**).

*Proof.* 1: $\|c\alpha\|^2=c\bar c\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle$. 2 is axiom 4.

3: If $\alpha=0$ both sides are $0$ and the vectors are dependent. Let $\alpha\ne0$ and put

$$\gamma=\beta-\frac{\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle}{\|\alpha\|^2}\,\alpha .$$

Then $\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle=\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle-\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle=0$. Therefore $\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle=\langle\gamma,\beta\rangle$, and

$$0\le\|\gamma\|^2=\langle\gamma,\beta\rangle=\|\beta\|^2-\frac{|\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle|^2}{\|\alpha\|^2}.$$

This is the inequality. Equality holds exactly when $\gamma=0$, that is, when $\beta$ is a multiple of $\alpha$.

4: $\|\alpha+\beta\|^2=\|\alpha\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle+\|\beta\|^2$, and $\operatorname{Re}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\le|\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle|\le\|\alpha\|\,\|\beta\|$. So the right side is at most $(\|\alpha\|+\|\beta\|)^2$. $\square$

The hinge of part 3 is the choice of $\gamma$: it is what remains of $\beta$ after removing its part along $\alpha$. Section 12 turns this one step into an algorithm.

**Angles.** In a real inner product space, for nonzero $\alpha,\beta$, Cauchy–Schwarz places $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle/(\|\alpha\|\|\beta\|)$ in $[-1,1]$. The *angle* between them is the number in $[0,\pi]$ with that cosine.

**On the running example.** $\cos\theta=\dfrac{1/2}{1\cdot\sqrt{1/3}}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, so the angle between $1$ and $x$ is $30^\circ$. Also $\|1+x\|=\sqrt{7/3}\approx1.5275$, below $\|1\|+\|x\|\approx1.5774$.

## 8. Two classical inequalities [21:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NYo2wbePAnI&t=1317s)

Each inner product turns Cauchy–Schwarz into a concrete inequality. For the standard inner product on $F^n$:

$$\Big|\sum_{j=1}^nx_j\overline{y_j}\Big|\le\Big(\sum_{j=1}^n|x_j|^2\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^n|y_j|^2\Big)^{1/2}.$$

For continuous functions on $[0,1]$:

$$\Big|\int_0^1f\,\overline g\,dt\Big|\le\Big(\int_0^1|f|^2dt\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_0^1|g|^2dt\Big)^{1/2}.$$

Neither needs a separate proof.

## 9. Orthogonality [25:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NYo2wbePAnI&t=1508s)

**Definition 9.1.** Vectors $\alpha,\beta$ are *orthogonal* if $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=0$.

By axiom 3 the relation is symmetric. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector, and by axiom 4 it is the only vector orthogonal to itself. If $\alpha,\beta$ are orthogonal, then $\|\alpha+\beta\|^2=\|\alpha\|^2+\|\beta\|^2$ (**Pythagoras**). Over $\mathbb{R}$ the converse holds; over $\mathbb{C}$ it fails (Exercise 4).

**Orthogonality depends on the inner product.** In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the standard inner product, $(-y,x)$ is orthogonal to $(x,y)$. Now take

$$\langle(x_1,x_2),(y_1,y_2)\rangle=x_1y_1-x_2y_1-x_1y_2+4x_2y_2 .$$

Its matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&4\end{pmatrix}$ has quadratic form $(x_1-x_2)^2+3x_2^2$, so it is positive definite. With $\alpha=(x,y)$ and $\beta=(-y,x)$,

$$\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=y^2+3xy-x^2 .$$

So these two vectors are orthogonal here only when $y=\frac{-3\pm\sqrt{13}}{2}\,x$. The same pair is orthogonal for one inner product and, for other values of $(x,y)$, not for the other.

**Functions** [34:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NYo2wbePAnI&t=2042s). In the real continuous functions on $[-\pi,\pi]$ with $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}fg$, the functions $\sin t$ and $\cos t$ are orthogonal, since $\sin t\cos t=\tfrac12\sin2t$ is odd.

## 10. Orthogonal and orthonormal sets [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yjx3j9IDn6o&t=25s)

**Definition 10.1.** A set $S$ of vectors is *orthogonal* if every two distinct members are orthogonal. It is *orthonormal* if it is orthogonal and every member has norm $1$.

The standard basis of $F^n$ is orthonormal for the standard inner product.

**A trigonometric system** [05:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yjx3j9IDn6o&t=318s). In $C[0,1]$ let $f_n(x)=\sqrt2\cos2\pi nx$ and $g_n(x)=\sqrt2\sin2\pi nx$ for $n\ge1$. The set $\{1,f_1,g_1,f_2,g_2,\dots\}$ is orthonormal. Each product of two members is, by the product-to-sum formulas, a combination of sines and cosines of $2\pi kx$ with integer $k$. For $k\ne0$ their integrals over $[0,1]$ vanish. A constant term $k=0$ appears only in the square of a member, where $f_n^2=1+\cos4\pi nx$ and $g_n^2=1-\cos4\pi nx$, both of integral $1$. This infinite orthonormal set is the starting point of Fourier series.

## 11. Orthogonal sets are independent [13:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yjx3j9IDn6o&t=781s)

**Theorem 11.1.** Let $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ be nonzero orthogonal vectors. If $\beta=\sum_kc_k\alpha_k$, then

$$c_k=\frac{\langle\beta,\alpha_k\rangle}{\|\alpha_k\|^2}.$$

Consequently an orthogonal set of nonzero vectors is linearly independent.

*Proof.* Take the inner product with $\alpha_k$: $\langle\beta,\alpha_k\rangle=\sum_jc_j\langle\alpha_j,\alpha_k\rangle=c_k\|\alpha_k\|^2$, and $\|\alpha_k\|^2\ne0$. If $\beta=0$, every $c_k$ is $0$. For an infinite set, apply this to each finite subset. $\square$

The word "nonzero" cannot be omitted, since the set $\{0\}$ is orthogonal and dependent. The formula is the practical gain: coordinates in an orthogonal basis need no linear system, only one inner product each.

## 12. The Gram–Schmidt process [19:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yjx3j9IDn6o&t=1180s)

**Theorem 12.1.** Let $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n$ be linearly independent in an inner product space. Define $\alpha_1=\beta_1$ and

$$\alpha_{m+1}=\beta_{m+1}-\sum_{k=1}^m\frac{\langle\beta_{m+1},\alpha_k\rangle}{\|\alpha_k\|^2}\,\alpha_k .$$

Then the $\alpha_k$ are nonzero and orthogonal, and $\operatorname{span}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m)=\operatorname{span}(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_m)$ for every $m\le n$.

*Proof.* Induct on $m$. The case $m=1$ is clear. Assume the claims for $m<n$; the formula is then defined, since $\alpha_k\ne0$. If $\alpha_{m+1}=0$, then $\beta_{m+1}$ lies in $\operatorname{span}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m)=\operatorname{span}(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_m)$, contradicting independence. For $j\le m$, orthogonality of the earlier $\alpha_k$ leaves one term of the sum:

$$\langle\alpha_{m+1},\alpha_j\rangle=\langle\beta_{m+1},\alpha_j\rangle-\frac{\langle\beta_{m+1},\alpha_j\rangle}{\|\alpha_j\|^2}\langle\alpha_j,\alpha_j\rangle=0 .$$

Finally $\alpha_{m+1}-\beta_{m+1}$ lies in the earlier span, so the two spans of the first $m+1$ vectors coincide. $\square$

Dividing each $\alpha_k$ by its norm gives an orthonormal list with the same spans. Hence **every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis**.

**An example in $\mathbb{R}^3$** [30:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yjx3j9IDn6o&t=1831s). Take $\beta_1=(3,0,4)$, $\beta_2=(-1,0,7)$, $\beta_3=(2,9,11)$ with the standard inner product. Then $\alpha_1=(3,0,4)$ with $\|\alpha_1\|^2=25$. Since $\langle\beta_2,\alpha_1\rangle=25$, $\alpha_2=\beta_2-\alpha_1=(-4,0,3)$. Since $\langle\beta_3,\alpha_1\rangle=50$ and $\langle\beta_3,\alpha_2\rangle=25$, $\alpha_3=\beta_3-2\alpha_1-\alpha_2=(0,9,0)$.

**On the running example.** Applied to $(1,x,x^2)$ with the integral inner product, the process gives

$$\alpha_1=1,\qquad\alpha_2=x-\tfrac12,\qquad\alpha_3=x^2-x+\tfrac16,$$

with squared norms $1,\ \tfrac1{12},\ \tfrac1{180}$. The second step answers the opening: $x=\tfrac12\cdot1+\alpha_2$ with $\alpha_2$ orthogonal to the constants. For any constant $c$, Pythagoras gives $\|x-c\|^2=\|\alpha_2\|^2+(\tfrac12-c)^2$, so the closest constant is $\tfrac12$, at squared distance $\tfrac1{12}$. [Chapter 22](ch22.md) develops this into the theory of best approximation.

## Exercises

1. (Computation.) In the running example find the norms of $x$ and $x^2$ and the angle between them.
2. (Trace form.) For $\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(AB^{*})$ on $F^{n\times n}$, find $\|I\|$ and deduce $|\operatorname{tr}A|\le\sqrt n\,\|A\|$. When is there equality?
3. (Classification.) Show that $G=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\\bar b&d\end{pmatrix}$ with real $a,d$ is positive definite exactly when $a>0$ and $ad-|b|^2>0$.
4. (Field dependence.) Show that in a complex inner product space $\|\alpha+\beta\|^2=\|\alpha\|^2+\|\beta\|^2$ holds exactly when $\operatorname{Re}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=0$, and give nonorthogonal vectors satisfying it.
5. (Polarization.) For $x=(1,i)$ and $y=(2,1+i)$ in $\mathbb{C}^2$ compute $\langle x,y\rangle$ directly and recover its real and imaginary parts from norms.
6. (Gram matrices.) For any vectors $v_1,\dots,v_m$ let $G_{jk}=\langle v_k,v_j\rangle$. Show that $X^{*}GX\ge0$ for all $X$ and that $G$ is invertible exactly when $v_1,\dots,v_m$ are independent.

1. $\|x\|^2=\int_0^1t^2=\tfrac13$ and $\|x^2\|^2=\int_0^1t^4=\tfrac15$. Also $\langle x,x^2\rangle=\int_0^1t^3=\tfrac14$. So $\cos\theta=\frac{1/4}{\sqrt{1/15}}=\frac{\sqrt{15}}4$ and $\theta\approx14.48^\circ$. The two functions point in more similar directions than $1$ and $x$.

2. $\|I\|^2=\operatorname{tr}(I)=n$. Since $\operatorname{tr}A=\operatorname{tr}(AI^{*})=\langle A,I\rangle$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives $|\operatorname{tr}A|\le\|A\|\,\|I\|=\sqrt n\,\|A\|$. By Theorem 7.1(3) equality holds exactly when $A$ and $I$ are dependent, that is, when $A$ is a scalar matrix.

3. $G$ is Hermitian by its form. For $X=(x_1,x_2)$, $X^{*}GX=a|x_1|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(\bar x_1bx_2)+d|x_2|^2$. If $G$ is positive definite, $X=(1,0)$ gives $a>0$. Then completing the square,
$$X^{*}GX=a\Big|x_1+\frac{b}{a}x_2\Big|^2+\frac{ad-|b|^2}{a}\,|x_2|^2 .$$
Choosing $x_2=1$ and $x_1=-b/a$ shows $ad-|b|^2>0$. Conversely, if $a>0$ and $ad-|b|^2>0$, both terms are nonnegative, and they vanish together only if $x_2=0$ and then $x_1=0$.

4. By the expansion of Section 4, $\|\alpha+\beta\|^2=\|\alpha\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle+\|\beta\|^2$, which gives the equivalence. In $\mathbb{C}$ take $\alpha=1$, $\beta=i$: $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=1\cdot\overline{i}=-i$, with real part $0$ but not zero. Indeed $|1+i|^2=2=1+1$.

5. $\langle x,y\rangle=1\cdot2+i\cdot\overline{(1+i)}=2+i(1-i)=3+i$. For the real part: $x+y=(3,1+2i)$ has squared norm $9+5=14$, and $x-y=(-1,-1)$ has squared norm $2$, so $\operatorname{Re}=\tfrac14(14-2)=3$. For the imaginary part: $x+iy=(1+2i,-1+2i)$ has squared norm $5+5=10$, and $x-iy=(1-2i,1)$ has squared norm $5+1=6$, so $\operatorname{Im}=\tfrac14(10-6)=1$.

6. Put $w=\sum_kx_kv_k$. Then $X^{*}GX=\sum_{j,k}\overline{x_j}x_k\langle v_k,v_j\rangle=\langle w,w\rangle\ge0$. If $GX=0$ then $\langle w,w\rangle=0$, so $w=0$. If the $v_k$ are independent this forces $X=0$, so $G$ is invertible. If they are dependent, take $X\ne0$ with $w=0$; then $(GX)_j=\langle w,v_j\rangle=0$ for every $j$, so $G$ is not invertible.
