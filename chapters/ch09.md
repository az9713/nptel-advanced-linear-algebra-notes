# Matrix representation of linear transformations and similarity

Let $T:\mathbb{Q}^3\to\mathbb{Q}^3$ be given by $TX=AX$ with

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&2&1\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

This operator is the running example (an original construction). Suppose we need $T^{10}$, the effect of applying $T$ ten times. Multiplying $A$ by itself repeatedly is possible but gives no insight. The vectors

$$
\beta_1=(1,0,0),\quad\beta_2=(1,1,0),\quad\beta_3=(1,1,1)
$$

satisfy $T\beta_1=\beta_1$, $T\beta_2=2\beta_2$ and $T\beta_3=3\beta_3$. In the basis $(\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3)$, the operator only stretches three directions, and $T^{10}$ multiplies them by $1$, $2^{10}$ and $3^{10}$. The same operator has two very different tables of numbers. This chapter explains what such a table is, how it depends on the bases, and how the two tables are related.

Throughout, $F$ is a field, $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional vector spaces over $F$ with $\dim V=n$ and $\dim W=m$ ($m,n\ge1$). An *ordered basis* is a basis listed in order, and $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}\in F^n$ is the column of coordinates of $\alpha$ relative to $\mathcal{B}$ ([Chapter 5](ch05.md)). A *linear operator* is a linear map $V\to V$. The superscript $\mathsf T$ on a row denotes the corresponding column.

## 1. The matrix of a linear map [04:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mB_27xaN1nU&t=293s)

Fix ordered bases $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ of $V$ and $\mathcal{C}=(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_m)$ of $W$. A linear map $T:V\to W$ is determined by the images $T\alpha_j$, and each image has coordinates in $\mathcal{C}$:

$$
T\alpha_j=\sum_{i=1}^mM_{ij}\beta_i\qquad(j=1,\dots,n).
$$

**Definition.** The $m\times n$ matrix $M=[T]_{\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}$ is the *matrix of $T$ relative to $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{C}$*. Its $j$th column is $[T\alpha_j]_{\mathcal{C}}$.

Note that the number of rows is $\dim W$ and the number of columns is $\dim V$.

**Theorem 1.1.** For every $\alpha\in V$, $[T\alpha]_{\mathcal{C}}=[T]_{\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}\,[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}$. No other matrix has this property.

**Proof.** Let $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)^{\mathsf T}$. By linearity,

$$
T\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^nx_jT\alpha_j=\sum_{j=1}^nx_j\sum_{i=1}^mM_{ij}\beta_i=\sum_{i=1}^m\Big(\sum_{j=1}^nM_{ij}x_j\Big)\beta_i .
$$

So the $i$th coordinate of $T\alpha$ is the $i$th entry of $M[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}$. If $N$ also satisfies the formula, apply it to $\alpha=\alpha_j$: since $[\alpha_j]_{\mathcal{B}}$ is the $j$th unit column, the $j$th column of $N$ equals $[T\alpha_j]_{\mathcal{C}}$, which is the $j$th column of $M$. $\square$

The matrix thus replaces the abstract map by arithmetic on coordinates. It answers three questions: a representation exists whenever $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional; it is unique once the ordered bases are fixed; and it is computed column by column from the images of the basis vectors.

## 2. Every matrix is the matrix of one map [15:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mB_27xaN1nU&t=927s)

**Theorem 2.1.** For every $M\in F^{m\times n}$ there is exactly one linear $T:V\to W$ with $[T]_{\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}=M$.

**Proof.** By [Chapter 7](ch07.md) there is exactly one linear map $T$ with the prescribed values $T\alpha_j=\sum_iM_{ij}\beta_i$ on the basis $\mathcal{B}$. This is the condition that its matrix is $M$. $\square$

For example, take $V=\mathbb{Q}^3$ with $\mathcal{B}^*=\big((1,1,1),(1,1,0),(1,0,0)\big)$, $W=\mathbb{Q}^2$ with $\mathcal{C}'=\big((1,0),(1,1)\big)$, and $M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&2\\3&1&-1\end{pmatrix}$. The map $U$ with matrix $M$ sends the three basis vectors to $(4,3)$, $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$; for instance $1\cdot(1,0)+3\cdot(1,1)=(4,3)$. Solving for the coordinates of a general $x$ in $\mathcal{B}^*$ and applying $M$ gives $U(x)=(x_1+3x_3,\;-x_1+2x_2+2x_3)$.

## 3. Examples [19:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mB_27xaN1nU&t=1197s)

**A map $\mathbb{Q}^3\to\mathbb{Q}^2$.** Let $T_1(x)=(x_1-2x_2,\;x_2+x_3)$. In the standard bases, the columns are the images of $e_1,e_2,e_3$:

$$
[T_1]=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

With $\mathcal{B}^*$ in the domain and the standard basis in the target, the images $T_1(1,1,1)=(-1,2)$, $T_1(1,1,0)=(-1,1)$, $T_1(1,0,0)=(1,0)$ give

$$
\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1&1\\2&1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

With $\mathcal{C}'$ in the target as well, the coordinates change again: $(-1,2)=-3(1,0)+2(1,1)$, and the matrix becomes $\begin{pmatrix}-3&-2&1\\2&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. One map, three matrices.

**Differentiation.** Let $P_3$ be the space of polynomials of degree at most $3$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and $D$ the derivative, an operator on $P_3$. In $(1,x,x^2,x^3)$ we have $D1=0$, $Dx=1$, $Dx^2=2x$, $Dx^3=3x^2$, so

$$
[D]=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0\\0&0&2&0\\0&0&0&3\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

In the basis $(1,x,x^2/2,x^3/6)$ the nonzero entries all become $1$. The operator satisfies $D^4=0$ but $D^3\neq0$.

**The order of the basis matters.** For the running operator, swapping the first two standard basis vectors, that is, using $(e_2,e_1,e_3)$, gives $\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1\\1&1&1\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}$: rows 1, 2 and columns 1, 2 are exchanged. Also note that the identity map has matrix $I$ only when the same ordered basis is used on both sides.

## 4. The correspondence is a linear isomorphism [32:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mB_27xaN1nU&t=1959s)

Let $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ be the space of linear maps $V\to W$.

**Theorem 4.1.** With $\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}$ fixed, the map $\Psi:\mathcal{L}(V,W)\to F^{m\times n}$, $\Psi(T)=[T]_{\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}$, is an isomorphism of vector spaces.

**Proof.** Theorem 2.1 says that $\Psi$ is bijective. For linearity, let $T_1,T_2$ have matrices $A_1,A_2$ and $c\in F$. Then

$$
(cT_1+T_2)\alpha_j=c\sum_i(A_1)_{ij}\beta_i+\sum_i(A_2)_{ij}\beta_i=\sum_i\big(cA_1+A_2\big)_{ij}\beta_i .
$$

So $\Psi(cT_1+T_2)=c\Psi(T_1)+\Psi(T_2)$. $\square$

Hence $\dim\mathcal{L}(V,W)=\dim F^{m\times n}=mn$, as in [Chapter 8](ch08.md): the maps whose matrices have a single entry $1$ form a basis. A statement about linear maps may therefore be computed with matrices, provided the bases stay fixed.

## 5. Composition corresponds to the matrix product

**Theorem 5.1.** Let $T:V\to W$ and $S:W\to Z$ be linear, with $Z$ finite-dimensional and ordered basis $\mathcal{D}$. Then

$$
[ST]_{\mathcal{D}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}=[S]_{\mathcal{D}\leftarrow\mathcal{C}}\,[T]_{\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}} .
$$

**Proof.** By Theorem 1.1 applied twice, $[ST\alpha]_{\mathcal{D}}=[S]_{\mathcal{D}\leftarrow\mathcal{C}}[T\alpha]_{\mathcal{C}}=[S]_{\mathcal{D}\leftarrow\mathcal{C}}[T]_{\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}$. The matrix with this property is unique by Theorem 1.1. $\square$

This explains the definition of the matrix product: it is exactly the rule that makes composition correspond to multiplication. For $S(y)=(y_1+y_2,\,y_1-y_2)$ after $T_1$,

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&1\\1&-3&-1\end{pmatrix},
$$

which is the matrix of $x\mapsto(x_1-x_2+x_3,\;x_1-3x_2-x_3)$. In particular, for an operator $T$ on $V$ and one ordered basis, $[T^k]_{\mathcal{B}}=[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^k$.

## 6. From a matrix back to a formula, and the row convention [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PiG2BMkK3s4&t=25s)

For $V=F^n$ and $W=F^m$ with standard bases, a matrix $M$ gives back the map explicitly. If $F^n$ is written as columns, the map is $X\mapsto MX$. If $F^n$ is written as rows $x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, then transposing gives $x\mapsto xM^{\mathsf T}$. For $T_2(x)=(x_1+x_2+x_3,\;x_2+x_3)$ the matrix is $M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}$, and in row notation $T_2(x)=xM^{\mathsf T}$, while in column notation $T_2X=MX$. The two formulas describe one map. This book uses columns.

## 7. Change of bases: similarity [10:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PiG2BMkK3s4&t=640s)

How does $[T]$ change when the bases change? Let $\mathcal{B}'$ and $\mathcal{C}'$ be new ordered bases, $P=P_{\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}'}$ and $Q=P_{\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{C}'}$ the change-of-basis matrices of [Chapter 5](ch05.md): $[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=P[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}$. Both are invertible.

**Theorem 7.1.** $[T]_{\mathcal{C}'\leftarrow\mathcal{B}'}=Q^{-1}[T]_{\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}P$. In particular, for an operator $T$ on $V$ and ordered bases $\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}'$ of $V$,

$$
[T]_{\mathcal{B}'}=P^{-1}[T]_{\mathcal{B}}P,
$$

where $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$ means $[T]_{\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}$.

**Proof.** Let $A=[T]_{\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}}$. For every $\alpha\in V$,

$$
Q[T\alpha]_{\mathcal{C}'}=[T\alpha]_{\mathcal{C}}=A[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}}=AP[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}.
$$

Multiply by $Q^{-1}$: $[T\alpha]_{\mathcal{C}'}=Q^{-1}AP[\alpha]_{\mathcal{B}'}$. By the uniqueness part of Theorem 1.1, $Q^{-1}AP$ is the matrix of $T$ in the new bases. For an operator take $W=V$, $\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{C}'=\mathcal{B}'$, so $Q=P$. $\square$

**Definition 7.2.** Square matrices $A,B\in F^{n\times n}$ are *similar* over $F$ if $B=P^{-1}AP$ for an invertible $P\in F^{n\times n}$. Rectangular matrices $A,A'\in F^{m\times n}$ are *equivalent* if $A'=Q^{-1}AP$ with $P,Q$ invertible.

Similarity is an equivalence relation: $A=I^{-1}AI$; $A=(P^{-1})^{-1}BP^{-1}$; and $R^{-1}(P^{-1}AP)R=(PR)^{-1}A(PR)$. So similar matrices are exactly the matrices that can represent one operator.

For the maps of Section 3, with $P$ having columns $(1,1,1),(1,1,0),(1,0,0)$ and $Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, one gets $Q^{-1}[T_1]P=\begin{pmatrix}-3&-2&1\\2&1&0\end{pmatrix}$, as computed there.

## 8. Every invertible matrix realizes a change of basis [23:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PiG2BMkK3s4&t=1411s)

**Theorem 8.1.** Let $T$ be an operator on $V$, $\mathcal{B}$ an ordered basis, and $P$ invertible. There is an ordered basis $\mathcal{B}'$ with $[T]_{\mathcal{B}'}=P^{-1}[T]_{\mathcal{B}}P$.

**Proof.** By [Chapter 5](ch05.md), the vectors $\alpha_j'=\sum_iP_{ij}\alpha_i$ form an ordered basis $\mathcal{B}'$ whose change-of-basis matrix from $\mathcal{B}'$ to $\mathcal{B}$ is $P$. Now Theorem 7.1 applies. $\square$

So if a matrix $B$ is similar to $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$, then $B$ is the matrix of the same operator in some other basis.

## 9. The running example and a second computation [27:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PiG2BMkK3s4&t=1661s)

For the running operator, $\mathcal{B}'=(\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3)$ has the change-of-basis matrix

$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The relations $T\beta_k=k\beta_k$ say that $[T]_{\mathcal{B}'}=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3)$, the diagonal matrix with these entries, and indeed $P^{-1}AP=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3)$. Hence $A=P\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3)P^{-1}$ and, since the inner factors cancel,

$$
A^n=P\operatorname{diag}(1,2^n,3^n)P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2^n-1&3^n-2^n\\0&2^n&3^n-2^n\\0&0&3^n\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $n=10$ the entries are $1$, $1023$, $58025$ in the first row, $1024$ and $58025$ in the second, and $59049$ in the third.

**A second example.** Let $G=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\1&0&1\\2&1&0\end{pmatrix}$ be the matrix of an operator in the standard basis of $\mathbb{Q}^3$, and let $\mathcal{B}''=\big((1,1,1),(1,1,0),(1,0,0)\big)$. Compute $G\alpha$ for each new vector and express it in $\mathcal{B}''$ by solving $c_1+c_2+c_3$, $c_1+c_2$, $c_1$ equal to its three entries. For the first vector, $G(1,1,1)^{\mathsf T}=(2,2,3)^{\mathsf T}$, so $c_1=3$, $c_2=-1$, $c_3=0$. The other two columns give $(3,-2,2)$ and $(2,-1,0)$. So

$$
H=[T]_{\mathcal{B}''}=\begin{pmatrix}3&3&2\\-1&-2&-1\\0&2&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The change-of-basis matrix $P''$ has the new vectors as columns, and $H=P''^{-1}GP''$.

## 10. What similarity preserves and what it does not

**Proposition 10.1.** Similar matrices have the same rank, trace, determinant and characteristic polynomial $\det(xI-A)$.

**Proof.** Let $B=P^{-1}AP$. Rank is the dimension of the range of the operator, which does not depend on the basis. For the determinant, multiplicativity gives $\det B=\det(P^{-1})\det A\det P=\det A$. For the characteristic polynomial, $xI-B=P^{-1}(xI-A)P$, so the same computation, over polynomial entries, gives $\det(xI-B)=\det(xI-A)$. The *trace* $\operatorname{tr}A$ is the sum of the diagonal entries; it is minus the coefficient of $x^{n-1}$ in $\det(xI-A)$, so it is preserved too. $\square$

For the running example and its diagonal form, the trace is $6$, the determinant $6$ and the characteristic polynomial $x^3-6x^2+11x-6$. For $G$ and $H$ the trace is $1$ and the determinant $2$.

The converse fails. The matrices $I$ and $J=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ have rank $2$, trace $2$, determinant $1$ and characteristic polynomial $(x-1)^2$. But $P^{-1}IP=I$ for every invertible $P$, so $I$ is similar only to itself.

**Equivalence is weaker than similarity.** When the two bases of $T:V\to W$ can be chosen independently, much more can be achieved. For $T_1$ of Section 3, choose $a_1=e_1$, $a_2=e_2$ and $a_3=(2,1,-1)$, which spans $\ker T_1$, and in $\mathbb{Q}^2$ the basis $(T_1a_1,T_1a_2)$. Then the matrix of $T_1$ is $\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Every linear map has such a *rank normal form* (Exercise 5), so equivalence remembers only the rank. For operators, where one basis serves both sides, the classification is much finer; it occupies [Chapter 12](ch12.md) onward.

**The field matters.** The rotation $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ is not similar over $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{R}$ to a diagonal matrix, since $x^2+1$ has no real root. Over $\mathbb{C}$, with $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-i&i\end{pmatrix}$, $P^{-1}RP=\operatorname{diag}(i,-i)$.

## Exercises

1. (Computation.) Find the matrix of $T(x_1,x_2)=(x_2,x_1+x_2)$ relative to the basis $\big((1,1),(1,-1)\big)$ of $\mathbb{Q}^2$, in both domain and target.
2. (Operator on polynomials.) Let $S$ on $P_2(\mathbb{Q})$ be $(Sp)(x)=p(x+1)$. Find $[S]$ in $(1,x,x^2)$, and show that $S$ is invertible.
3. (Invariant.) Show that $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ are not similar.
4. (Proof.) Prove that if $A$ and $B$ are similar, then $A^k$ and $B^k$ are similar for every $k\ge1$, and that $p(A)$ and $p(B)$ are similar for every polynomial $p$.
5. (Rank normal form.) Prove that for every linear $T:V\to W$ of rank $r$ there are ordered bases making the matrix of $T$ equal to $\begin{pmatrix}I_r&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$.
6. (Nilpotent.) Let $N$ be an operator on a two-dimensional space with $N\neq0$ and $N^2=0$. Show that some basis gives $N$ the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, and deduce that any two such $2\times2$ matrices are similar.
