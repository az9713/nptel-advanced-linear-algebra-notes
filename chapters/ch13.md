# Diagonalizable operators and annihilating polynomials

The two rational matrices

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}5&-6&-6\\-1&4&2\\3&-6&-4\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
$$

have the same characteristic polynomial $(x-1)(x-2)^2$. Yet $A$ is similar to $\operatorname{diag}(1,2,2)$ and $B$ is not similar to any diagonal matrix. They are the running examples of this chapter. A direct computation shows a difference that no determinant sees:

$$
(A-I)(A-2I)=0,\qquad(B-I)(B-2I)\neq0 .
$$

So $A$ satisfies a quadratic equation and $B$ does not. This chapter shows that this is the whole difference. We collect all polynomials that an operator satisfies, find the smallest one, and prove that the operator is diagonalizable exactly when that polynomial is a product of distinct linear factors.

Throughout, $F$ is a field, $V$ is a vector space over $F$, and $T$ is an operator on $V$ (a linear map $V\to V$). Finite dimension is stated where used. For a polynomial $p=\sum a_mx^m\in F[x]$, where $F[x]$ is the set of polynomials over $F$, we write $p(T)=\sum a_mT^m$ with $T^0=I$, and similarly $p(M)$ for a square matrix $M$. Since powers of $T$ commute, $(pq)(T)=p(T)q(T)=q(T)p(T)$ and $(p+q)(T)=p(T)+q(T)$. An *eigenvalue* $c$ has *eigenspace* $E_c=\ker(T-cI)\neq0$; $c_T$ denotes the characteristic polynomial $\det(xI-T)$ ([Chapter 12](ch12.md)).

## 1. The matrix of a diagonalizable operator [00:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5GnTjNp0C4c&t=25s)

Let $\dim V=n$. The operator $T$ is *diagonalizable* if $V$ has an ordered basis $\mathcal{B}=(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ of eigenvectors. If $T\alpha_i=c_i\alpha_i$, then $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}=\operatorname{diag}(c_1,\dots,c_n)$. Every other matrix of $T$ is similar to this diagonal matrix.

The scalars may repeat. Let $c_1,\dots,c_k$ now be the distinct eigenvalues, each $c_i$ occurring $d_i$ times, and order the basis so that equal eigenvalues are adjacent. Then

$$
[T]_{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}c_1I_{d_1}&&\\&\ddots&\\&&c_kI_{d_k}\end{pmatrix},\qquad d_1+\cdots+d_k=n,
$$

where $I_d$ is the $d\times d$ identity. Two facts follow at once. First, $c_T(x)=(x-c_1)^{d_1}\cdots(x-c_k)^{d_k}$. Second, $[T-c_iI]_{\mathcal{B}}$ is diagonal with exactly $d_i$ zero entries, so $\dim E_{c_i}=d_i$. For a diagonalizable operator, the characteristic polynomial therefore already tells the dimensions of the eigenspaces.

## 2. Polynomials act on eigenvectors by evaluation [11:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5GnTjNp0C4c&t=669s)

**Lemma 2.1.** If $T\alpha=c\alpha$ and $f\in F[x]$, then $f(T)\alpha=f(c)\alpha$.

**Proof.** First $T^m\alpha=c^m\alpha$ for every $m\ge0$: it holds for $m=0$, and $T^{m+1}\alpha=T(c^m\alpha)=c^m(T\alpha)=c^{m+1}\alpha$. Then for $f=\sum a_mx^m$, linearity gives $f(T)\alpha=\sum a_mT^m\alpha=\sum a_mc^m\alpha=f(c)\alpha$. $\square$

For example, with $f=7-2x+5x^3+x^4$ we have $f(1)=11$ and $f(2)=59$. So $f(A)$ multiplies the eigenvector $(3,-1,3)$ of $A$ for $1$ by $11$, and the eigenvectors $(2,1,0)$, $(2,0,1)$ for $2$ by $59$.

## 3. Eigenspaces of distinct eigenvalues are independent [15:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5GnTjNp0C4c&t=917s)

**Lemma 3.1.** Let $c_1,\dots,c_k$ be distinct eigenvalues with eigenspaces $W_i=E_{c_i}$, and suppose $\beta_1+\cdots+\beta_k=0$ with $\beta_i\in W_i$. Then every $\beta_i=0$.

**Proof.** Consider the *Lagrange polynomials*

$$
f_i(x)=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{x-c_j}{c_i-c_j},
$$

which satisfy $f_i(c_j)=1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise. Apply $f_i(T)$ to the relation. By Lemma 2.1, $f_i(T)\beta_j=f_i(c_j)\beta_j$, so only the term $j=i$ survives and $\beta_i=0$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.2.** If each $W_i$ is finite-dimensional with basis $\mathcal{B}_i$, then the list $(\mathcal{B}_1,\dots,\mathcal{B}_k)$ is a basis of $W=W_1+\cdots+W_k$, and $\dim W=\sum\dim W_i$.

**Proof.** The list spans $W$. A relation among its members can be grouped as $\beta_1+\cdots+\beta_k=0$ with $\beta_i$ a combination of $\mathcal{B}_i$. Lemma 3.1 gives $\beta_i=0$, and the independence of $\mathcal{B}_i$ gives that all its coefficients vanish. $\square$

The corollary rules out any hidden relation between eigenvectors of different eigenvalues, so dimensions simply add.

## 4. Diagonalizable means that the eigenspaces fill $V$ [30:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5GnTjNp0C4c&t=1805s)

**Theorem 4.1.** Let $\dim V=n$ and let $c_1,\dots,c_k$ be the distinct eigenvalues of $T$ in $F$. The following are equivalent: (a) $T$ is diagonalizable; (b) $V=E_{c_1}+\cdots+E_{c_k}$; (c) $\sum\dim E_{c_i}=n$.

**Proof.** (a) implies (b) because a basis of eigenvectors lies in the union of the eigenspaces. (b) and (c) are equivalent by Corollary 3.2: the sum has dimension $\sum\dim E_{c_i}$, so it is all of $V$ exactly when this number is $n$. (c) implies (a): the concatenated bases form a basis of $V$ made of eigenvectors. $\square$

For $A$, the eigenspace $E_1$ is spanned by $(3,-1,3)$ and $E_2$ by $(2,1,0)$, $(2,0,1)$; dimensions $1+2=3$. With $P$ having these three columns, $P^{-1}AP=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,2)$, equivalently $A=P\operatorname{diag}(1,2,2)P^{-1}$. For $B$, $E_1$ is spanned by $(0,0,1)$ and $E_2$ by $(1,0,0)$ only; $1+1\lt3$, so $B$ is not diagonalizable.

## 5. Annihilating polynomials [00:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6wLBCQ12WvE&t=9s)

A polynomial $f$ *annihilates* $T$ if $f(T)=0$. Let $J=\{f\in F[x]:f(T)=0\}$. The set $J$ is an *ideal* of $F[x]$: it contains $0$, is closed under sums, and $f\in J$ implies $gf\in J$ for every $g\in F[x]$, since $(gf)(T)=g(T)f(T)=0$.

**Theorem 5.1.** If $\dim V=n$, then $J$ contains a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $n^2$.

**Proof.** The operators on $V$ form a space of dimension $n^2$ ([Chapter 8](ch08.md)). So the $n^2+1$ operators $I,T,T^2,\dots,T^{n^2}$ are linearly dependent: there are scalars, not all zero, with $\sum_{i=0}^{n^2}c_iT^i=0$. The polynomial $\sum c_ix^i$ is nonzero and lies in $J$. $\square$

**Infinite dimension.** On $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ let $D$ be differentiation. No nonzero polynomial annihilates $D$. If $p=\sum p_ix^i$ has lowest nonzero coefficient $p_j$, then $p(D)x^j=p_j\,j!\neq0$: the terms with $i<j$ have $p_i=0$, and $D^ix^j=0$ for $i>j$. So $J=0$ here.

## 6. The minimal polynomial [10:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6wLBCQ12WvE&t=640s)

We use the division algorithm: for $g$ and $h\neq0$ in $F[x]$ there are unique $q,r$ with $g=qh+r$ and $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg h$.

**Theorem 6.1.** Let $\dim V$ be finite. There is a unique monic polynomial $m_T$ (leading coefficient $1$) of least degree with $m_T(T)=0$. A polynomial $g$ satisfies $g(T)=0$ if and only if $m_T$ divides $g$.

**Proof.** By Theorem 5.1, $J\neq0$. Choose a nonzero element of $J$ of least degree and divide it by its leading coefficient; call it $m$. If $g\in J$, write $g=qm+r$. Then $r=g-qm\in J$, and if $r\neq0$ it has degree less than $\deg m$, contradicting the choice. So $r=0$ and $m$ divides $g$. Conversely multiples of $m$ lie in $J$. If $m'$ is another monic element of least degree, then $m$ divides $m'$ and the degrees agree, so $m=m'$. $\square$

$m_T$ is the *minimal polynomial* of $T$. For a matrix $M$ it is defined the same way with $M$ in place of $T$, and $m_T=m_{[T]_{\mathcal{B}}}$ for every basis, because $p\mapsto p(T)$ corresponds to $p\mapsto p([T]_{\mathcal{B}})$.

In practice $m_M$ is found by testing the powers $I,M,M^2,\dots$ for the first linear dependency; each test is a linear system. For $A$ the first dependency is among $I,A,A^2$: $m_A=x^2-3x+2=(x-1)(x-2)$. For $B$ it first occurs among $I,B,B^2,B^3$: $m_B=(x-1)(x-2)^2$.

**Similar matrices have the same minimal polynomial** [17:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6wLBCQ12WvE&t=1032s). If $M'=P^{-1}MP$, then $M'^r=P^{-1}M^rP$, so $f(M')=P^{-1}f(M)P$ for every $f$. Hence $f(M')=0$ exactly when $f(M)=0$. The two matrices have the same ideal of annihilators and the same monic generator.

## 7. Roots of the minimal polynomial are the eigenvalues [21:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6wLBCQ12WvE&t=1286s)

**Theorem 7.1.** Let $\dim V$ be finite and $c\in F$. Then $m_T(c)=0$ if and only if $c$ is an eigenvalue of $T$.

**Proof.** Suppose $m_T(c)=0$. Then $m_T(x)=(x-c)q(x)$ with $q$ of smaller degree. By minimality $q(T)\neq0$: choose $\beta$ with $\alpha=q(T)\beta\neq0$. Then $(T-cI)\alpha=m_T(T)\beta=0$, so $\alpha$ is an eigenvector for $c$. Conversely, if $T\alpha=c\alpha$ with $\alpha\neq0$, then by Lemma 2.1, $0=m_T(T)\alpha=m_T(c)\alpha$, so $m_T(c)=0$. $\square$

So $m_T$ and $c_T$ have the same roots in $F$. Their multiplicities may differ: for $A$ the root $2$ has multiplicity $2$ in $c_A$ and $1$ in $m_A$; for $B$ it has multiplicity $2$ in both. The sharper statement that $m_T$ divides $c_T$ is the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, proved in [Chapter 14](ch14.md); it is not used in the present chapter.

## 8. The criterion for diagonalizability [29:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6wLBCQ12WvE&t=1798s)

**Theorem 8.1.** Let $\dim V=n\ge1$. Then $T$ is diagonalizable if and only if $m_T=(x-c_1)\cdots(x-c_k)$ with distinct $c_1,\dots,c_k\in F$.

**Proof.** Let $T$ be diagonalizable with distinct eigenvalues $c_1,\dots,c_k$, and put $p=\prod(x-c_i)$. Each $\alpha\in V$ is a sum $\beta_1+\cdots+\beta_k$ with $\beta_i\in E_{c_i}$ (Theorem 4.1). By Lemma 2.1, $p(T)\beta_i=p(c_i)\beta_i=0$. So $p(T)=0$ and $m_T$ divides $p$. By Theorem 7.1 each $c_i$ is a root of $m_T$, so $p$ divides $m_T$. Both are monic, hence $m_T=p$.

Conversely, let $m_T=\prod(x-c_i)$ with distinct $c_i$, and let $f_i$ be the Lagrange polynomials of Section 3. The polynomial $\sum f_i-1$ has degree at most $k-1$ and vanishes at the $k$ points $c_j$, so it is zero: $\sum_if_i=1$. Hence $\alpha=\sum_if_i(T)\alpha$ for every $\alpha\in V$. Moreover $(x-c_i)f_i$ is a nonzero constant multiple of $m_T$, so $(T-c_iI)f_i(T)=0$. Thus $f_i(T)\alpha\in E_{c_i}$. Therefore $V=E_{c_1}+\cdots+E_{c_k}$, and $T$ is diagonalizable by Theorem 4.1. $\square$

The hinge of the converse is the identity $\sum f_i=1$, which splits every vector into pieces that automatically lie in eigenspaces. For $A$ these are the operators $f_1(A)=2I-A$ and $f_2(A)=A-I$. They satisfy $f_1(A)+f_2(A)=I$, $f_i(A)^2=f_i(A)$, $f_1(A)f_2(A)=0$, and their ranks are $1$ and $2$, the dimensions of the eigenspaces.

**A consequence for powers.** Since $A^2=3A-2I$, every power of $A$ is a combination of $I$ and $A$. Explicitly $A^k=(2^k-1)A+(2-2^k)I$, which follows from Lemma 2.1 applied to the eigenvectors: both sides multiply the eigenvectors for $1$ by $1$ and those for $2$ by $2^k$.

**Field dependence.** The rotation $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has $m_R=x^2+1$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ this is not a product of linear factors, so $R$ is not diagonalizable. Over $\mathbb{C}$, $m_R=(x-i)(x+i)$ has distinct roots, so it is.

## Exercises

1. (Computation.) Find the minimal polynomials of $\operatorname{diag}(3,3,5)$ and of $\begin{pmatrix}3&1&0\\0&3&0\\0&0&5\end{pmatrix}$.
2. (Inverse.) Use $m_A$ to write $A^{-1}$ as a polynomial in $A$.
3. (Idempotents.) Show that an operator with $E^2=E$ on a finite-dimensional space is diagonalizable over every field.
4. (Field dependence.) Let $S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$. Find $m_S$ and decide diagonalizability over $\mathbb{R}$ and over $\mathbb{C}$.
5. (Restriction.) Let $T$ be diagonalizable on a finite-dimensional space and $W$ a $T$-invariant subspace. Show that the restriction of $T$ to $W$ is diagonalizable.
6. (Block sum.) Find the minimal polynomial of the $6\times6$ block diagonal matrix with blocks $A$ and $B$.

1. The first matrix $D$ has eigenvalues $3,5$ and $(D-3I)(D-5I)=0$, while neither factor is zero; so $m_D=(x-3)(x-5)$. For the second matrix $M$, $m_M$ has the roots $3$ and $5$ (Theorem 7.1). The product $(M-3I)(M-5I)$ is not zero: its $(1,2)$ entry comes from the block $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}$, which has $(1,2)$ entry $-2$. On the other hand $(M-3I)^2(M-5I)=0$, since $(M-3I)^2$ vanishes on the first two coordinates and $M-5I$ on the third. So $m_M=(x-3)^2(x-5)$.

2. From $m_A=x^2-3x+2$, $A^2-3A+2I=0$, so $A(3I-A)=2I$. Hence $A^{-1}=\frac12(3I-A)$.

3. $E$ is annihilated by $x^2-x=x(x-1)$, so $m_E$ divides it. Every monic divisor of $x(x-1)$ other than $1$ is a product of distinct linear factors, and $m_E=1$ happens only for $V=0$. Since $0\neq1$ in every field, Theorem 8.1 applies.

4. $S^2=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $S^2+S+I=0$. Since $S$ is not a scalar matrix, $m_S$ has degree $2$: $m_S=x^2+x+1$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ its discriminant is $1-4=-3<0$, so it has no roots and $S$ is not diagonalizable. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it has the two distinct roots $(-1\pm i\sqrt3)/2$, so $S$ is diagonalizable.

5. Let $T_W$ be the restriction and $m=m_T$. For $w\in W$, $m(T_W)w=m(T)w=0$, so $m(T_W)=0$ and $m_{T_W}$ divides $m$. By Theorem 8.1, $m$ is a product of distinct linear factors, so every monic divisor of it is too. Again by Theorem 8.1 (applied on $W$, if $W\neq0$; the zero space is trivially diagonalizable), $T_W$ is diagonalizable.

6. For a block diagonal $M$ with blocks $A,B$, $p(M)$ has blocks $p(A),p(B)$. So $p(M)=0$ exactly when $m_A$ and $m_B$ both divide $p$, that is, when their least common multiple divides $p$. With $m_A=(x-1)(x-2)$ and $m_B=(x-1)(x-2)^2$, the least common multiple is $(x-1)(x-2)^2$.
