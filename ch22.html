22. Orthogonal projection, Gram-Schmidt, and best approximation

In $\mathbb{R}^3$ let $W$ be the plane spanned by $u_1=(1,1,0)$ and $u_2=(1,0,1)$, and let $\beta=(1,2,4)$. The vector $\beta$ does not lie in $W$. Which vector of $W$ is closest to it, and how far away is it? This is the problem behind curve fitting: the vectors of $W$ are the data a model can produce, and $\beta$ is the data observed. The answer turns out to be $\alpha=(8/3,\,1/3,\,7/3)$, at distance $5/\sqrt3$. It is found by a right angle: $\beta-\alpha$ is perpendicular to the whole plane. This chapter proves that this principle holds in every inner product space, says when a closest vector exists, and builds from it the orthogonal projection.

This plane and vector are the running example (an original construction), with the standard inner product of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Conventions. $V$ is an inner product space over $F=\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, with $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ linear in the first argument and conjugate-linear in the second, and $\|\alpha\|^2=\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle$. Vectors $\alpha,\beta$ are orthogonal if $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=0$. A set is orthogonal if its members are pairwise orthogonal (Chapter 21). The dimension of $V$ is arbitrary unless stated. We use the expansion

$$\|a+b\|^2=\|a\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle a,b\rangle+\|b\|^2 .$$

1. Best approximation 00:25

Definition 1.1. Let $W$ be a subspace of $V$ and $\beta\in V$. A vector $\alpha\in W$ is a best approximation to $\beta$ by vectors of $W$ if

$$\|\beta-\alpha\|\le\|\beta-\gamma\|\qquad\text{for all }\gamma\in W.$$

The definition asks for a minimum, and a minimum need not exist; Section 8 gives an example. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ with $W$ the $xy$-plane 03:39, the best approximation to $(x,y,z)$ is its foot $(x,y,0)$, and the difference $(0,0,z)$ is perpendicular to the plane.

2. The characterization theorem 10:52

Theorem 2.1. Let $W$ be a subspace of $V$ and $\beta\in V$.

  1. A vector $\alpha\in W$ is a best approximation to $\beta$ if and only if $\beta-\alpha$ is orthogonal to every vector of $W$.
  2. A best approximation, if one exists, is unique.
  3. If $W$ is finite-dimensional with orthogonal basis $(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$, a best approximation exists and is $$\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{\langle\beta,\alpha_k\rangle}{\|\alpha_k\|^2}\,\alpha_k .$$

Proof. Let $\alpha\in W$ and $\gamma\in W$, and put $r=\alpha-\gamma\in W$. Then

$$\|\beta-\gamma\|^2=\|(\beta-\alpha)+r\|^2=\|\beta-\alpha\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle\beta-\alpha,r\rangle+\|r\|^2 .\tag{2.1}$$

As $\gamma$ runs through $W$, the vector $r$ runs through all of $W$.

1, sufficiency. If $\beta-\alpha$ is orthogonal to $W$, the middle term vanishes, so $\|\beta-\gamma\|^2=\|\beta-\alpha\|^2+\|r\|^2\ge\|\beta-\alpha\|^2$.

1, necessity. If $\alpha$ is a best approximation, (2.1) gives $2\operatorname{Re}\langle\beta-\alpha,r\rangle+\|r\|^2\ge0$ for every $r\in W$. Let $s\in W$ be nonzero and choose $$r=-\frac{\langle\beta-\alpha,s\rangle}{\|s\|^2}\,s .$$ Then $\langle\beta-\alpha,r\rangle=-|\langle\beta-\alpha,s\rangle|^2/\|s\|^2$, which is real, and $\|r\|^2=|\langle\beta-\alpha,s\rangle|^2/\|s\|^2$. The inequality becomes $-|\langle\beta-\alpha,s\rangle|^2/\|s\|^2\ge0$. So $\langle\beta-\alpha,s\rangle=0$.

  1. Let $\alpha,\alpha'$ be best approximations. By part 1, $\beta-\alpha$ and $\beta-\alpha'$ are orthogonal to $W$, so their difference $\alpha'-\alpha$ is too. This difference lies in $W$, so it is orthogonal to itself and hence zero.

  2. For each $j$, orthogonality of the basis gives $\langle\alpha,\alpha_j\rangle=\langle\beta,\alpha_j\rangle$. So $\beta-\alpha$ is orthogonal to every $\alpha_j$, hence to $W$. By part 1, $\alpha$ is a best approximation. $\square$

The hinge of necessity is the choice of $r$: it moves from $\alpha$ a short step in the direction that would reduce the distance if $\beta-\alpha$ had any component along $s$. Choosing the complex coefficient, not only a real one, makes the argument work over $\mathbb{C}$.

On the running example. Gram–Schmidt on $(u_1,u_2)$ gives the orthogonal basis $a_1=(1,1,0)$ and $a_2=u_2-\tfrac12a_1=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,1)$, with $\|a_1\|^2=2$ and $\|a_2\|^2=\tfrac32$. Then $\langle\beta,a_1\rangle=3$ and $\langle\beta,a_2\rangle=\tfrac72$, so the coefficients are $\tfrac32$ and $\tfrac73$, and

$$\alpha=\tfrac32a_1+\tfrac73a_2=\Big(\tfrac83,\tfrac13,\tfrac73\Big),\qquad\beta-\alpha=\Big(-\tfrac53,\tfrac53,\tfrac53\Big).$$

The difference is orthogonal to $u_1$ and $u_2$, and $\|\beta-\alpha\|^2=\tfrac{25}{3}$.

A line in $\mathbb{R}^3$ 35:53. Take $\beta=(-10,2,8)$ and $W$ spanned by $w=(3,12,-1)$. Then $\langle\beta,w\rangle=-30+24-8=-14$ and $\|w\|^2=154$. The best approximation is

$$\alpha=\frac{-14}{154}\,w=-\frac1{11}(3,12,-1).$$

3. Gram–Schmidt as repeated projection, and the normal equations

The Gram–Schmidt formula of Chapter 21,

$$\alpha_{m+1}=\beta_{m+1}-\sum_{k=1}^m\frac{\langle\beta_{m+1},\alpha_k\rangle}{\|\alpha_k\|^2}\,\alpha_k,$$

subtracts from $\beta_{m+1}$ its best approximation in $W_m=\operatorname{span}(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_m)$. Theorem 2.1 explains why the result is orthogonal to $W_m$. Gram–Schmidt also gives every finite-dimensional subspace an orthogonal basis, so part 3 applies to every finite-dimensional $W$.

An orthogonal basis is not necessary for computation. Let $(w_1,\dots,w_n)$ be any basis of $W$ and write $\alpha=\sum_kx_kw_k$. The condition $\beta-\alpha\perp w_j$ for each $j$ reads

$$\sum_{k=1}^n\langle w_k,w_j\rangle\,x_k=\langle\beta,w_j\rangle\qquad(1\le j\le n).$$

These are the normal equations. Their matrix is the Gram matrix of the basis, which is invertible (Chapter 21). For the running example in the basis $(u_1,u_2)$ they are

$$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\5\end{pmatrix},$$

with solution $x=(\tfrac13,\tfrac73)$, and $\tfrac13u_1+\tfrac73u_2=\alpha$.

4. The orthogonal complement 00:09

Definition 4.1. For a subset $S\subseteq V$, the orthogonal complement $S^{\perp}$ is the set of vectors orthogonal to every member of $S$.

Proposition 4.2. $S^{\perp}$ is a subspace, and $W\cap W^{\perp}=0$ for every subspace $W$.

Proof. The zero vector lies in $S^{\perp}$. If $\alpha,\beta\in S^{\perp}$, $c\in F$ and $\gamma\in S$, then $\langle c\alpha+\beta,\gamma\rangle=c\langle\alpha,\gamma\rangle+\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle=0$. A vector in $W\cap W^{\perp}$ is orthogonal to itself, hence zero. $\square$

For the running example, $x\perp u_1,u_2$ means $x_1+x_2=0$ and $x_1+x_3=0$, so $W^{\perp}$ is the line spanned by $n=(1,-1,-1)$. Indeed $\beta-\alpha=-\tfrac53n$.

5. The orthogonal projection 08:12

Definition 5.1. If $\beta$ has a best approximation in $W$, it is the orthogonal projection of $\beta$ on $W$. If every $\beta\in V$ has one, the map $E:V\to V$ sending $\beta$ to it is the orthogonal projection of $V$ on $W$.

By Theorem 2.1(3), $E$ exists whenever $W$ is finite-dimensional. Its value is characterized by two properties: $E\beta\in W$ and $\beta-E\beta\in W^{\perp}$.

Theorem 5.2 23:11. Suppose the orthogonal projection $E$ of $V$ on $W$ exists. Then

  1. $E$ is linear, $E^2=E$, and $\operatorname{im}E=W$;
  2. $\ker E=W^{\perp}$;
  3. $V=W\oplus W^{\perp}$;
  4. $I-E$ is the orthogonal projection of $V$ on $W^{\perp}$ 12:08.

Proof. 1: For $\beta,\gamma\in V$ and $c\in F$, the vector $cE\beta+E\gamma$ lies in $W$, and $(c\beta+\gamma)-(cE\beta+E\gamma)=c(\beta-E\beta)+(\gamma-E\gamma)$ lies in $W^{\perp}$. By Theorem 2.1, $cE\beta+E\gamma=E(c\beta+\gamma)$. If $\beta\in W$, then $\beta$ itself is the best approximation, so $E\beta=\beta$. Hence $\operatorname{im}E=W$ and $E^2=E$.

2: $E\beta=0$ holds exactly when $0$ is the best approximation, that is, when $\beta-0\in W^{\perp}$.

3: Every $\beta$ equals $E\beta+(\beta-E\beta)$ with the first term in $W$ and the second in $W^{\perp}$. The sum is direct by Proposition 4.2.

4: Let $\beta\in V$. The vector $(I-E)\beta$ lies in $W^{\perp}$. The difference $\beta-(I-E)\beta=E\beta$ lies in $W$, so it is orthogonal to every vector of $W^{\perp}$. By Theorem 2.1(1), applied to the subspace $W^{\perp}$, $(I-E)\beta$ is its best approximation. $\square$

The hinge of linearity is uniqueness: it is enough to guess $cE\beta+E\gamma$ and check the two characterizing properties.

On the running example. Applying the formula to $e_1,e_2,e_3$ gives

$$E=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\1&2&-1\\1&-1&2\end{pmatrix}.$$

One checks $E^2=E$, $E\beta=\alpha$, $En=0$ and $\operatorname{tr}E=2=\dim W$. The matrix is symmetric, which reflects orthogonality (Exercise 3).

Not every projection is orthogonal. $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ satisfies $P^2=P$ and has range the first axis $L$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$. For $\beta=(0,1)$, $P\beta=(1,0)$ at squared distance $2$, but the best approximation in $L$ is $0$ at squared distance $1$. The kernel of $P$ is spanned by $(1,-1)$, which is not perpendicular to $L$.

6. Bessel's inequality 30:40

Theorem 6.1. Let $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ be nonzero orthogonal vectors of $V$ and $\beta\in V$. Then

$$\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{|\langle\beta,\alpha_k\rangle|^2}{\|\alpha_k\|^2}\le\|\beta\|^2,$$

with equality exactly when $\beta\in\operatorname{span}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$.

Proof. Let $\alpha$ be the orthogonal projection of $\beta$ on the span. Since $\alpha\perp\beta-\alpha$, Pythagoras gives $\|\beta\|^2=\|\alpha\|^2+\|\beta-\alpha\|^2$. Expanding $\alpha$ by Theorem 2.1(3) and using orthogonality, $\|\alpha\|^2$ equals the sum on the left. So the inequality holds, and equality means $\beta=\alpha$. $\square$

On the running example. With the orthogonal basis $a_1,a_2,n$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$, the partial sums are $\tfrac92$, $\tfrac{38}{3}$ and $21$, and $\|\beta\|^2=21$. The last term closes the gap, because $(a_1,a_2,n)$ spans the space. When $\dim V=n$ and the $\alpha_k$ form a basis, equality holds for every $\beta$. Equality for one particular $\beta$ only says that $\beta$ lies in the span.

7. What the projection answers

The distance from $\beta$ to the subspace $W$ is $\|\beta-E\beta\|$. For the running example it is $\sqrt{25/3}=5/\sqrt3\approx2.886751$. For every other $\gamma\in W$,

$$\|\beta-\gamma\|^2=\tfrac{25}{3}+\|\alpha-\gamma\|^2,$$

which is (2.1) with the middle term gone.

8. Infinite dimension: no closest vector 07:12

Theorem 2.1(1) and (2) hold in every dimension, but existence can fail. Let $V$ be the real space of sequences with finitely many nonzero entries, with $\langle x,y\rangle=\sum x_ky_k$. Let $W=\{x\in V:\sum_kx_k/k=0\}$. This is a proper subspace, since $e_1\notin W$.

$W^{\perp}=0$. For all $j,k$ the vector $je_j-ke_k$ lies in $W$. So $v\in W^{\perp}$ satisfies $jv_j=kv_k$. Since $v_k=0$ for large $k$, every $v_j=0$.

So $e_1$ has no best approximation. If $\alpha$ were one, then $e_1-\alpha\in W^{\perp}=0$, and $e_1$ would lie in $W$.

The infimum of the distances still exists. For $s_n=\sum_{k\le n}1/k^2$ the vector $w_n=e_1-\frac1{s_n}\sum_{k\le n}e_k/k$ lies in $W$, with $\|e_1-w_n\|=1/\sqrt{s_n}$. These numbers decrease to $\sqrt6/\pi\approx0.779697$. Any $w\in W$ supported in the first $n$ places satisfies $\sum_{k\le n}(e_1-w)_k/k=1$, so Cauchy–Schwarz gives $\|e_1-w\|\ge1/\sqrt{s_n}>\sqrt6/\pi$. The infimum is never attained. In this example $V\ne W\oplus W^{\perp}$ and $(W^{\perp})^{\perp}=V\ne W$. Existence is restored when the space is complete and the subspace $W$ is closed; both notions belong to functional analysis and are not developed here.

Exercises

  1. (Computation.) For the running example find the best approximation to $e_1$ in $W$ and its distance.
  2. (Least squares.) Find the line $y=x_0+x_1t$ that minimizes the sum of squared errors at the points $(0,1)$, $(1,3)$, $(2,4)$.
  3. (Characterization.) Let $E$ be an operator with $E^2=E$. Show that $E$ is the orthogonal projection on its range if and only if $\langle E\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,E\beta\rangle$ for all $\alpha,\beta$.
  4. (Double complement.) Show that $(W^{\perp})^{\perp}=W$ when $W$ is finite-dimensional, and say where finiteness is used.
  5. (Complex case.) Find the best approximation to $(1,0)$ in the line spanned by $(1,i)$ in $\mathbb{C}^2$ with the standard inner product, and verify orthogonality.
  6. (Contraction.) Let $E$ be an orthogonal projection. Show that $\|E\beta\|\le\|\beta\|$ with equality exactly when $E\beta=\beta$, and show that $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ violates the inequality.
Fully worked solutions

  1. $Ee_1$ is the first column of $E$: $\tfrac13(2,1,1)$. The difference $e_1-Ee_1=\tfrac13(1,-1,-1)=\tfrac13n$ lies in $W^{\perp}$. The distance is $\|n\|/3=\sqrt3/3$.

  2. The model values are $x_0(1,1,1)+x_1(0,1,2)$, and the data are $\beta=(1,3,4)$. The normal equations are $3x_0+3x_1=8$ and $3x_0+5x_1=11$. So $x_1=\tfrac32$ and $x_0=\tfrac76$. The residual $\beta-\alpha=(-\tfrac16,\tfrac13,-\tfrac16)$ is orthogonal to $(1,1,1)$ and to $(0,1,2)$.

  3. Let $E$ be the orthogonal projection on $R=\operatorname{im}E$. Then $\langle E\alpha,\beta-E\beta\rangle=0$ and $\langle\alpha-E\alpha,E\beta\rangle=0$, so both $\langle E\alpha,\beta\rangle$ and $\langle\alpha,E\beta\rangle$ equal $\langle E\alpha,E\beta\rangle$. Conversely, assume the identity. For $\beta\in V$ and $\gamma=E\delta\in R$, $\langle\beta-E\beta,E\delta\rangle=\langle E(\beta-E\beta),\delta\rangle=\langle E\beta-E^2\beta,\delta\rangle=0$. So $\beta-E\beta\in R^{\perp}$, and $E\beta\in R$. By Theorem 2.1, $E\beta$ is the best approximation in $R$.

  4. Every $w\in W$ is orthogonal to every member of $W^{\perp}$, so $W\subseteq(W^{\perp})^{\perp}$. Let $v\in(W^{\perp})^{\perp}$. Since $W$ is finite-dimensional, $E$ exists and $v=Ev+(v-Ev)$ with $v-Ev\in W^{\perp}$. Then $0=\langle v,v-Ev\rangle=\langle Ev,v-Ev\rangle+\|v-Ev\|^2=\|v-Ev\|^2$. So $v=Ev\in W$. Finiteness is used to obtain $E$; Section 8 shows that the statement fails without it.

  5. Here $w=(1,i)$, $\|w\|^2=2$ and $\langle(1,0),w\rangle=1\cdot\overline1+0=1$. So $\alpha=\tfrac12w=(\tfrac12,\tfrac i2)$. The difference is $(\tfrac12,-\tfrac i2)$, and $\langle(\tfrac12,-\tfrac i2),w\rangle=\tfrac12+(-\tfrac i2)(-i)=\tfrac12-\tfrac12=0$.

  6. Since $E\beta\perp\beta-E\beta$, $\|\beta\|^2=\|E\beta\|^2+\|\beta-E\beta\|^2$. This gives the inequality, with equality exactly when $\beta-E\beta=0$. For $P$ and $\beta=(1,1)$, $P\beta=(2,0)$ has norm $2$, larger than $\sqrt2$.

"""Chapter 22 laboratory: best approximation and orthogonal projection.

Exact arithmetic over Q (fractions.Fraction) for the real inner product
<u, v> = sum u_i v_i on Q^n.  A vector is a list of Fractions.  The
floating-point parts (the infinite-dimensional example and one complex
check) use explicit tolerances.

What is checked: Gram-Schmidt, the coefficient formula, the normal
equations, the characterisation "beta - alpha is orthogonal to W", the
Pythagorean identity behind minimality, idempotence, kernel and direct
sum of the orthogonal projection, a non-orthogonal projection that is not
a best approximation, and Bessel's inequality.  What is not checked: any
statement about a general inner product space; the proofs are in the
chapter.
"""
import math
from fractions import Fraction as Fr

import numpy as np


def vec(*xs):
    return [Fr(x) for x in xs]


def dot(u, v):
    return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(u, v))


def add(u, v):
    return [a + b for a, b in zip(u, v)]


def sub(u, v):
    return [a - b for a, b in zip(u, v)]


def scale(c, u):
    return [c * a for a in u]


def norm2(u):
    return dot(u, u)


def matvec(m, v):
    return [dot(row, v) for row in m]


def gram_schmidt(vectors):
    """Orthogonal (not normalised) basis of span(vectors)."""
    out = []
    for v in vectors:
        w = list(v)
        for a in out:
            w = sub(w, scale(dot(v, a) / norm2(a), a))
        if all(x == 0 for x in w):
            raise ValueError('vectors are linearly dependent')
        out.append(w)
    return out


def coefficients(beta, ortho):
    return [dot(beta, a) / norm2(a) for a in ortho]


def project_orthogonal(beta, ortho):
    """Best approximation of beta in span(ortho); ortho is orthogonal."""
    alpha = [Fr(0)] * len(beta)
    for c, a in zip(coefficients(beta, ortho), ortho):
        alpha = add(alpha, scale(c, a))
    return alpha


def solve(m, b):
    """Solve m x = b exactly for a square invertible m."""
    n = len(m)
    aug = [list(row) + [b[i]] for i, row in enumerate(m)]
    for col in range(n):
        piv = next(r for r in range(col, n) if aug[r][col] != 0)
        aug[col], aug[piv] = aug[piv], aug[col]
        p = aug[col][col]
        aug[col] = [x / p for x in aug[col]]
        for r in range(n):
            if r != col and aug[r][col] != 0:
                f = aug[r][col]
                aug[r] = [x - f * y for x, y in zip(aug[r], aug[col])]
    return [aug[i][n] for i in range(n)]


def project_normal(beta, cols):
    """Best approximation via the normal equations A^T A x = A^T beta."""
    g = [[dot(a, b) for b in cols] for a in cols]
    rhs = [dot(a, beta) for a in cols]
    x = solve(g, rhs)
    alpha = [Fr(0)] * len(beta)
    for c, a in zip(x, cols):
        alpha = add(alpha, scale(c, a))
    return x, alpha


def nullspace(rows, n):
    """Basis of {x in Q^n : row . x = 0 for every row} by RREF."""
    m = [list(r) for r in rows]
    pivots = []
    r = 0
    for c in range(n):
        piv = next((i for i in range(r, len(m)) if m[i][c] != 0), None)
        if piv is None:
            continue
        m[r], m[piv] = m[piv], m[r]
        p = m[r][c]
        m[r] = [x / p for x in m[r]]
        for i in range(len(m)):
            if i != r and m[i][c] != 0:
                f = m[i][c]
                m[i] = [x - f * y for x, y in zip(m[i], m[r])]
        pivots.append(c)
        r += 1
        if r == len(m):
            break
    basis = []
    for free in (c for c in range(n) if c not in pivots):
        x = [Fr(0)] * n
        x[free] = Fr(1)
        for i, pc in enumerate(pivots):
            x[pc] = -m[i][free]
        basis.append(x)
    return basis


def projection_matrix(ortho, n):
    cols = []
    for j in range(n):
        e = [Fr(1) if i == j else Fr(0) for i in range(n)]
        cols.append(project_orthogonal(e, ortho))
    return [[cols[j][i] for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def show(v):
    return '(' + ', '.join(str(x) for x in v) + ')'


def running_example():
    u1, u2 = vec(1, 1, 0), vec(1, 0, 1)
    beta = vec(1, 2, 4)
    a1, a2 = gram_schmidt([u1, u2])
    assert a1 == vec(1, 1, 0) and a2 == vec(Fr(1, 2), Fr(-1, 2), 1)
    assert dot(a1, a2) == 0
    assert norm2(a1) == 2 and norm2(a2) == Fr(3, 2)
    c = coefficients(beta, [a1, a2])
    assert c == [Fr(3, 2), Fr(7, 3)]
    alpha = project_orthogonal(beta, [a1, a2])
    assert alpha == vec(Fr(8, 3), Fr(1, 3), Fr(7, 3))
    resid = sub(beta, alpha)
    assert resid == vec(Fr(-5, 3), Fr(5, 3), Fr(5, 3))
    # characterisation: beta - alpha is orthogonal to W
    assert dot(resid, u1) == 0 and dot(resid, u2) == 0
    print('alpha =', show(alpha), ' beta - alpha =', show(resid))
    print('|beta|^2 =', norm2(beta), ' |alpha|^2 =', norm2(alpha),
          ' |beta - alpha|^2 =', norm2(resid))
    assert norm2(beta) == norm2(alpha) + norm2(resid)
    print('distance^2 =', norm2(resid), ' distance =',
          round(math.sqrt(norm2(resid)), 6))

    # normal equations in the non-orthogonal basis (u1, u2)
    x, alpha2 = project_normal(beta, [u1, u2])
    assert x == [Fr(1, 3), Fr(7, 3)] and alpha2 == alpha
    print('normal-equation coordinates in (u1, u2):', show(x))

    # W-perp by null space, and the matrix of E
    perp = nullspace([u1, u2], 3)
    assert len(perp) == 1
    n = vec(1, -1, -1)
    assert perp[0] == scale(Fr(-1), n)   # a multiple of n
    e = projection_matrix([a1, a2], 3)
    third = Fr(1, 3)
    assert e == [vec(2 * third, third, third), vec(third, 2 * third, -third),
                 vec(third, -third, 2 * third)]
    assert matvec(e, beta) == alpha
    ee = [[dot(row, [r[j] for r in e]) for j in range(3)] for row in e]
    assert ee == e                                   # idempotent
    assert all(e[i][j] == e[j][i] for i in range(3) for j in range(3))
    assert matvec(e, n) == vec(0, 0, 0)              # kernel contains W-perp
    assert sum(e[i][i] for i in range(3)) == 2       # trace = dim W
    print('E = (1/3) *', [[int(3 * x) for x in row] for row in e])

    # Pythagorean identity: |beta - g|^2 = |beta - alpha|^2 + |alpha - g|^2
    count = 0
    for a in range(-4, 5):
        for b in range(-4, 5):
            g = add(scale(Fr(a), u1), scale(Fr(b), u2))
            lhs = norm2(sub(beta, g))
            rhs = norm2(resid) + norm2(sub(alpha, g))
            assert lhs == rhs and lhs >= norm2(resid)
            count += 1
    print('Pythagorean identity verified at', count, 'points of W')

    # Bessel: partial and full orthogonal sets
    s1 = dot(beta, a1) ** 2 / norm2(a1)
    s2 = s1 + dot(beta, a2) ** 2 / norm2(a2)
    s3 = s2 + dot(beta, n) ** 2 / norm2(n)
    assert (s1, s2, s3) == (Fr(9, 2), Fr(38, 3), Fr(21))
    assert s1 <= s2 <= norm2(beta) and s3 == norm2(beta)
    print('Bessel sums:', s1, s2, s3, ' |beta|^2 =', norm2(beta))
    # direct sum: beta = E beta + (beta - E beta), unique components
    assert add(alpha, resid) == beta
    assert dot(alpha, resid) == 0


def one_dimensional_example():
    beta, w = vec(-10, 2, 8), vec(3, 12, -1)
    assert norm2(w) == 154 and dot(beta, w) == -14
    alpha = project_orthogonal(beta, [w])
    assert alpha == scale(Fr(-1, 11), w)
    assert dot(sub(beta, alpha), w) == 0
    print('line example: coefficient', dot(beta, w) / norm2(w),
          ' alpha =', show(alpha))


def non_orthogonal_projection():
    p = [vec(1, 1), vec(0, 0)]
    pp = [[dot(row, [r[j] for r in p]) for j in range(2)] for row in p]
    assert pp == p                                   # idempotent
    assert p[0][1] != p[1][0]                        # not symmetric
    beta = vec(0, 1)
    pb = matvec(p, beta)
    best = project_orthogonal(beta, [vec(1, 0)])
    assert pb == vec(1, 0) and best == vec(0, 0)
    assert norm2(sub(beta, pb)) == 2 and norm2(sub(beta, best)) == 1
    assert dot(sub(beta, pb), vec(1, 0)) != 0
    print('non-orthogonal projection: |beta - P beta|^2 = 2 against',
          'best-approximation distance^2 = 1')


def gram_schmidt_four():
    vs = [vec(1, 1, 1, 1), vec(1, 2, 3, 4), vec(2, 0, 1, 5)]
    g = gram_schmidt(vs)
    for i in range(3):
        for j in range(i):
            assert dot(g[i], g[j]) == 0
    # each span(v_1..v_k) equals span(g_1..g_k): v_k - g_k in earlier span
    for k in range(3):
        diff = sub(vs[k], g[k])
        assert project_orthogonal(diff, g[:k]) == diff if k else diff == \
            vec(0, 0, 0, 0)
    try:
        gram_schmidt([vec(1, 2), vec(2, 4)])
        raise AssertionError('dependent input must be rejected')
    except ValueError:
        pass
    print('Gram-Schmidt in Q^4 gave', [show(x) for x in g])


def infinite_dimensional_failure():
    # V = finitely supported real sequences, W = kernel of
    # phi(x) = sum x_k / k.  Check j e_j - k e_k lies in W (exactly).
    for j in range(1, 7):
        for k in range(1, 7):
            assert j * Fr(1, j) - k * Fr(1, k) == 0
    s = 0.0
    seen = {}
    prev = None
    limit = math.sqrt(6) / math.pi
    for k in range(1, 10 ** 6 + 1):
        s += 1.0 / (k * k)
        if k in (1, 10, 100, 10 ** 4, 10 ** 6):
            dist = 1.0 / math.sqrt(s)
            seen[k] = dist
            assert dist > limit                      # never attained
            assert prev is None or dist < prev       # decreasing
            prev = dist
    assert abs(seen[10 ** 6] - limit) < 1e-5
    print('c00 example: sqrt(6)/pi =', round(limit, 6))
    for k, d in seen.items():
        print('   n =', k, ' |e1 - w_n| =', round(d, 6))


def complex_check():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(22)
    a = rng.standard_normal((5, 2)) + 1j * rng.standard_normal((5, 2))
    q, _ = np.linalg.qr(a)                  # orthonormal basis of W
    beta = rng.standard_normal(5) + 1j * rng.standard_normal(5)
    # <x, y> = y^* x, linear in the first slot
    coef = q.conj().T @ beta
    alpha = q @ coef
    assert np.allclose(q.conj().T @ (beta - alpha), 0, atol=1e-12)
    assert abs(np.vdot(beta, beta).real
               - np.vdot(alpha, alpha).real
               - np.vdot(beta - alpha, beta - alpha).real) < 1e-12
    assert np.sum(np.abs(coef) ** 2) <= np.vdot(beta, beta).real + 1e-12
    for _ in range(200):
        gam = q @ (rng.standard_normal(2) + 1j * rng.standard_normal(2))
        assert np.linalg.norm(beta - gam) >= np.linalg.norm(beta - alpha)
    print('complex check passed: residual orthogonal, Pythagoras holds')
