Two rational matrices serve as the running examples of this chapter:

$$ A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1&-1\\2&2&-1\\2&2&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}5&-6&-6\\-1&4&2\\3&-6&-4\end{pmatrix}. $$

Suppose each describes one step of a system whose state is a vector in $\mathbb{Q}^3$, so that after $k$ steps the state is $A^kv$ or $B^kv$. How fast do the states grow? Computing the powers for $k=10$ and $k=20$ gives a surprise. For both matrices the entries grow roughly like $2^k$, but $\max|(B^k)_{ij}|/2^k$ stays near $6$, whereas $\max|(A^k)_{ij}|/2^k$ is about $18$ at $k=10$ and $38$ at $k=20$. It grows with $k$. The two matrices have the same trace $5$, the same determinant $4$ and even the same characteristic polynomial. Something finer separates them. The tool that finds it is the study of vectors that a map only rescales.

Throughout, $F$ is a field and $V$ is a vector space over $F$; finite dimension is stated where it is used. An operator on $V$ is a linear map $V\to V$, and $I$ is the identity. Determinants of square matrices with entries in a commutative ring are used with their standard properties: $\det(PQ)=\det P\det Q$, a matrix over $F$ is invertible exactly when its determinant is nonzero, and the Leibniz formula $\det M=\sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_iM_{i\sigma(i)}$, summed over the permutations $\sigma$ of $\{1,\dots,n\}$, where $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=\pm1$ is the sign of the permutation.

1. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors 00:26

Definition 1.1. Let $T$ be an operator on $V$. A scalar $c\in F$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ if there is a vector $\alpha\neq0$ with $T\alpha=c\alpha$. Every such $\alpha$ is an eigenvector of $T$ associated with $c$. For a matrix $M\in F^{n\times n}$, eigenvalues and eigenvectors are those of the operator $X\mapsto MX$ on $F^n$.

The condition $\alpha\neq0$ is essential: $T0=c0$ holds for every $c$. The eigenvalue $0$ is allowed; it means that $\ker T\neq0$. The equation $T\alpha=c\alpha$ says $(T-cI)\alpha=0$. So $c$ is an eigenvalue exactly when $T-cI$ has a nonzero null space, that is, when $T-cI$ is not injective. The terms characteristic value and characteristic vector are also used.

Definition 1.2 13:24. The eigenspace of $c$ is $E_c=\ker(T-cI)=\{\alpha:T\alpha=c\alpha\}$.

$E_c$ is a subspace, being the null space of a linear map. Its nonzero vectors are the eigenvectors of $c$; $0$ belongs to $E_c$ although it is not an eigenvector. The dimension of $E_c$ is the geometric multiplicity of $c$.

2. The characteristic polynomial 11:24

Let $\dim V=n$ and let $M$ be the matrix of $T$ in some ordered basis. Then $c$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ exactly when $M-cI$ is not invertible, that is, when $\det(M-cI)=0$. This suggests a polynomial.

Definition 2.1. The characteristic polynomial of $M\in F^{n\times n}$ is $c_M(x)=\det(xI-M)$, a determinant of a matrix with polynomial entries.

Some texts use $\det(M-xI)=(-1)^nc_M(x)$; the roots are the same. The sign convention $xI-M$ makes $c_M$ monic, with leading coefficient $1$.

Theorem 2.2. $c_M$ is a monic polynomial of degree $n$. The coefficient of $x^{n-1}$ is $-\operatorname{tr}M$, where $\operatorname{tr}M=\sum_iM_{ii}$ is the trace, and the constant term is $(-1)^n\det M$.

Proof. In the Leibniz formula for $\det(xI-M)$, the identity permutation contributes $\prod_{i}(x-M_{ii})=x^n-\big(\sum_iM_{ii}\big)x^{n-1}+\cdots$. A permutation other than the identity moves at least two indices, so its product contains at most $n-2$ diagonal factors and has degree at most $n-2$. Hence the two leading coefficients come from the identity alone. The constant term is $c_M(0)=\det(-M)=(-1)^n\det M$. $\square$

Theorem 2.3. For $c\in F$, the following are equivalent: (a) $c$ is an eigenvalue of $M$; (b) $M-cI$ is not invertible; (c) $c_M(c)=0$.

Proof. (a) says that $(M-cI)X=0$ has a nonzero solution, which for a square matrix means that $M-cI$ is not invertible (Chapter 2). This is (b). And $M-cI$ is not invertible exactly when $\det(cI-M)=(-1)^n\det(M-cI)=0$, which is (c). $\square$

So the eigenvalues of $M$ in $F$ are the roots in $F$ of $c_M$. A polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots, so an operator on an $n$-dimensional space has at most $n$ eigenvalues. The equation $c_M(x)=0$ is the characteristic equation.

For a root $c$ of $c_M$, its algebraic multiplicity is the largest $a$ with $(x-c)^a$ dividing $c_M$.

3. Similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial 17:40

Theorem 3.1. If $M'=P^{-1}MP$ with $P$ invertible, then $c_{M'}=c_M$.

Proof. $xI-P^{-1}MP=P^{-1}(xI-M)P$. By multiplicativity, $\det(xI-M')=\det(P^{-1})\det(xI-M)\det P=\det(xI-M)$, since $\det(P^{-1})\det P=1$. $\square$

Matrices of one operator in two bases are similar (Chapter 9). So we may define $c_T=c_M$ for any matrix $M$ of $T$; it does not depend on the basis. Consequently trace, determinant, eigenvalues and algebraic multiplicities are properties of $T$. The eigenvectors themselves transform: if $M\alpha=c\alpha$, then $P^{-1}\alpha$ is an eigenvector of $P^{-1}MP$.

4. Does every operator have an eigenvalue? 22:01

No. Let $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Then $c_R(x)=x^2+1$, which has no real root. So $R$, viewed over $\mathbb{R}$, has no eigenvalue: a rotation by a quarter turn keeps no line. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the roots are $\pm i$, with eigenvectors $(1,\mp i)$: $R(1,-i)=i\,(1,-i)$ and $R(1,i)=-i\,(1,i)$. The existence of eigenvalues depends on the field.

Over $\mathbb{C}$, every operator on a finite-dimensional space of dimension $n\ge1$ has an eigenvalue, because $c_T$ is a nonconstant polynomial and by the fundamental theorem of algebra it has a complex root. Finite dimension is necessary.

An infinite-dimensional operator with no eigenvalue 02:15. Let $V$ be the space of continuous real functions on $\mathbb{R}$ and $(Tf)(x)=\int_0^xf(t)\,dt$. Suppose $Tf=\lambda f$ with $f\neq0$. If $\lambda=0$, then $\int_0^xf=0$ for all $x$; differentiating gives $f=0$. If $\lambda\neq0$, then $f=\lambda^{-1}Tf$ is differentiable, and $f'=\lambda^{-1}f$. So $f(x)=Ke^{x/\lambda}$. But $f(0)=\lambda^{-1}(Tf)(0)=0$, so $K=0$ and $f=0$. In both cases there is a contradiction. Here $V$ may even be taken complex; the conclusion does not change.

An infinite-dimensional operator with every eigenvalue 08:57. On the space $C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ of infinitely differentiable real functions, let $Df=f'$. For every $a\in\mathbb{R}$, $De^{ax}=ae^{ax}$, so every real number is an eigenvalue. The space must be closed under differentiation: on continuously differentiable functions alone, $Df$ need not be differentiable again, and $D$ would not be an operator on that space.

5. The running examples 26:13

Both matrices have

$$ c_A(x)=c_B(x)=(x-1)(x-2)^2=x^3-5x^2+8x-4 . $$

Check with Theorem 2.2: the traces are $3+2+0=5$ and $5+4-4=5$, and the determinants are both $4$. So the eigenvalues are $1$ (algebraic multiplicity $1$) and $2$ (algebraic multiplicity $2$) for both.

Eigenspaces of $A$. For $c=1$ solve $(A-I)X=0$. The matrix $A-I$ has rows $(2,1,-1)$, $(2,1,-1)$, $(2,2,-1)$. It has rank $2$, so $\dim E_1=1$. Subtracting the equations $2x_1+x_2-x_3=0$ and $2x_1+2x_2-x_3=0$ gives $x_2=0$ and then $x_3=2x_1$: $E_1$ is spanned by $(1,0,2)$. For $c=2$, $A-2I$ again has rank $2$, and $E_2$ is spanned by $(1,1,2)$. So $\dim E_1+\dim E_2=2\lt3$.

Eigenspaces of $B$ 11:06. $B-I$ has rows $(4,-6,-6)$, $(-1,3,2)$, $(3,-6,-5)$ and rank $2$; its null space is spanned by $(3,-1,3)$. The matrix $B-2I$ has rows $(3,-6,-6)$, $(-1,2,2)$, $(3,-6,-6)$, all multiples of $(1,-2,-2)$. So its rank is $1$, and $E_2$ is the plane $x_1=2x_2+2x_3$ with basis $(2,1,0)$, $(2,0,1)$. Hence $\dim E_1+\dim E_2=1+2=3$.

6. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent

Theorem 6.1. Let $c_1,\dots,c_k$ be distinct eigenvalues of $T$ (no finiteness assumed). If $\alpha_i\in E_{c_i}$ and $\alpha_1+\cdots+\alpha_k=0$, then all $\alpha_i=0$. In particular, eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.

Proof. For a polynomial $q$ write $q(T)$ for the operator obtained by substituting $T$ for $x$ (with $T^0=I$). If $T\alpha=c\alpha$, then $T^m\alpha=c^m\alpha$, so $q(T)\alpha=q(c)\alpha$. Fix $j$ and set $q(x)=\prod_{i\neq j}(x-c_i)$. Apply $q(T)$ to the relation: $q(T)\alpha_i=q(c_i)\alpha_i=0$ for $i\neq j$, so $q(c_j)\alpha_j=0$. Since the $c_i$ are distinct, $q(c_j)\neq0$, and so $\alpha_j=0$. For the last claim, if $\sum a_i\alpha_i=0$ with nonzero eigenvectors $\alpha_i$, apply the first claim to the vectors $a_i\alpha_i\in E_{c_i}$: each $a_i\alpha_i=0$, so $a_i=0$. $\square$

The hinge is the polynomial $q$ that kills every eigenspace except the $j$th.

Theorem 6.2. Let $\dim V=n$ and $c$ an eigenvalue of $T$ with geometric multiplicity $g$ and algebraic multiplicity $a$. Then $g\le a$.

Proof. Take a basis $(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_g)$ of $E_c$ and extend it to a basis of $V$. Since $T\alpha_i=c\alpha_i$ for $i\le g$, the matrix of $T$ has the block form $\begin{pmatrix}cI_g&C\\0&D\end{pmatrix}$. The determinant of a block upper triangular matrix is the product of the determinants of the diagonal blocks, so $c_T(x)=(x-c)^g\det(xI-D)$. Hence $(x-c)^g$ divides $c_T$, and $g\le a$. $\square$

The inequality can be strict: for $J=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ the algebraic multiplicity of $2$ is $2$ and the geometric multiplicity is $1$. The same happens to $A$ at $c=2$: algebraic multiplicity $2$, geometric multiplicity $1$.

7. Diagonalizable operators 34:19

Definition 7.1. Let $\dim V$ be finite. The operator $T$ is diagonalizable if $V$ has a basis consisting of eigenvectors of $T$.

In such a basis $(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$, with $T\alpha_i=c_i\alpha_i$, the matrix of $T$ is $\operatorname{diag}(c_1,\dots,c_n)$. Group equal values: let $c_1,\dots,c_k$ now denote the distinct eigenvalues, each $c_i$ occurring $d_i$ times. Then the matrix is block diagonal with blocks $c_iI_{d_i}$.

Theorem 7.2. If $T$ is diagonalizable as above, then $c_T(x)=\prod_i(x-c_i)^{d_i}$ and $\dim E_{c_i}=d_i$. So geometric and algebraic multiplicity agree for every eigenvalue.

Proof. The first formula is the determinant of the diagonal matrix $xI-[T]$. For the second, in this basis $T-c_iI$ is diagonal, with entries $0$ in the $d_i$ places of the block of $c_i$ and nonzero entries $c_j-c_i$ elsewhere. So its null space has dimension $d_i$. $\square$

Theorem 7.3. Let $\dim V=n$, and let $c_1,\dots,c_k$ be the distinct eigenvalues of $T$ in $F$. Then $T$ is diagonalizable if and only if $\sum_i\dim E_{c_i}=n$.

Proof. If $T$ is diagonalizable, Theorem 7.2 gives $\sum\dim E_{c_i}=\sum d_i=n$. Conversely, take a basis of each $E_{c_i}$ and combine them. By Theorem 6.1, the combined list is independent: a relation splits as $\sum\alpha_i=0$ with $\alpha_i\in E_{c_i}$, so each $\alpha_i=0$, and the basis of $E_{c_i}$ is independent. The list has $n$ elements, so it is a basis of eigenvectors. $\square$

In particular, $n$ distinct eigenvalues in $F$ imply diagonalizability. The converse is false: $I$ is diagonal with one eigenvalue.

8. The two matrices decided 21:36

For $A$ the sum of the eigenspace dimensions is $2\lt3$, so $A$ is not diagonalizable; no invertible $P$ makes $P^{-1}AP$ diagonal. For $B$ it is $3$. Put the eigenvectors in the columns of

$$ P=\begin{pmatrix}3&2&2\\-1&1&0\\3&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2&2\\-1&3&2\\3&-6&-5\end{pmatrix}. $$

Then $BP$ has columns $B$ times each eigenvector, which are $1\cdot(3,-1,3)$, $2\cdot(2,1,0)$ and $2\cdot(2,0,1)$. So $BP=P\operatorname{diag}(1,2,2)$, and, since $\det P=-1\neq0$,

$$ P^{-1}BP=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,2). $$

Therefore $B^k=P\operatorname{diag}(1,2^k,2^k)P^{-1}$, and each entry of $B^k$ has the form $a+b\,2^k$ with fixed $a,b$. This explains the bounded ratio $|(B^k)_{ij}|/2^k$. The matrix $A$ behaves differently. Let $u=(1,1,2)$, the eigenvector for $2$, and $w=(1,1,1)$. Direct multiplication gives $Aw=(3,3,4)=2w+u$. By induction, $A^kw=2^kw+k\,2^{k-1}u$: it holds for $k=1$, and $A^{k+1}w=2^kAw+k\,2^{k-1}Au=2^{k+1}w+(k+1)2^ku$. Hence $A^kw/2^k=w+\tfrac k2u$, which grows linearly in $k$ because $u\neq0$. So the entries of $A^k$ divided by $2^k$ cannot stay bounded, and no formula of the form $a+b\,2^k$ holds for all of them. Chapter 20 explains the general shape through a diagonalizable and a nilpotent part.

The polynomial does not determine the matrix. $A$ and $B$ have the same characteristic polynomial but are not similar: $\operatorname{rank}(A-2I)=2$ while $\operatorname{rank}(B-2I)=1$, and similar matrices $M,M'$ have $M'-2I=P^{-1}(M-2I)P$, of the same rank. Another test is the product $(M-I)(M-2I)$, which is zero for $M=B$ and nonzero for $M=A$. Chapter 13 makes this the criterion of diagonalizability.

9. The questions ahead 29:54

Can every operator be represented by a diagonal matrix? No: $A$ cannot. Which operators can? Theorem 7.3 answers in terms of eigenspaces, but one wants a test that does not require computing them. And for an operator that cannot, what is the simplest matrix it can have? The next chapters answer these through annihilating polynomials, invariant subspaces and direct sums.

The chapter laboratory uses exact fractions. It computes both characteristic polynomials, checks traces and determinants, the eigenspaces, the matrices $P$, $P^{-1}$ and $\operatorname{diag}(1,2,2)$, the ranks that separate $A$ from $B$, the powers $A^{10}$, $B^{10}$, and the growth ratios at $k=10,20$. These are checks of particular matrices; the proofs above carry the general statements.

Exercises

  1. (Polynomials.) Let $T\alpha=c\alpha$. Show that $p(T)\alpha=p(c)\alpha$ for every polynomial $p$, and check it for $B$ with $p(x)=x^2-3x+1$ on all three eigenvectors.
  2. (Computation.) Diagonalize $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&2&1\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}$.
  3. (Counterexample.) Find an operator $T$ without eigenvalues over $\mathbb{R}$ such that $T^2$ has an eigenvalue.
  4. (Field dependence.) Find the eigenvalues of $K=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and over $\mathbb{R}$, with eigenvectors.
  5. (Inverse.) Let $T$ be invertible on a finite-dimensional space. Show that $0$ is not an eigenvalue, that $c$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ if and only if $c^{-1}$ is one of $T^{-1}$, with the same eigenspace.
  6. (Classification.) Show that two diagonalizable $n\times n$ matrices over $F$ with the same characteristic polynomial are similar.
Fully worked solutions

  1. By induction $T^m\alpha=c^m\alpha$: it holds for $m=0$, and $T^{m+1}\alpha=T(c^m\alpha)=c^mT\alpha=c^{m+1}\alpha$. By linearity, $p(T)\alpha=\sum p_mT^m\alpha=\sum p_mc^m\alpha=p(c)\alpha$. For $p(x)=x^2-3x+1$ we have $p(1)=-1$ and $p(2)=-1$. So $p(B)$ multiplies all three eigenvectors of $B$ by $-1$. Since they form a basis, $p(B)=-I$, which says $B^2-3B+2I=0$, that is, $(B-I)(B-2I)=0$.

  2. The matrix is upper triangular, so $c_C(x)=(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)$. Three distinct eigenvalues, so $C$ is diagonalizable. $(C-I)X=0$ gives $x_2=0$, $x_3=0$: eigenvector $(1,0,0)$. $(C-2I)X=0$ gives $-x_1+x_2=0$, $x_3=0$: eigenvector $(1,1,0)$. $(C-3I)X=0$ gives $-x_2+x_3=0$ and $-2x_1+x_2=0$: with $x_1=1$, $x_2=x_3=2$: eigenvector $(1,2,2)$. With $P_C$ having these columns, $P_C^{-1}CP_C=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3)$.

  3. Take $R$, the quarter turn. It has no real eigenvalue. But $R^2=-I$, which has the eigenvalue $-1$ with eigenspace all of $\mathbb{R}^2$. So eigenvalues of $T^2$ need not be squares of eigenvalues of $T$ in the same field. Over $\mathbb{C}$ they are: $(\pm i)^2=-1$.

  4. $c_K(x)=x^2-2$. It has no rational root, since $\sqrt2$ is irrational: $K$ has no eigenvalue over $\mathbb{Q}$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the eigenvalues are $\pm\sqrt2$. The equation $KX=\lambda X$ reads $2x_2=\lambda x_1$, $x_1=\lambda x_2$; with $x_2=1$ this gives $(\lambda,1)$. So the eigenvectors are $(\sqrt2,1)$ and $(-\sqrt2,1)$.

  5. If $T\alpha=0$ with $\alpha\neq0$, then $T$ would not be injective, so $0$ is not an eigenvalue. If $T\alpha=c\alpha$ with $\alpha\neq0$, then $c\neq0$ and applying $T^{-1}$ gives $\alpha=cT^{-1}\alpha$, so $T^{-1}\alpha=c^{-1}\alpha$. Applying the same argument to $T^{-1}$ gives the converse. Both directions show that the eigenvectors, and hence the eigenspaces, coincide.

  6. Let $M,M'$ be diagonalizable with $c_M=c_{M'}$. By Theorem 7.2 each is similar to a diagonal matrix whose entries are its eigenvalues, each repeated as often as its algebraic multiplicity. The characteristic polynomials agree, so the two diagonal matrices have the same entries, in some order. A permutation of diagonal entries is achieved by a similarity with a permutation matrix (reordering the basis vectors). So $M$ and $M'$ are similar to the same diagonal matrix, hence to each other. The hypothesis is needed: $A$ and $B$ above have the same characteristic polynomial and are not similar.

"""Chapter 12 laboratory: eigenvalues, eigenvectors, characteristic polynomial.

Exact arithmetic over Q (fractions.Fraction).  A matrix is a list of rows.
Every routine returns a new object and never changes its input.  A
polynomial is a list of coefficients, highest degree first: [1, -5, 8, -4]
stands for x^3 - 5x^2 + 8x - 4.
"""
from fractions import Fraction


def Q(rows):
    """Matrix over Q from integer or Fraction entries."""
    return [[Fraction(x) for x in row] for row in rows]


def identity(n):
    return [[Fraction(int(i == j)) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def mat_mul(a, b):
    return [[sum(a[i][k] * b[k][j] for k in range(len(b)))
             for j in range(len(b[0]))] for i in range(len(a))]


def mat_add(a, b, s=1):
    """Return a + s*b."""
    return [[x + s * y for x, y in zip(r, t)] for r, t in zip(a, b)]


def scalar_mul(s, a):
    return [[s * x for x in row] for row in a]


def mat_pow(a, k):
    out = identity(len(a))
    for _ in range(k):
        out = mat_mul(out, a)
    return out


def mat_vec(a, v):
    return [sum(x * y for x, y in zip(row, v)) for row in a]


def trace(a):
    return sum(a[i][i] for i in range(len(a)))


def rref(a):
    """Reduced row echelon form and the list of pivot columns."""
    m = [row[:] for row in a]
    rows, cols = len(m), len(m[0])
    pivots, r = [], 0
    for c in range(cols):
        p = next((i for i in range(r, rows) if m[i][c] != 0), None)
        if p is None:
            continue
        m[r], m[p] = m[p], m[r]
        lead = m[r][c]
        m[r] = [x / lead for x in m[r]]
        for i in range(rows):
            if i != r and m[i][c] != 0:
                f = m[i][c]
                m[i] = [x - f * y for x, y in zip(m[i], m[r])]
        pivots.append(c)
        r += 1
        if r == rows:
            break
    return m, pivots


def rank(a):
    return len(rref(a)[1])


def null_basis(a):
    """Basis of {x : a x = 0}, one vector per free column."""
    m, pivots = rref(a)
    n = len(a[0])
    basis = []
    for f in (c for c in range(n) if c not in pivots):
        v = [Fraction(0)] * n
        v[f] = Fraction(1)
        for row, pc in zip(m, pivots):
            v[pc] = -row[f]
        basis.append(v)
    return basis


def det(a):
    """Determinant by fraction-exact elimination."""
    m = [row[:] for row in a]
    n, d = len(m), Fraction(1)
    for c in range(n):
        p = next((i for i in range(c, n) if m[i][c] != 0), None)
        if p is None:
            return Fraction(0)
        if p != c:
            m[c], m[p] = m[p], m[c]
            d = -d
        d *= m[c][c]
        for i in range(c + 1, n):
            f = m[i][c] / m[c][c]
            m[i] = [x - f * y for x, y in zip(m[i], m[c])]
    return d


def inverse(a):
    n = len(a)
    aug = [list(r) + list(e) for r, e in zip(a, identity(n))]
    m, pivots = rref(aug)
    assert pivots[:n] == list(range(n)), 'matrix is singular'
    return [row[n:] for row in m]


def charpoly(a):
    """det(xI - A) by the Faddeev-LeVerrier recurrence, exact over Q."""
    n = len(a)
    coeffs = [Fraction(1)]
    m = identity(n)
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        am = mat_mul(a, m)
        ck = -trace(am) / k
        coeffs.append(ck)
        m = mat_add(am, identity(n), ck)
    return coeffs


def poly_mul(p, q):
    out = [Fraction(0)] * (len(p) + len(q) - 1)
    for i, x in enumerate(p):
        for j, y in enumerate(q):
            out[i + j] += x * y
    return out


def poly_eval(p, x):
    out = Fraction(0)
    for c in p:
        out = out * x + c
    return out


def poly_div_linear(p, c):
    """Quotient and remainder of p by (x - c), synthetic division."""
    q = [p[0]]
    for coef in p[1:]:
        q.append(coef + c * q[-1])
    return q[:-1], q[-1]


def multiplicity(p, c):
    """Largest m with (x - c)^m dividing p."""
    m = 0
    while len(p) > 1:
        q, r = poly_div_linear(p, c)
        if r != 0:
            break
        p, m = q, m + 1
    return m


def eigenspace(a, c):
    """Basis of E_c = ker(A - cI)."""
    return null_basis(mat_add(a, scalar_mul(c, identity(len(a))), -1))


def is_eigenpair(a, c, v):
    return any(x != 0 for x in v) and mat_vec(a, v) == [c * x for x in v]


def columns_to_matrix(vectors):
    return [[v[i] for v in vectors] for i in range(len(vectors[0]))]


def show(m):
    return [[str(x) for x in row] for row in m]


def main():
    # ---- A and B, the two running matrices --------------------------
    A = Q([[3, 1, -1], [2, 2, -1], [2, 2, 0]])
    B = Q([[5, -6, -6], [-1, 4, 2], [3, -6, -4]])
    target = poly_mul(poly_mul([1, -1], [1, -2]), [1, -2])
    target = [Fraction(x) for x in target]
    print('target (x-1)(x-2)^2 =', [str(x) for x in target])
    for name, M in (('A', A), ('B', B)):
        cp = charpoly(M)
        print(name, 'charpoly coefficients:', [str(x) for x in cp])
        assert cp == target
        assert cp[1] == -trace(M) == -5
        assert cp[3] == -det(M) == -4
        assert multiplicity(cp, 1) == 1 and multiplicity(cp, 2) == 2
        # roots are exactly the eigenvalues (Theorem 2.3)
        for c in range(-3, 5):
            has_vec = len(eigenspace(M, Fraction(c))) > 0
            assert has_vec == (poly_eval(cp, Fraction(c)) == 0)
        assert det(mat_add(M, scalar_mul(2, identity(3)), -1)) == 0
    print('det A =', det(A), ' tr A =', trace(A), ' det B =', det(B),
          ' tr B =', trace(B))

    # ---- eigenvectors of A (the defective matrix) -------------------
    EA1 = eigenspace(A, Fraction(1))
    EA2 = eigenspace(A, Fraction(2))
    print('A: E_1 basis', show(EA1), ' E_2 basis', show(EA2))
    assert [len(EA1), len(EA2)] == [1, 1]
    assert is_eigenpair(A, Fraction(1), [1, 0, 2])
    assert is_eigenpair(A, Fraction(2), [1, 1, 2])
    assert rank(mat_add(A, identity(3), -1)) == 2
    assert rank(mat_add(A, scalar_mul(2, identity(3)), -1)) == 2
    assert rank(columns_to_matrix([[1, 0, 2], [1, 1, 2]])) == 2
    assert len(EA1) + len(EA2) < 3   # A is not diagonalizable
    print('A: sum of eigenspace dimensions =', len(EA1) + len(EA2), '< 3')

    # ---- eigenvectors of B and diagonalization ----------------------
    EB1 = eigenspace(B, Fraction(1))
    EB2 = eigenspace(B, Fraction(2))
    print('B: E_1 basis', show(EB1), ' E_2 basis', show(EB2))
    assert [len(EB1), len(EB2)] == [1, 2]
    a1, a2, a3 = Q([[3, -1, 3], [2, 1, 0], [2, 0, 1]])
    for c, v in ((1, a1), (2, a2), (2, a3)):
        assert is_eigenpair(B, Fraction(c), v)
    P = columns_to_matrix([a1, a2, a3])
    Pinv = inverse(P)
    D = mat_mul(mat_mul(Pinv, B), P)
    print('P =', show(P), ' det P =', det(P))
    print('P^-1 =', show(Pinv))
    print('P^-1 B P =', show(D))
    assert D == Q([[1, 0, 0], [0, 2, 0], [0, 0, 2]])
    assert det(P) == -1
    assert mat_mul(B, P) == mat_mul(P, D)

    # ---- same charpoly, different similarity class ------------------
    rA = rank(mat_add(A, scalar_mul(2, identity(3)), -1))
    rB = rank(mat_add(B, scalar_mul(2, identity(3)), -1))
    print('rank(A-2I) =', rA, ' rank(B-2I) =', rB)
    assert (rA, rB) == (2, 1)       # rank is a similarity invariant
    I3 = identity(3)

    def f(M):
        return mat_mul(mat_add(M, I3, -1),
                       mat_add(M, scalar_mul(2, I3), -1))

    zero = Q([[0] * 3] * 3)
    assert f(B) == zero and f(A) != zero
    print('(A-I)(A-2I) =', show(f(A)))

    # ---- opening problem: powers -----------------------------------
    for k in (10, 20):
        Ak, Bk = mat_pow(A, k), mat_pow(B, k)
        assert Bk == mat_mul(mat_mul(P, mat_pow(D, k)), Pinv)
        mA = max(abs(x) for r in Ak for x in r) / 2 ** k
        mB = max(abs(x) for r in Bk for x in r) / 2 ** k
        print('k =', k, ' max|A^k|/2^k =', float(mA),
              ' max|B^k|/2^k =', float(mB))
    A10, B10 = mat_pow(A, 10), mat_pow(B, 10)
    print('A^10 =', show(A10))
    print('B^10 =', show(B10))
    assert max(abs(x) for r in mat_pow(A, 20) for x in r) / 2 ** 20 > \
        1.9 * max(abs(x) for r in A10 for x in r) / 2 ** 10
    assert max(abs(x) for r in mat_pow(B, 20) for x in r) / 2 ** 20 < \
        max(abs(x) for r in B10 for x in r) / 2 ** 10 * 1.01

    # ---- field dependence: the rotation ----------------------------
    R = Q([[0, -1], [1, 0]])
    cpR = charpoly(R)
    print('rotation charpoly:', [str(x) for x in cpR])
    assert cpR == Q([[1, 0, 1]])[0]
    for c in range(-5, 6):
        assert poly_eval(cpR, Fraction(c)) >= 1   # x^2 + 1 >= 1 on Q
    assert null_basis(mat_add(R, identity(2), 1)) == []
    for c, v in ((1j, [1, -1j]), (-1j, [1, 1j])):
        Rv = [R[0][0] * v[0] + R[0][1] * v[1],
              R[1][0] * v[0] + R[1][1] * v[1]]
        assert Rv == [c * x for x in v]
    print('R(1,-i) = i(1,-i) and R(1,i) = -i(1,i) verified')

    # ---- similarity invariance (Theorem 3.1) ----------------------
    S = Q([[1, 1, 0], [0, 1, 1], [1, 0, 1]])
    assert det(S) == 2
    A_s = mat_mul(mat_mul(inverse(S), A), S)
    assert charpoly(A_s) == charpoly(A)
    v = Q([[1, 0, 2]])[0]
    assert is_eigenpair(A_s, Fraction(1), mat_vec(inverse(S), v))
    print('charpoly(S^-1 A S) = charpoly(A);  S^-1 (1,0,2) is a 1-eigenvector')

    # ---- coefficients of charpoly (Theorem 2.2), a 4x4 test -------
    M4 = Q([[2, -1, 0, 3], [1, 4, 1, 0], [0, 2, -3, 1], [5, 0, 1, 1]])
    c4 = charpoly(M4)
    print('4x4 charpoly:', [str(x) for x in c4], ' tr =', trace(M4),
          ' det =', det(M4))
    assert c4[1] == -trace(M4) and c4[4] == det(M4)

    # ---- geometric <= algebraic (Theorem 6.2) --------------------
    J = Q([[2, 1], [0, 2]])
    assert multiplicity(charpoly(J), Fraction(2)) == 2
    assert len(eigenspace(J, Fraction(2))) == 1
    for M in (A, B, J, R):
        cp = charpoly(M)
        for c in range(-3, 4):
            assert len(eigenspace(M, Fraction(c))) <= multiplicity(
                cp, Fraction(c))
    print('Jordan block: algebraic multiplicity 2, geometric 1')

    # ---- distinct eigenvalues (Theorem 6.1, Exercise 2) -----------
    C = Q([[1, 1, 0], [0, 2, 1], [0, 0, 3]])
    assert charpoly(C) == [Fraction(x) for x in (1, -6, 11, -6)]
    vecs = []
    for c in (1, 2, 3):
        E = eigenspace(C, Fraction(c))
        assert len(E) == 1
        vecs.append(E[0])
    print('C eigenvectors:', [show([v])[0] for v in vecs])
    PC = columns_to_matrix(Q([[1, 0, 0], [1, 1, 0], [1, 2, 2]]))
    for c, col in zip((1, 2, 3), ([1, 0, 0], [1, 1, 0], [1, 2, 2])):
        assert is_eigenpair(C, Fraction(c), Q([col])[0])
    DC = mat_mul(mat_mul(inverse(PC), C), PC)
    assert DC == Q([[1, 0, 0], [0, 2, 0], [0, 0, 3]])
    assert rank(columns_to_matrix(vecs)) == 3
    print('P_C^-1 C P_C =', show(DC))

    # ---- Exercise 1: p(T) alpha = p(c) alpha -----------------------
    pB = mat_add(mat_add(mat_pow(B, 2), B, -3), I3)       # B^2-3B+I
    for c, v in ((1, a1), (2, a2), (2, a3)):
        p_c = c * c - 3 * c + 1
        assert mat_vec(pB, v) == [p_c * x for x in v]
    print('p(x) = x^2-3x+1: p(B)v = p(c)v on all three eigenvectors')

    # ---- Exercise 3: rotation squared ------------------------------
    assert mat_mul(R, R) == Q([[-1, 0], [0, -1]])
    assert len(eigenspace(mat_mul(R, R), Fraction(-1))) == 2

    # ---- Exercise 4: field dependence ------------------------------
    K = Q([[0, 2], [1, 0]])
    cpK = charpoly(K)
    assert cpK == Q([[1, 0, -2]])[0]
    assert all(poly_eval(cpK, Fraction(c)) != 0
               for c in (-2, -1, 1, 2, Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(-1, 2)))
    root = 2 ** 0.5
    for lam, vec in ((root, [root, 1]), (-root, [-root, 1])):
        Kv = [2 * vec[1], vec[0]]
        assert all(abs(x - lam * y) < 1e-12 for x, y in zip(Kv, vec))
    print('K charpoly x^2-2: no rational root; real eigenvalues +-sqrt 2')

    # ---- Exercise 6: same charpoly, both diagonalizable => similar --
    D2 = Q([[1, 0, 0], [0, 2, 0], [0, 0, 2]])
    B2 = mat_mul(mat_mul(S, D2), inverse(S))
    assert charpoly(B2) == charpoly(B) and B2 != B
    W = mat_mul(S, Pinv)                 # B2 = W B W^-1
    assert mat_mul(mat_mul(W, B), inverse(W)) == B2
    print('B2 = S diag(1,2,2) S^-1 =', show(B2), ' is similar to B')
    print('all assertions passed')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
