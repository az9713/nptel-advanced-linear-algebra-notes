Consider the system of three differential equations $X'(t)=AX(t)$, $X(0)=X_0$, with

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1&-1\\2&2&-1\\2&2&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

This matrix is the first running example of Chapter 12, where it could not be diagonalized and where its powers grew like $k\,2^k$. Its minimal polynomial is $(x-1)(x-2)^2$. If $A$ had a basis of eigenvectors $u_i$ with eigenvalues $c_i$, the solutions would be combinations of $e^{c_it}u_i$. It has only two independent eigenvectors, so a third solution is missing. This chapter supplies it. The same structure then describes every operator: first as a diagonalizable operator plus a nilpotent one, and finally as a block diagonal matrix of a fixed shape.

Conventions. $F$ is a field, $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$, and $T$ is a linear operator on $V$ with minimal polynomial $m_T$. A subspace is $T$-invariant if $T$ maps it into itself. An operator $N$ is nilpotent if $N^r=0$ for some $r\ge1$; the least such $r$ is its index. The primary decomposition theorem (Chapter 19) says that if $m_T=p_1^{r_1}\cdots p_k^{r_k}$ with distinct monic irreducible $p_i$, then $V=W_1\oplus\cdots\oplus W_k$ with $W_i=\ker p_i(T)^{r_i}$ invariant, the minimal polynomial of $T$ on $W_i$ is $p_i^{r_i}$, and the projections $E_i$ on the $W_i$ are polynomials in $T$.

1. Linear initial value problems 00:15

Let $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ be constant. We look for differentiable functions $X:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^n$ with $X'=AX$. Substituting $X(t)=e^{\lambda t}u$ with a constant vector $u$ gives $\lambda e^{\lambda t}u=e^{\lambda t}Au$. Since $e^{\lambda t}\ne0$, this is a solution exactly when $(A-\lambda I)u=0$. A nonzero solution of this form is an eigenvector $u$ times $e^{\lambda t}$.

A system with too few eigenvectors 06:32. Let $B=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}$. Its only eigenvalue is $-2$, and its eigenvectors are the multiples of $v_1=(1,0)$. One solution is $X_1=e^{-2t}v_1$. Guided by the repeated-root case of a single differential equation, try $X_2=e^{-2t}(tu+v)$. Then

$$X_2'=e^{-2t}\big(-2tu-2v+u\big),\qquad BX_2=e^{-2t}\big(tBu+Bv\big).$$

Comparing the coefficients of $te^{-2t}$ and $e^{-2t}$ gives $(B+2I)u=0$ and $(B+2I)v=u$. Take $u=v_1$ and $v=v_2=(0,1)$. So $X_2=e^{-2t}(tv_1+v_2)$ is a second solution. Since $X_1(0)=v_1$ and $X_2(0)=v_2$ are independent, $c_1X_1+c_2X_2$ meets every initial value. The vector $v_2$ is not an eigenvector, but $(B+2I)^2v_2=0$.

2. Generalized eigenvectors and chains 19:45

Definition 2.1. Let $\lambda\in F$ be an eigenvalue of $T$. A vector $v$ is a generalized eigenvector of rank $r$ for $\lambda$ if $(T-\lambda I)^rv=0$ and $(T-\lambda I)^{r-1}v\ne0$.

Rank $1$ means an ordinary eigenvector. Given $v$ of rank $r$, put $v_r=v$ and $v_{i}=(T-\lambda I)v_{i+1}$ for $i=r-1,\dots,1$. Then $v_i=(T-\lambda I)^{r-i}v$, and with $v_0=0$,

$$(T-\lambda I)v_i=v_{i-1},\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad Tv_i=\lambda v_i+v_{i-1}\quad(1\le i\le r).$$

The list $(v_1,\dots,v_r)$ is the chain generated by $v$. Its first member is an eigenvector.

Lemma 2.2. A chain is linearly independent.

Proof. Suppose $\sum_{i=1}^ra_iv_i=0$ with some $a_i\ne0$, and let $j$ be the largest such index. Apply $(T-\lambda I)^{j-1}$. It sends $v_i$ to $0$ for $i<j$ and sends $v_j$ to $v_1$. So $a_jv_1=0$. But $v_1=(T-\lambda I)^{r-1}v\ne0$, a contradiction. $\square$

For $B$ above, $(v_1,v_2)$ is a chain for $\lambda=-2$. For the running matrix $A$ and $\lambda=2$, the vector $w=(1,1,1)$ has $(A-2I)w=(1,1,2)=u$ and $(A-2I)u=0$. So $(u,w)$ is a chain of length $2$.

3. Solutions from a chain 06:36

Theorem 3.1. Let $(v_1,\dots,v_r)$ be a chain of $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ for $\lambda$. For $1\le j\le r$ the functions

$$X_j(t)=e^{\lambda t}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{t^{j-i}}{(j-i)!}\,v_i$$

solve $X'=AX$, and they are linearly independent.

Proof. Differentiating,

$$X_j'=\lambda X_j+e^{\lambda t}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\frac{t^{j-i-1}}{(j-i-1)!}v_i .$$

On the other side, $Av_i=\lambda v_i+v_{i-1}$ with $v_0=0$, so

$$AX_j=\lambda X_j+e^{\lambda t}\sum_{i=2}^{j}\frac{t^{j-i}}{(j-i)!}v_{i-1}.$$

Replacing $i$ by $i+1$ in the last sum shows that the two expressions agree. At $t=0$ we have $X_j(0)=v_j$, which are independent by Lemma 2.2. A linear relation among the functions would give the same relation at $t=0$. $\square$

The factorials are needed: without them the derivative of $t^{j-i}$ leaves an unwanted factor $j-i$.

On the running example. The eigenvector for $1$ is $u_1=(1,0,2)$. Three independent solutions are

$$e^{t}u_1,\qquad e^{2t}u,\qquad e^{2t}(tu+w).$$

Their values at $t=0$ form the basis $(u_1,u,w)$ of $\mathbb{C}^3$.

A six-dimensional pattern 00:25. Let $A\in\mathbb{C}^{6\times6}$ have characteristic polynomial $(x-\lambda_1)^2(x-\lambda_2)^4$ and minimal polynomial $(x-\lambda_1)(x-\lambda_2)^3$ with $\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2$. The factor for $\lambda_1$ is simple, so $\ker(A-\lambda_1I)$ has dimension $2$: two eigenvector solutions. The exponent $3$ for $\lambda_2$ guarantees a chain of length $3$, giving three solutions. The primary component has dimension $4$, so one more independent eigenvector for $\lambda_2$ remains. Section 8 shows that this count is forced.

4. The diagonalizable and nilpotent parts 19:25

Theorem 4.1. Suppose $m_T=(x-c_1)^{r_1}\cdots(x-c_k)^{r_k}$ with distinct $c_i\in F$. There are unique operators $D$ and $N$ with $T=D+N$, $D$ diagonalizable, $N$ nilpotent and $DN=ND$. Both are polynomials in $T$.

Proof. Existence. Chapter 19 gives it: $D=\sum c_iE_i$ and $N=T-D$ are polynomials in $T$, $D$ is diagonalizable, and $N^r=\sum(T-c_iI)^rE_i=0$ for $r\ge\max r_i$.

Uniqueness. Let $T=D'+N'$ with the same properties. Then $D'$ commutes with $N'$ and so with $T=D'+N'$, hence with every polynomial in $T$, in particular with $D$ and $N$. The same holds for $N'$. Now

$$D-D'=N'-N.$$

The right side is a difference of commuting nilpotent operators. If $N^a=0$ and $(N')^b=0$, every term of the binomial expansion of $(N'-N)^{a+b}$ contains $N^a$ or $(N')^b$, so $N'-N$ is nilpotent. On the left, $D$ and $D'$ are commuting diagonalizable operators, so they have a common basis of eigenvectors (Chapter 16, Exercise 6), and $D-D'$ is diagonal in it. A diagonal matrix that is nilpotent is zero. So $D=D'$ and $N=N'$. $\square$

The hinge of uniqueness is that an operator which is both diagonalizable and nilpotent is $0$. The requirement $DN=ND$ cannot be dropped (Exercise 4).

On the running example. Here $E_1=(A-2I)^2$ is the projection onto $\ker(A-I)$, since the polynomial $(x-2)^2$ takes the value $1$ at $x=1$ and vanishes to order $2$ at $x=2$. Then $D=E_1+2(I-E_1)=2I-E_1$ and $N=A-D$:

$$D=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&2&0\\-2&2&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad N=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&-1\\2&0&-1\\4&0&-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad N^2=0,\quad DN=ND.$$

All solutions at once. Over $\mathbb{C}$ define

$$\Phi(t)=\sum_{i=1}^ke^{c_it}\Big(\sum_{s=0}^{r_i-1}\frac{t^s}{s!}(A-c_iI)^s\Big)E_i .$$

Theorem 4.2. The problem $X'=AX$, $X(0)=X_0$ has exactly one solution, $X(t)=\Phi(t)X_0$.

Proof. Write $\Phi(t)=\sum_ie^{c_it}Q_i(t)E_i$ with $Q_i(t)=\sum_{s<r_i}t^sN_i^s/s!$ and $N_i=A-c_iI$, so $N_i^{r_i}E_i=0$. Then $Q_i'(t)E_i=N_iQ_i(t)E_i$, because the extra top term contains $N_i^{r_i}E_i=0$. Hence $\Phi'=\sum_ie^{c_it}(c_iI+N_i)Q_iE_i=A\Phi$, and $\Phi(0)=\sum E_i=I$. So $\Phi(t)X_0$ is a solution. For uniqueness, note that $\Phi(-t)\Phi(t)=\sum_iQ_i(-t)Q_i(t)E_i$, since $E_iE_j=0$ for $i\ne j$. By the binomial theorem the coefficient of $N_i^s/s!$ in $Q_i(-t)Q_i(t)$ is $(t-t)^s$ for $s<r_i$, and higher powers vanish on $\operatorname{im}E_i$. So $\Phi(-t)\Phi(t)=I$. Now let $X$ be any solution. All factors of $\Phi(-t)$ are polynomials in $A$ and commute with $A$, and $\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(-t)=-A\Phi(-t)$. So $\frac{d}{dt}\big(\Phi(-t)X(t)\big)=-A\Phi(-t)X+\Phi(-t)AX=0$. Hence $\Phi(-t)X(t)=X(0)=X_0$, and $X(t)=\Phi(t)X_0$. $\square$

5. Cyclic subspaces and companion matrices 15:02

The decomposition into $D$ and $N$ needs a polynomial that splits. To describe operators over every field we use subspaces generated by one vector.

Definition 5.1. For $\alpha\in V$, the $T$-cyclic subspace generated by $\alpha$ is $Z(\alpha;T)=\{f(T)\alpha:f\in F[x]\}$. The $T$-annihilator of $\alpha$ is the monic generator $p_\alpha$ of the ideal $\{f:f(T)\alpha=0\}$. If $Z(\alpha;T)=V$, then $\alpha$ is a cyclic vector.

$Z(\alpha;T)$ is a subspace, and it is $T$-invariant because $Tf(T)\alpha=(xf)(T)\alpha$.

Theorem 5.2. Let $\alpha\ne0$ and $p_\alpha=x^k+a_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+a_0$. Then $(\alpha,T\alpha,\dots,T^{k-1}\alpha)$ is a basis of $Z(\alpha;T)$, the restriction of $T$ to $Z(\alpha;T)$ has minimal polynomial $p_\alpha$, and its matrix in this basis is the companion matrix of $p_\alpha$,

$$C(p_\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&-a_0\\1&0&\cdots&0&-a_1\\0&1&\cdots&0&-a_2\\\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\0&0&\cdots&1&-a_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}.$$

Proof. Divide $f$ by $p_\alpha$: $f=qp_\alpha+s$ with $\deg s<k$. Then $f(T)\alpha=s(T)\alpha$, so the $k$ vectors span. A nontrivial relation among them would be a nonzero polynomial of degree below $k$ in the ideal, which is impossible. For the minimal polynomial: $g(T)$ vanishes on $Z(\alpha;T)$ exactly when $g(T)\alpha=0$, since $g(T)f(T)\alpha=f(T)g(T)\alpha$. The matrix follows from $T(T^i\alpha)=T^{i+1}\alpha$ for $i<k-1$ and $T(T^{k-1}\alpha)=-\sum_ia_iT^i\alpha$. $\square$

6. The cyclic decomposition for a prime power 39:22

Theorem 6.1. Let $p$ be monic irreducible of degree $d$ and $m_T=p^r$. Then $V=Z(\alpha_1;T)\oplus\cdots\oplus Z(\alpha_q;T)$ for some nonzero $\alpha_i$.

The proof needs one fact. If $\beta\in\ker p(T)$ and $\beta\ne0$, its annihilator is $p$. For $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_s\in\ker p(T)$ we call them $p$-independent if $\sum g_i(T)\beta_i=0$ forces $p\mid g_i$ for all $i$. For $g\in F[x]$ let $[g]$ be its residue class modulo $p$, the set of all $g+hp$ with $h\in F[x]$. Sums and products of classes, computed on any representatives, do not depend on the choice, and the classes form a commutative ring $F[x]/(p)$. It is a field $K$: if $[g]\ne0$, then $p$ does not divide $g$. The ideal of all $ag+bp$ is generated by one monic polynomial, which divides the irreducible $p$ and also $g$, so it is $1$. Hence $ag+bp=1$ for some $a,b\in F[x]$, and $[a][g]=1$. The rule $[g]\beta=g(T)\beta$ is well defined on $\ker p(T)$, because $p(T)\beta=0$, and it makes $\ker p(T)$ a vector space over $K$. So $p$-independence is linear independence over $K$, and every $p$-independent set in $\ker p(T)$ extends to a $K$-basis.

Proof of Theorem 6.1. Induct on $\dim V$; for $V=0$ there is nothing to prove. Let $U=\operatorname{im}p(T)$. It is invariant, and it is smaller than $V$, since $p(T)$ is nilpotent. On $U$ the minimal polynomial divides $p^r$. By induction, $U=\bigoplus_jZ(u_j;T)$ with $u_j\ne0$ and annihilators $p^{e_j}$. Choose $v_j$ with $p(T)v_j=u_j$, and put $z_j=p(T)^{e_j}v_j=p(T)^{e_j-1}u_j$. Then $z_j$ is a nonzero element of $\ker p(T)$ in $Z(u_j;T)$. Because the $Z(u_j;T)$ form a direct sum, the $z_j$ are $p$-independent. Extend them to a $K$-basis of $\ker p(T)$ by vectors $w_1,\dots,w_s$. We claim that $V$ is the direct sum of the $Z(v_j;T)$ and the $Z(w_l;T)$.

Independence. Suppose $\sum_jf_j(T)v_j+\sum_lh_l(T)w_l=0$. Apply $p(T)$. The second sum dies, and $\sum_jf_j(T)u_j=0$. By directness in $U$, $f_j(T)u_j=0$, so $f_j=g_jp^{e_j}$. Then $f_j(T)v_j=g_j(T)z_j$, and the original relation reads $\sum_jg_j(T)z_j+\sum_lh_l(T)w_l=0$. It lies in $\ker p(T)$, and the $z_j,w_l$ are $p$-independent. So $p$ divides every $g_j$ and $h_l$. Hence every term $f_j(T)v_j$ and $h_l(T)w_l$ is zero.

Spanning. Let $S$ be the sum. Then $p(T)S$ contains every $Z(u_j;T)$, so $p(T)S=U$. Also $\ker p(T)\subseteq S$, since it is spanned over $K$ by the $z_j$ and $w_l$, and $z_j\in Z(v_j;T)$. Given $\alpha\in V$, there is $\sigma\in S$ with $p(T)\sigma=p(T)\alpha$. Then $\alpha-\sigma\in\ker p(T)\subseteq S$, so $\alpha\in S$. $\square$

The hinge is the lifting step: each cyclic summand of the smaller space $\operatorname{im}p(T)$ is the image of a longer cyclic summand upstairs, and the summands that die under $p(T)$ come from a basis of the kernel.

Corollary 6.2 (uniqueness of the exponents). In any such decomposition, the number of summands with annihilator $p^e$, $e\ge i$, is $(\dim\ker p(T)^i-\dim\ker p(T)^{i-1})/d$. So the multiset of annihilators depends only on $T$.

Proof. Kernels split over invariant direct sums. On one summand $Z$ with annihilator $p^e$, $p(T)^if(T)\alpha=0$ exactly when $p^{e-i}\mid f$ (for $i\le e$). By Theorem 5.2 the space of such $f(T)\alpha$ has dimension $di$. So on $Z$, $\dim\ker p(T)^i=d\min(i,e)$, and the difference between $i$ and $i-1$ is $d$ if $e\ge i$ and $0$ otherwise. $\square$

7. Nilpotent operators and Jordan nilpotent blocks 22:27

Take $p=x$, so $T=N$ is nilpotent. Here $d=1$. A cyclic subspace with annihilator $x^e$ has the basis $(N^{e-1}\alpha,\dots,N\alpha,\alpha)$, a chain for the eigenvalue $0$. In this order $N$ sends each basis vector to the one before it, so its matrix is

$$J_e=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&&\\&0&\ddots&\\&&\ddots&1\\&&&0\end{pmatrix}\in F^{e\times e},$$

the Jordan nilpotent block, with ones just above the diagonal. It satisfies $J_e^e=0$ and $J_e^{e-1}\ne0$.

Theorem 7.1 00:25. Every nilpotent operator $N$ of index $r$ has a matrix $\operatorname{diag}(J_{e_1},\dots,J_{e_q})$ with $r=e_1\ge e_2\ge\cdots\ge e_q$. The number of blocks is $q=\dim\ker N$, and with $\mu_i=\dim\ker N^i$ the number of blocks of size exactly $i$ is

$$2\mu_i-\mu_{i-1}-\mu_{i+1}.$$

Proof. Existence is Theorem 6.1 with $p=x$. By Corollary 6.2 the number of blocks of size at least $i$ is $\mu_i-\mu_{i-1}$. For $i=1$ this gives $q=\mu_1$. The number of size exactly $i$ is $(\mu_i-\mu_{i-1})-(\mu_{i+1}-\mu_i)$. The largest block has size $r$ because $N^s$ vanishes exactly when every $J_{e}^s$ does. $\square$

Two examples 04:30. Let $N_1,N_2$ be nilpotent operators on $F^5$, both of index $3$. If $\operatorname{rank}N_1=2$, then $N_1$ has $5-2=3$ blocks, one of size $3$, and the others must be $1$ and $1$. If $\operatorname{rank}N_2=3$, then $N_2$ has $2$ blocks, of sizes $3$ and $2$. The index alone does not decide the form.

8. The Jordan canonical form 38:17

For $c\in F$ the Jordan block of size $e$ is $J_e(c)=cI_e+J_e$.

Theorem 8.1. Suppose $m_T$ splits, $m_T=\prod(x-c_i)^{r_i}$. There is a basis in which the matrix of $T$ is block diagonal with Jordan blocks $J_e(c_i)$. For each $c_i$:

  1. the largest block has size $r_i$, the exponent in $m_T$;
  2. the sizes add up to the algebraic multiplicity of $c_i$;
  3. the number of blocks equals the geometric multiplicity $\dim\ker(T-c_iI)$.

The blocks are determined by $T$ up to their order.

Proof. Apply Theorem 7.1 to the nilpotent operator $T-c_iI$ on the primary component $W_i$, whose index is $r_i$. The matrix of $T$ on $W_i$ is $c_iI$ plus the nilpotent form. Concatenate the bases. Item 1 is the index; item 2 is $\dim W_i$, which is the algebraic multiplicity (Chapter 19); item 3 is $\dim\ker$ of $T-c_iI$ on $W_i$, and $\ker(T-c_iI)\subseteq W_i$. For uniqueness, the sizes are given by the formula of Theorem 7.1 in terms of $\dim\ker(T-c_iI)^s$. $\square$

On the running example. The basis $(u_1,u,w)$ gives

$$P^{-1}AP=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&2&1\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&1\\2&2&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

The six-dimensional pattern of Section 3 is now forced: blocks $J_1(\lambda_1),J_1(\lambda_1),J_3(\lambda_2),J_1(\lambda_2)$.

Listing the possible forms. Let $c_T=(x-1)^3(x-2)^4$ and $m_T=(x-1)^3(x-2)^2$. For eigenvalue $1$ there is one block $J_3(1)$. For eigenvalue $2$ the sizes add to $4$ with largest size $2$: either $2+2$ or $2+1+1$. So there are exactly two forms. The geometric multiplicity of $2$ is $2$ in the first and $3$ in the second. A value of $4$ is impossible: it would mean four blocks of size $1$, and then the exponent of $x-2$ in $m_T$ would be $1$.

9. The rational canonical form 25:46

When $m_T$ does not split, blocks with a single eigenvalue do not exist, but companion blocks do.

Theorem 9.1. Let $m_T=p_1^{r_1}\cdots p_k^{r_k}$. There is a basis in which $T$ has the block diagonal matrix with blocks $C(p_i^{e_{ij}})$, where $r_i=e_{i1}\ge e_{i2}\ge\cdots$ for each $i$. These exponents are determined by $T$.

Proof. Split $V$ into primary components, apply Theorem 6.1 to each, and use Theorem 5.2 on each cyclic summand. The largest exponent is $r_i$ because the minimal polynomial of a direct sum is the least common multiple. Uniqueness is Corollary 6.2. $\square$

This matrix is a rational canonical form of $T$. It is built with field operations from the factorization of $m_T$, and it needs no roots. Some books use a different normalization, grouping blocks into invariant factors.

An eight-dimensional example 46:54. Over $\mathbb{Q}$, let $\dim V=8$ and $m_T=p_1p_2^2$ with $p_1=x^4-4x^3+6x^2-4x-7$ and $p_2=x-3$. Note that $p_1=(x-1)^4-8$, which is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. It has no rational root. With $y=x-1$, a factorization $y^4-8=(y^2+by+c)(y^2-by+d)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ needs $c+d=b^2$, $b(d-c)=0$ and $cd=-8$. If $b=0$, then $d=-c$ and $c^2=8$; if $b\ne0$, then $c=d$ and $c^2=-8$. Neither has a rational solution. The block $C(p_1)$ has size $4$ and occurs at least once. A second copy would need all $8$ dimensions and leave no room for $p_2^2$. So $C(p_1)$ occurs once, and the remaining $4$ dimensions carry blocks $C(p_2^{e})$ with largest $e=2$. Two possibilities remain:

$$C(p_1)\oplus C(p_2^2)\oplus C(p_2^2)\qquad\text{or}\qquad C(p_1)\oplus C(p_2^2)\oplus C(p_2)\oplus C(p_2),$$

with

$$C(p_1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&7\\1&0&0&4\\0&1&0&-6\\0&0&1&4\end{pmatrix},\quad C(p_2^2)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-9\\1&6\end{pmatrix},\quad C(p_2)=(3).$$

Over $\mathbb{R}$ the polynomial $p_1$ factors further, and the form changes with it.

10. Executable check

The chapter laboratory uses exact fractions. It checks $E_1$, $D$, $N$, the Jordan basis and the identity $A^kw=2^kw+k2^{k-1}u$; the chain relations for $B$; the block-count formula and the chain basis of Theorem 6.1 (for $p=x$) on random nilpotent matrices of several shapes; the two rational forms of Section 9; and the matrices of Exercises 4 and 5. It does not integrate differential equations numerically, and it tests finitely many matrices; the general statements are proved above.

Exercises

  1. (Initial value.) Solve $X'=AX$ for the running matrix with $X(0)=(0,0,1)$.
  2. (Forms.) List all Jordan forms with characteristic polynomial $(x-5)^5$ and minimal polynomial $(x-5)^3$, with the geometric multiplicity of each.
  3. (Invariants.) Find two nilpotent $7\times7$ matrices with the same characteristic polynomial, the same minimal polynomial and the same geometric multiplicity that are not similar.
  4. (Commutation.) Write $T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ as diagonalizable plus nilpotent in two ways, and say which one Theorem 4.1 describes.
  5. (Fields.) Let $T$ on $\mathbb{Q}^4$ have $m_T=x^2+1$. Find its rational canonical form over $\mathbb{Q}$ and its Jordan form over $\mathbb{C}$.
  6. (Cyclic vectors.) Prove that $T$ has a cyclic vector if and only if $\deg m_T=\dim V$.
Fully worked solutions

  1. Expand $X(0)$ in the basis $(u_1,u,w)$ with $u_1=(1,0,2)$, $u=(1,1,2)$, $w=(1,1,1)$. One finds $(0,0,1)=u-w$. By Theorem 3.1 the solution with $X(0)=u$ is $e^{2t}u$ and with $X(0)=w$ is $e^{2t}(tu+w)$. So $X(t)=e^{2t}\big((1-t)u-w\big)=e^{2t}(-t,\,-t,\,1-2t)$. Check: at $t=0$ this is $(0,0,1)$, and $X'(0)=(-1,-1,0)$ equals the third column of $A$, which is $(-1,-1,0)$.

  2. The sizes add to $5$ and the largest is $3$: either $3+2$ or $3+1+1$. The geometric multiplicity is the number of blocks, $2$ or $3$.

  3. Take Jordan nilpotent forms with block sizes $(3,2,2)$ and $(3,3,1)$. Both have characteristic polynomial $x^7$, minimal polynomial $x^3$ and three blocks, so geometric multiplicity $3$. But $\dim\ker N^2=\sum\min(2,e)$ is $6$ for the first and $5$ for the second, and similar matrices have equal values of this number. So they are not similar.

  4. First, $T=\operatorname{diag}(1,2)+\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, with the first diagonalizable and the second nilpotent. These do not commute: the two products are $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Second, $T$ has distinct eigenvalues $1,2$, so it is diagonalizable and $T=T+0$. The second pair commutes, and it is the one of Theorem 4.1.

  5. The polynomial $x^2+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ and has degree $2$, so $\dim V=4$ gives two cyclic summands, each with annihilator $x^2+1$: the form is $C(x^2+1)\oplus C(x^2+1)$ with $C(x^2+1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Over $\mathbb{C}$, regarding the same matrix as an operator on $\mathbb{C}^4$, $m_T=(x-i)(x+i)$ has distinct roots, so the Jordan form is diagonal. The characteristic polynomial $(x^2+1)^2$ shows that it is $\operatorname{diag}(i,i,-i,-i)$.

  6. If $V=Z(\alpha;T)$, then by Theorem 5.2 the minimal polynomial of $T$ is $p_\alpha$ of degree $\dim V$. Conversely, let $\deg m_T=\dim V$. In each primary component $W_i$ choose $v_i$ with annihilator exactly $p_i^{r_i}$; one exists because $p_i(T)^{r_i-1}$ does not vanish on $W_i$. Put $\alpha=\sum v_i$. If $f(T)\alpha=0$, then each $f(T)v_i=0$, since $f(T)v_i\in W_i$ and the sum is direct. So every $p_i^{r_i}$ divides $f$, hence $m_T$ divides $f$. Thus $p_\alpha=m_T$ and $\dim Z(\alpha;T)=\deg m_T=\dim V$.

"""Chapter 20 laboratory: diagonalizable plus nilpotent, canonical forms.

Exact arithmetic over Q (fractions.Fraction).  A matrix is a list of
rows, a vector a list.  The laboratory checks the running example (the
3 x 3 matrix with minimal polynomial (x-1)(x-2)^2), the chain relations
behind the solutions of X' = AX, the nilpotent block counts, the chain
basis built by induction on random nilpotent matrices, companion
matrices, and the computations of the exercises.
"""
from fractions import Fraction
import random


def Q(rows):
    return [[Fraction(x) for x in row] for row in rows]


def vec(*xs):
    return [Fraction(x) for x in xs]


def eye(n):
    return [[Fraction(int(i == j)) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def zeros(n):
    return [[Fraction(0)] * n for _ in range(n)]


def mul(A, B):
    return [[sum((a * b for a, b in zip(r, c)), Fraction(0))
             for c in zip(*B)] for r in A]


def add(A, B, c=1):
    return [[x + c * y for x, y in zip(r, s)] for r, s in zip(A, B)]


def apply(A, v):
    return [sum((a * x for a, x in zip(r, v)), Fraction(0)) for r in A]


def power(A, k):
    out = eye(len(A))
    for _ in range(k):
        out = mul(out, A)
    return out


def cols(vectors):
    return [list(r) for r in zip(*vectors)]


def rref(A):
    R = [r[:] for r in A]
    piv, r = [], 0
    for c in range(len(R[0]) if R else 0):
        s = next((i for i in range(r, len(R)) if R[i][c] != 0), None)
        if s is None:
            continue
        R[r], R[s] = R[s], R[r]
        R[r] = [x / R[r][c] for x in R[r]]
        for i in range(len(R)):
            if i != r and R[i][c] != 0:
                f = R[i][c]
                R[i] = [x - f * y for x, y in zip(R[i], R[r])]
        piv.append(c)
        r += 1
        if r == len(R):
            break
    return R, piv


def rank(vectors):
    return len(rref(cols(vectors))[1]) if vectors else 0


def nullspace(A):
    R, piv = rref(A)
    n = len(A[0])
    basis = []
    for f in (c for c in range(n) if c not in piv):
        x = [Fraction(0)] * n
        x[f] = Fraction(1)
        for row, p in zip(R, piv):
            x[p] = -row[f]
        basis.append(x)
    return basis


def solve(vectors, b):
    """Coefficients x with sum x_j vectors[j] = b (vectors independent)."""
    M = [list(row) + [y] for row, y in zip(cols(vectors), b)]
    R, piv = rref(M)
    k = len(vectors)
    assert k not in piv, 'b is not in the span'
    x = [Fraction(0)] * k
    for row, p in zip(R, piv):
        x[p] = row[k]
    return x


def inverse(A):
    n = len(A)
    R, piv = rref([r + e for r, e in zip(A, eye(n))])
    assert piv[:n] == list(range(n))
    return [r[n:] for r in R]


def nullity(A):
    return len(nullspace(A))


def jordan(blocks):
    """Block diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks given as (c, size)."""
    n = sum(s for _, s in blocks)
    J = zeros(n)
    at = 0
    for c, s in blocks:
        for i in range(s):
            J[at + i][at + i] = Fraction(c)
            if i + 1 < s:
                J[at + i][at + i + 1] = Fraction(1)
        at += s
    return J


def block_counts(N):
    """Number of nilpotent blocks of each size, from the nullities."""
    n = len(N)
    m = [nullity(power(N, i)) for i in range(n + 2)]
    return {i: 2 * m[i] - m[i - 1] - m[i + 1]
            for i in range(1, n + 1) if 2 * m[i] - m[i - 1] - m[i + 1]}


def chain_basis(N, space):
    """Chains [v, Nv, ...] forming a basis of the invariant span(space).

    This is the induction of the chapter: build chains for N(space),
    lift each head one step, then complete the chain ends to a basis of
    the kernel of N inside the space.
    """
    if not space:
        return []
    images = [apply(N, v) for v in space]
    piv = rref(cols(images))[1]
    image = [images[j] for j in piv]
    assert len(image) < len(space), 'N is not nilpotent on the space'
    chains = []
    for chain in chain_basis(N, image):
        head = chain[0]
        coef = solve(image, head)
        lift = [sum(c * space[piv[i]][t] for i, c in enumerate(coef))
                for t in range(len(head))]
        assert apply(N, lift) == head
        chains.append([lift] + chain)
    ends = [c[-1] for c in chains]
    # kernel of N restricted to span(space)
    kernel = [[sum(c * space[i][t] for i, c in enumerate(z))
               for t in range(len(space[0]))]
              for z in nullspace(cols(images))]
    for w in kernel:
        if rank(ends + [w]) > len(ends):
            ends.append(w)
            chains.append([w])
    return chains


def companion(coeffs):
    """Companion matrix of x^k + a_{k-1} x^{k-1} + ... + a_0.

    coeffs lists a_0, ..., a_{k-1}.
    """
    k = len(coeffs)
    C = zeros(k)
    for i in range(1, k):
        C[i][i - 1] = Fraction(1)
    for i in range(k):
        C[i][k - 1] = Fraction(-coeffs[i])
    return C


def block_diag(*blocks):
    n = sum(len(b) for b in blocks)
    M = zeros(n)
    at = 0
    for b in blocks:
        for i in range(len(b)):
            for j in range(len(b)):
                M[at + i][at + j] = b[i][j]
        at += len(b)
    return M


def poly_at(coeffs, A):
    """p(A) for p given by coefficients, constant term first."""
    out = zeros(len(A))
    for c in reversed(coeffs):
        out = add(mul(out, A), eye(len(A)), Fraction(c))
    return out


def main():
    # running example
    A = Q([[3, 1, -1], [2, 2, -1], [2, 2, 0]])
    one = eye(3)
    M2 = add(A, one, -2)
    E1 = mul(M2, M2)
    E2 = add(one, E1, -1)
    D = add(E1, E2, 2)
    N = add(A, D, -1)
    assert mul(E1, E1) == E1 and mul(E1, E2) == zeros(3)
    assert mul(D, N) == mul(N, D) and mul(N, N) == zeros(3) != N
    assert E1 == Q([[1, -1, 0], [0, 0, 0], [2, -2, 0]])
    assert D == Q([[1, 1, 0], [0, 2, 0], [-2, 2, 2]])
    assert N == Q([[2, 0, -1], [2, 0, -1], [4, 0, -2]])
    print('E1 =', [[str(x) for x in r] for r in E1])
    print('D =', [[str(x) for x in r] for r in D])
    print('N =', [[str(x) for x in r] for r in N])
    u1, u, w = vec(1, 0, 2), vec(1, 1, 2), vec(1, 1, 1)
    assert apply(A, u1) == u1
    assert apply(M2, u) == vec(0, 0, 0) and apply(M2, w) == u
    P = cols([u1, u, w])
    J = jordan([(1, 1), (2, 2)])
    assert mul(A, P) == mul(P, J)
    for k in range(1, 12):
        lhs = apply(power(A, k), w)
        assert lhs == [2**k * b + k * 2**(k - 1) * a for a, b in zip(u, w)]
    assert solve([u1, u, w], vec(0, 0, 1)) == [0, 1, -1]
    print('Jordan basis (1,0,2), (1,1,2), (1,1,1): checked')

    # first recording: the 2 x 2 system
    B = Q([[-2, 1], [0, -2]])
    v1, v2 = vec(1, 0), vec(0, 1)
    assert apply(add(B, eye(2), 2), v2) == v1
    assert apply(add(B, eye(2), 2), v1) == vec(0, 0)

    # nilpotent block counts: sizes (3,1,1) and (3,2)
    random.seed(20)
    for sizes in ((3, 1, 1), (3, 2), (3, 2, 2), (3, 3, 1), (2, 2)):
        n = sum(sizes)
        J = jordan([(0, s) for s in sizes])
        while True:
            S = Q([[random.randint(-2, 2) for _ in range(n)]
                   for _ in range(n)])
            if rank(cols(S)) == n:
                break
        Nm = mul(mul(S, J), inverse(S))
        expected = {s: sizes.count(s) for s in set(sizes)}
        assert block_counts(Nm) == expected
        chains = chain_basis(Nm, eye(n))
        assert sorted(map(len, chains)) == sorted(sizes)
        assert rank([v for c in chains for v in c]) == n
        for c in chains:
            assert [apply(Nm, v) for v in c] == c[1:] + [[0] * n]
        print('sizes', sizes, 'nullity', nullity(Nm), 'm2',
              nullity(mul(Nm, Nm)), 'recovered', expected)

    # rational forms: p1 = (t-1)^4 - 8, p2 = t - 3 on an 8-dim space
    p1 = [-7, -4, 6, -4, 1]
    C1 = companion(p1[:-1])
    C2 = companion([9, -6])
    c3 = companion([-3])
    assert poly_at(p1, C1) == zeros(4)
    for M in (block_diag(C1, C2, C2), block_diag(C1, C2, c3, c3)):
        a = poly_at(p1, M)
        b = add(M, eye(8), -3)
        assert mul(a, mul(b, b)) == zeros(8)
        assert mul(a, b) != zeros(8) and mul(b, b) != zeros(8)
    print('companion of p1, last column:',
          [str(r[-1]) for r in C1], '; two rational forms checked')
    # (t-1)^4 = 8 has no rational root
    assert all((Fraction(a, 7) - 1)**4 != 8 for a in range(-60, 61))

    # exercise 4: non-commuting split of [[1,1],[0,2]]
    T = Q([[1, 1], [0, 2]])
    Dg, Ng = Q([[1, 0], [0, 2]]), Q([[0, 1], [0, 0]])
    assert add(Dg, Ng) == T and mul(Dg, Ng) != mul(Ng, Dg)
    # exercise 5: two copies of x^2 + 1
    R = block_diag(companion([1, 0]), companion([1, 0]))
    assert mul(R, R) == add(zeros(4), eye(4), -1)
    print('all checks passed')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
