Which linear changes of $\mathbb{C}^2$ leave every length and every angle unchanged? Consider

$$U=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&4i\\4i&3\end{pmatrix}.$$

A direct computation gives $U^*U=I$, so $\|UX\|=\|X\|$ for every $X$. This matrix is the running example of the chapter. We shall see that such maps are exactly those whose inverse is their adjoint, that every invertible matrix can be turned into a unitary one by a unique triangular factor (so that $U$ is what remains of $\begin{pmatrix}3&4i\\0&5\end{pmatrix}$), and that $U$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. The last fact opens the question answered in Chapter 25.

Conventions. $V,W$ are inner product spaces over the same field $F=\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, with $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ linear in the first argument. The standard inner product of $F^n$ is $\langle X,Y\rangle=Y^*X$, where $A^*$ is the conjugate transpose. The adjoint $T^*$ of an operator $T$ is the operator with $\langle T\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,T^*\beta\rangle$; it exists in finite dimension and has the matrix $A^*$ in an orthonormal basis (Chapter 23).

1. Maps that preserve inner products 00:25

Definition 1.1. A linear map $T:V\to W$ preserves inner products if $\langle T\alpha,T\beta\rangle_W=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_V$ for all $\alpha,\beta\in V$. An isomorphism of inner product spaces is a bijective linear map that preserves inner products.

Proposition 1.2. A linear map preserves inner products if and only if $\|T\alpha\|=\|\alpha\|$ for all $\alpha$. Such a map is injective.

Proof. One direction is the case $\alpha=\beta$. For the other, the polarization identity of Chapter 21 expresses $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$ through norms of linear combinations of $\alpha,\beta$; linearity and the preservation of norms give the same expression for $\langle T\alpha,T\beta\rangle$. If $T\alpha=0$, then $\|\alpha\|=0$, so $\alpha=0$. $\square$

2. Equivalent conditions in finite dimension 10:47

A basis is orthonormal if its members have norm $1$ and are pairwise orthogonal.

Theorem 2.1. Let $\dim V=\dim W=n$ be finite and $T:V\to W$ linear. The following are equivalent:

  1. $T$ preserves inner products;
  2. $T$ is an isomorphism of inner product spaces;
  3. $T$ carries every orthonormal basis of $V$ to an orthonormal basis of $W$;
  4. $T$ carries some orthonormal basis of $V$ to an orthonormal basis of $W$.

Proof. $1\Rightarrow2$: $T$ is injective by Proposition 1.2, hence surjective because the dimensions agree. $2\Rightarrow3$: for an orthonormal basis $(e_j)$, $\langle Te_j,Te_k\rangle=\delta_{jk}$ (equal to $1$ if $j=k$ and $0$ otherwise), and $n$ orthonormal vectors form a basis of $W$. $3\Rightarrow4$: $V$ has an orthonormal basis by Gram–Schmidt. $4\Rightarrow1$: let $(e_j)$ and $(Te_j)$ be orthonormal, $\alpha=\sum x_je_j$ and $\beta=\sum y_je_j$. Then

$$\langle T\alpha,T\beta\rangle=\sum_{j,k}x_j\overline{y_k}\,\langle Te_j,Te_k\rangle=\sum_jx_j\overline{y_j}=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle.\qquad\square$$

Corollary 2.2 23:11. Two finite-dimensional inner product spaces over the same field are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension.

Proof. An isomorphism is in particular a linear bijection, so it preserves dimension. If the dimensions agree, choose orthonormal bases $(e_j)$, $(f_j)$ and define $Te_j=f_j$; apply $4\Rightarrow2$. $\square$

Coordinates 29:09. For an orthonormal basis of $V$, the coordinate map $V\to F^n$ sends that basis to the standard basis, so it is an isomorphism onto $F^n$ with the standard inner product. Every $n$-dimensional inner product space is therefore a copy of $F^n$, one copy for each orthonormal basis.

3. Preserving is not enough in general 34:41

Let $V=C[0,1]$, the continuous functions, with $\langle f,g\rangle_V=\int_0^1t^2f\bar g$, and let $W$ be the same space with $\int_0^1f\bar g$. (The first rule is positive: if $\int t^2|f|^2=0$, then $f=0$ on $(0,1]$, hence at $0$ by continuity.) The map $(Tf)(t)=tf(t)$ satisfies $\langle Tf,Tg\rangle_W=\langle f,g\rangle_V$. It is not onto, because every $Tf$ vanishes at $0$ and the constant $1$ does not. So it preserves inner products without being an isomorphism.

The equality of dimensions in Theorem 2.1 also matters: the inclusion $x\mapsto(x,0)$ of $\mathbb{R}$ into $\mathbb{R}^2$ preserves inner products and is not onto.

4. Unitary operators 00:25

Definition 4.1. A unitary operator on $V$ is an isomorphism of the inner product space $V$ onto itself. (Over $\mathbb{R}$ it is often called an orthogonal operator.)

The identity is unitary; a composition of unitary operators is unitary; and the inverse of a unitary operator is unitary, since $\|\alpha\|=\|U(U^{-1}\alpha)\|=\|U^{-1}\alpha\|$. So the unitary operators of $V$ form a group under composition.

A sum of unitary operators need not be unitary: $U+U$ doubles every length.

Theorem 4.2 08:29. Let $T$ be an operator on an inner product space of any dimension. Then $T$ is unitary if and only if $T$ has an adjoint with $T^*T=TT^*=I$.

Proof. Let $T$ be unitary. For all $\alpha,\beta$, $$\langle T\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle T\alpha,TT^{-1}\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,T^{-1}\beta\rangle .$$ So the adjoint exists and equals $T^{-1}$. Conversely, the two identities make $T$ bijective, and $\langle T\alpha,T\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,T^*T\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$. $\square$

Both identities are needed. On sequences with finitely many nonzero entries, with $\langle x,y\rangle=\sum x_k\overline{y_k}$, the shift $S(x_1,x_2,\dots)=(0,x_1,x_2,\dots)$ has the adjoint $S^*(x_1,x_2,\dots)=(x_2,x_3,\dots)$. Here $S^*S=I$ but $SS^*e_1=0$. So $S$ preserves inner products and is not unitary. In finite dimension this cannot happen, since $T^*T=I$ makes $T$ injective, hence invertible, with $T^{-1}=T^*$.

5. Unitary and orthogonal matrices 15:07

Definition 5.1. $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ is unitary if $A^*A=I$. A matrix $A\in F^{n\times n}$ is orthogonal if $A^{\mathsf T}A=I$.

For square matrices a one-sided inverse is two-sided, so $A^*A=I$ implies $AA^*=I$. Reading the two identities entry by entry: $A$ is unitary exactly when its columns are orthonormal, and exactly when its rows are orthonormal, for the standard inner product.

Squareness is needed: for the column $B=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, $B^*B=(1)$ but $BB^*=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$.

Theorem 5.2. Let $V$ be finite-dimensional, $\mathcal{B}$ an orthonormal basis and $A=[T]_{\mathcal{B}}$. Then $T$ is unitary if and only if $A$ is unitary.

Proof. $[T^*]_{\mathcal{B}}=A^*$, so $[T^*T]_{\mathcal{B}}=A^*A$. Hence $T^*T=I$ exactly when $A^*A=I$, and in finite dimension this is equivalent to unitarity (Section 4). $\square$

Since the statement holds for each orthonormal basis, it holds for some one exactly when it holds for all. For a basis that is not orthonormal it fails. The rotation $R=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-4\\4&3\end{pmatrix}$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$ has, in the basis $((1,0),(1,1))$, the matrix $\frac15\begin{pmatrix}-1&-8\\4&7\end{pmatrix}$, whose first column has squared length $17/25$.

If $\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}'$ are both orthonormal and $P$ is the change of basis matrix, then $P$ is unitary and $[T]_{\mathcal{B}'}=P^*[T]_{\mathcal{B}}P$. Matrices so related are unitarily equivalent.

Orthogonal is not unitary over $\mathbb{C}$ 30:06. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix}5/4&-3i/4\\3i/4&5/4\end{pmatrix}$ satisfies $A^{\mathsf T}A=I$, but its first column has squared length $34/16$. For real matrices the two notions coincide.

Size one. $(c)$ is orthogonal when $c=\pm1$, and unitary when $|c|=1$, that is, $c=e^{i\theta}$.

Size two 32:48. An orthogonal $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ has $(\det A)^2=1$ and $A^{\mathsf T}=A^{-1}$. For $\det A=1$ the equation reads $\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\b&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$, so $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\-b&a\end{pmatrix}$. For $\det A=-1$ it gives $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&-a\end{pmatrix}$. In both cases $a^2+b^2=1$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ these are the rotations and the reflections.

A unitary $2\times2$ matrix has the form $\begin{pmatrix}a&-\lambda\bar c\\c&\lambda\bar a\end{pmatrix}$ with $|a|^2+|c|^2=1$ and $|\lambda|=1$: the first column $(a,c)$ is a unit vector; the second is a unit vector orthogonal to it, and the orthogonal complement of $(a,c)$ is the line spanned by the unit vector $(-\bar c,\bar a)$. Conversely these columns are orthonormal. Here $\det A=\lambda$. The running example is $a=3/5$, $c=4i/5$, $\lambda=1$.

6. The unitary group and triangular factorization 00:25

Let $U(n)$ be the set of unitary $n\times n$ matrices. It is a group under multiplication, with $A^{-1}=A^*$. Let $T^+_n$ be the set of lower triangular matrices with positive real diagonal entries. It is a group too: the product of lower triangular matrices is lower triangular with diagonal entries equal to the products of the diagonal entries; and forward substitution solves $MX=I$ with $X$ lower triangular and diagonal entries $1/M_{kk}$.

Theorem 6.1. For every invertible $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ there is exactly one $M\in T^+_n$ with $MA\in U(n)$.

Proof. Existence 04:30. The rows $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n$ of $A$ are linearly independent. Gram–Schmidt produces orthogonal $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ with

$$\alpha_k=\beta_k-\sum_{j\lt k}\frac{\langle\beta_k,\alpha_j\rangle}{\|\alpha_j\|^2}\alpha_j,$$

and $\operatorname{span}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k)=\operatorname{span}(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_k)$. So the sum can be rewritten as $\alpha_k=\beta_k-\sum_{j\lt k}C_{kj}\beta_j$. Put $M_{kk}=1/\|\alpha_k\|$, $M_{kj}=-C_{kj}/\|\alpha_k\|$ for $j\lt k$, and $M_{kj}=0$ for $j\gt k$. Then $M\in T^+_n$, and row $k$ of $MA$ is $\sum_jM_{kj}\beta_j=\alpha_k/\|\alpha_k\|$. The rows of $MA$ are orthonormal, so $MA\in U(n)$.

Uniqueness 13:30. Let $M_1,M_2\in T^+_n$ with $M_1A,M_2A\in U(n)$. Then $N=M_1M_2^{-1}=(M_1A)(M_2A)^{-1}$ lies in $U(n)\cap T^+_n$. So $N^*=N^{-1}$. The left side is upper triangular and the right side lower triangular, so $N$ is diagonal. A diagonal unitary matrix has entries of modulus $1$, and positive ones equal $1$. Hence $N=I$. $\square$

The hinge of uniqueness is that two groups, $U(n)$ and $T^+_n$, meet only in $I$. Equivalently, $A=LQ$ with $L=M^{-1}\in T^+_n$ and $Q\in U(n)$, uniquely. Applying this to $A^*$ gives the factorization $A=QR$ with $R$ upper triangular, used in numerical work. Positivity of the diagonal is necessary for uniqueness: $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)M$ also makes $A$ unitary.

On the running example. For $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&4i\\0&5\end{pmatrix}$ the first row has norm $5$. The second step gives $\langle\beta_2,\alpha_1\rangle=-20i$ with $\alpha_1=(3,4i)$, so $\alpha_2=\beta_2+\tfrac{4i}{5}\alpha_1=(\tfrac{12i}{5},\tfrac95)$, of norm $3$. Hence

$$M=\begin{pmatrix}1/5&0\\4i/15&1/3\end{pmatrix},\qquad MA=U,\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\-4i&3\end{pmatrix}U .$$

A family of $3\times3$ examples 00:25. Let $x_1,x_2$ be real with $x_1^2+x_2^2=1$ and $x_1\gt0$, and let $A$ have rows $(x_1,x_2,0)$, $(0,1,0)$, $(0,0,1)$. Then $\alpha_2=\beta_2-x_2\beta_1=(-x_1x_2,x_1^2,0)$ has norm $x_1$, and $\beta_3$ is already orthogonal. So

$$M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\-x_2/x_1&1/x_1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad MA=\begin{pmatrix}x_1&x_2&0\\-x_2&x_1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

A consequence: Schur's theorem. Every operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space has an upper triangular matrix in some orthonormal basis. Indeed, Chapter 16 gives a basis $(b_1,\dots,b_n)$ with $Tb_k\in\operatorname{span}(b_1,\dots,b_k)$. Gram–Schmidt and normalization keep each of these spans, so the new orthonormal basis has the same property.

7. Toward orthonormal eigenbases: normal operators 12:17

When does $V$ have an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$? First find what such a basis forces. Suppose $\dim V$ is finite, $(e_j)$ is orthonormal and $Te_j=c_je_j$. Then $[T]=\operatorname{diag}(c_j)$ and $[T^*]=\operatorname{diag}(\bar c_j)$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ this says $T^*=T$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it says only that the two diagonal matrices commute.

Definition 7.1 21:25. An operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space is normal if $TT^*=T^*T$. It is self-adjoint if $T^*=T$.

Self-adjoint operators ($TT^*=T^2=T^*T$) and unitary operators ($TT^*=I=T^*T$) are normal. So an orthonormal eigenbasis requires $T$ normal, and over $\mathbb{R}$ self-adjoint. Chapter 25 proves the converses. Over $\mathbb{R}$, normality alone is not sufficient: a quarter turn is orthogonal, hence normal, and has no real eigenvalue.

8. Self-adjoint operators 24:30

Theorem 8.1. Let $T$ be a self-adjoint operator on an inner product space (that is, $\langle T\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,T\beta\rangle$). Then every eigenvalue of $T$ is real, and eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Proof. Let $T\alpha=c\alpha$ with $\alpha\ne0$. Then $c\|\alpha\|^2=\langle T\alpha,\alpha\rangle=\langle\alpha,T\alpha\rangle=\bar c\|\alpha\|^2$, so $c=\bar c$. Let also $T\beta=d\beta$ with $d\ne c$. Then $c\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle T\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,T\beta\rangle=d\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$, using $d=\bar d$. So $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=0$. $\square$

The theorem says nothing about the existence of eigenvalues.

On the running example. $U(1,1)^{\mathsf T}=\frac{3+4i}{5}(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ and $U(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}=\frac{3-4i}{5}(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$. The eigenvectors are orthogonal and the eigenvalues have modulus $1$ without being real. So $U$ is normal, not self-adjoint, and $(1,1)/\sqrt2$, $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$ is an orthonormal eigenbasis.

Exercises

  1. (Spectrum.) Show that the eigenvalues of a unitary operator have modulus $1$ and that eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal.
  2. (Computation.) Find $M\in T^+_2$ with $MA$ orthogonal for $A=\begin{pmatrix}4&3\\0&5\end{pmatrix}$, and write $A=LQ$.
  3. (Field dependence.) Show that a complex matrix which is both orthogonal and unitary has real entries.
  4. (Isomorphism.) Give an isomorphism of inner product spaces from $P_1$, with $\int_0^1pq$, onto $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the standard inner product, and check it on $p=x$.
  5. (Reflections.) For $v\ne0$ in $\mathbb{C}^n$ let $H=I-2vv^*/\|v\|^2$. Show that $H$ is self-adjoint and unitary, and compute $H$ for $v=(1,i)$.
  6. (Normality by lengths.) On a finite-dimensional space show that $T$ is normal if and only if $\|T\alpha\|=\|T^*\alpha\|$ for all $\alpha$, and test $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$.
Fully worked solutions

  1. If $U\alpha=c\alpha$ with $\alpha\ne0$, then $\|\alpha\|=\|U\alpha\|=|c|\,\|\alpha\|$, so $|c|=1$. If also $U\beta=d\beta$ with $d\ne c$, then $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle U\alpha,U\beta\rangle=c\bar d\,\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$. Since $|d|=1$, $\bar d=1/d$, so $c\bar d=c/d\ne1$. Hence $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=0$.

  2. $\beta_1=(4,3)$ has norm $5$, so $q_1=(4/5,3/5)$. $\langle\beta_2,\beta_1\rangle=15$, so $\alpha_2=(0,5)-\tfrac{15}{25}(4,3)=(-12/5,16/5)$, of norm $4$, and $q_2=(-3/5,4/5)$. Then $q_2=\tfrac14(\beta_2-\tfrac35\beta_1)$, so $M=\begin{pmatrix}1/5&0\\-3/20&1/4\end{pmatrix}$ and $Q=MA$ has rows $q_1,q_2$. Finally $L=M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$.

  3. Orthogonality gives $A^{-1}=A^{\mathsf T}$ and unitarity gives $A^{-1}=A^*=\overline{A}^{\mathsf T}$. The inverse is unique, so $A^{\mathsf T}=\overline{A}^{\mathsf T}$, hence $A=\overline A$.

  4. $e_1=1$ and $e_2=\sqrt{12}(x-\tfrac12)$ are an orthonormal basis, so take coordinates. Since $a+bx=(a+\tfrac b2)e_1+\tfrac{b}{\sqrt{12}}e_2$, the map is $a+bx\mapsto(a+\tfrac b2,\ b/\sqrt{12})$. For $p=x$ the image has squared length $\tfrac14+\tfrac1{12}=\tfrac13=\int_0^1x^2$.

  5. $(vv^*)^*=vv^*$, so $H^*=H$. Put $P=vv^*/\|v\|^2$. Then $P^2=v(v^*v)v^*/\|v\|^4=P$, so $H^2=I-4P+4P^2=I$. Hence $H^*H=H^2=I$. For $v=(1,i)$, $vv^*=\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\|v\|^2=2$, so $H=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\-i&0\end{pmatrix}$.

  6. $\|T\alpha\|^2-\|T^*\alpha\|^2=\langle(T^*T-TT^*)\alpha,\alpha\rangle$. If $T$ is normal this is $0$. Conversely, $S=T^*T-TT^*$ is self-adjoint with $\langle S\alpha,\alpha\rangle=0$ for all $\alpha$. Expanding at $\alpha+\beta$ gives $2\operatorname{Re}\langle S\alpha,\beta\rangle=0$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ this is the claim; over $\mathbb{C}$ replace $\beta$ by $i\beta$ to kill the imaginary part too. So $S=0$. For the given matrix, $\alpha=(0,1)$ has $\|T\alpha\|=1$ and $\|T^*\alpha\|=0$, so it is not normal.

"""Chapter 24 laboratory: unitary matrices and triangular factorization.

Exact arithmetic over the Gaussian rationals: a number is a pair of
Fractions (class GQ).  The general theorems are proved in the chapter;
this file checks the printed instances.
"""
from fractions import Fraction as Fr
from math import isqrt


class GQ:
    """a + b i with rational a and b."""

    def __init__(self, re=0, im=0):
        self.re, self.im = Fr(re), Fr(im)

    @staticmethod
    def lift(x):
        return x if isinstance(x, GQ) else GQ(x)

    def __add__(self, o):
        o = GQ.lift(o)
        return GQ(self.re + o.re, self.im + o.im)

    __radd__ = __add__

    def __sub__(self, o):
        o = GQ.lift(o)
        return GQ(self.re - o.re, self.im - o.im)

    def __mul__(self, o):
        o = GQ.lift(o)
        return GQ(self.re * o.re - self.im * o.im,
                  self.re * o.im + self.im * o.re)

    __rmul__ = __mul__

    def __truediv__(self, o):
        o = GQ.lift(o)
        d = o.re ** 2 + o.im ** 2
        n = self * o.conj()
        return GQ(n.re / d, n.im / d)

    def __eq__(self, o):
        o = GQ.lift(o)
        return (self.re, self.im) == (o.re, o.im)

    def conj(self):
        return GQ(self.re, -self.im)


def mat(rows):
    return [[GQ.lift(x) for x in r] for r in rows]


def mul(a, b):
    return [[sum((a[i][k] * b[k][j] for k in range(len(b))), GQ())
             for j in range(len(b[0]))] for i in range(len(a))]


def tr(a):
    return [list(r) for r in zip(*a)]


def adj(a):
    return [[x.conj() for x in r] for r in tr(a)]


def eye(n):
    return mat([[int(i == j) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)])


def unitary(a):
    return mul(adj(a), a) == eye(len(a[0]))


def ip(x, y):
    return sum((a * b.conj() for a, b in zip(x, y)), GQ())


def lq(a):
    """Lower triangular M, positive diagonal, with M a unitary.  Raises
    if some Gram-Schmidt norm is irrational."""
    orth, comb, m = [], [], []
    n = len(a)
    for k in range(n):
        v, t = list(a[k]), [GQ(int(i == k)) for i in range(n)]
        for u, s in zip(orth, comb):
            c = ip(a[k], u) / ip(u, u)
            v = [x - c * y for x, y in zip(v, u)]
            t = [x - c * y for x, y in zip(t, s)]
        q = ip(v, v).re
        norm = Fr(isqrt(q.numerator), isqrt(q.denominator))
        assert norm ** 2 == q and norm > 0
        orth.append(v)
        comb.append(t)
        m.append([x / norm for x in t])
    return m


U = mat([[Fr(3, 5), GQ(0, Fr(4, 5))], [GQ(0, Fr(4, 5)), Fr(3, 5)]])


def running_example():
    assert unitary(U) and mul(U, adj(U)) == eye(2)
    for sign in (1, -1):
        lam = GQ(Fr(3, 5), sign * Fr(4, 5))
        v = mat([[1], [sign]])
        assert mul(U, v) == [[lam * v[0][0]], [lam * v[1][0]]]
        assert lam.re ** 2 + lam.im ** 2 == 1
    a = mat([[3, GQ(0, 4)], [0, 5]])
    m = lq(a)
    assert m == mat([[Fr(1, 5), 0], [GQ(0, Fr(4, 15)), Fr(1, 3)]])
    assert mul(m, a) == U
    assert mul(mat([[5, 0], [GQ(0, -4), 3]]), U) == a
    other = mul(mat([[1, 0], [0, -1]]), m)
    assert unitary(mul(other, a)) and other[1][1] == GQ(Fr(-1, 3))
    print('running example: unitary, eigenpairs, A = L U verified')


def other_matrices():
    f35, f45 = Fr(3, 5), Fr(4, 5)
    r = mat([[f35, -f45], [f45, f35]])
    p, pinv = mat([[1, 1], [0, 1]]), mat([[1, -1], [0, 1]])
    rb = mul(pinv, mul(r, p))
    assert rb == mat([[Fr(-1, 5), Fr(-8, 5)], [Fr(4, 5), Fr(7, 5)]])
    assert not unitary(rb)
    assert ip(tr(rb)[0], tr(rb)[0]) == Fr(17, 25)
    h = Fr(3, 4)
    c = mat([[Fr(5, 4), GQ(0, -h)], [GQ(0, h), Fr(5, 4)]])
    assert mul(tr(c), c) == eye(2) and not unitary(c)
    assert ip(tr(c)[0], tr(c)[0]) == Fr(34, 16)
    for w in (mat([[f35, f45], [-f45, f35]]),
              mat([[f35, f45], [f45, -f35]])):
        assert mul(tr(w), w) == eye(2)
    a, cc = GQ(f35), GQ(0, f45)
    for lam in (GQ(1), GQ(0, 1), GQ(f35, f45)):
        w = [[a, GQ() - lam * cc.conj()], [cc, lam * a.conj()]]
        assert unitary(w)
        assert w[0][0] * w[1][1] - w[0][1] * w[1][0] == lam
    a3 = mat([[f35, f45, 0], [0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 1]])
    m3 = lq(a3)
    assert m3 == mat([[1, 0, 0], [Fr(-4, 3), Fr(5, 3), 0], [0, 0, 1]])
    assert mul(m3, a3) == mat([[f35, f45, 0], [-f45, f35, 0], [0, 0, 1]])
    print('basis change, orthogonal forms, unitary family, 3x3 verified')


def shift_and_exercises():
    x = [2, 1, -3]
    assert ([0] + x)[1:] == x
    e1 = [1, 0, 0]
    assert [0] + e1[1:] != e1
    a = mat([[4, 3], [0, 5]])
    m = lq(a)
    assert m == mat([[Fr(1, 5), 0], [Fr(-3, 20), Fr(1, 4)]])
    assert mul(mat([[5, 0], [3, 4]]), mul(m, a)) == a
    h = mat([[0, GQ(0, 1)], [GQ(0, -1), 0]])
    assert unitary(h) and adj(h) == h
    assert Fr(1, 4) + Fr(1, 12) == Fr(1, 3)
    nil = mat([[0, 1], [0, 0]])
    assert mul(nil, adj(nil)) != mul(adj(nil), nil)
    print('shift identities and exercise data verified')
